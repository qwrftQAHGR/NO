# I01 · Address & Greeting: the Titelbuch and the Ladder of Du

*How to speak to anyone in the Double Crown without starting a feud: the official book of titles, the pronoun ladder, the bows and curtsies, the hand-kiss and its rules, the drink that turns "Sie" into "Du," and how people greet each other from the Greifenburg to a Grenzer farmyard.*

**See also:** R05 Nobility · I02 The Card Visit · I69 The Ceremonial · I04 Below Stairs · X04 Glossary, Speech & Names

---

## 1. The Titelbuch

Wiedenbrunn has a book for this. **Das Titelbuch** (formally the *Titular- und Anredebuch der Kronen Valdau und Rocalba*) was first printed in **1674** by the **Titelkanzlei**, a small office of the Hofkammer, and revised in **1691** under the eye of House Ehrenreith (→ I43). It sets out the correct written and spoken address for every rank, office and honor in the realm:
- in German, Catalan and court Almagran;
- with the correct opening and closing formulas for letters;
- with a table of which titles a widow keeps;
- and an appendix of foreign forms, which the Kesrâni embassy says is wrong.

It costs **2 Bräutlinge**. Every Bürger household that hopes to rise owns one. Every Portier keeps one in the lodge. Every clerk who writes a petition consults it. A person who knows it by heart is **titelfest**, title-secure, which is a compliment to a secretary and an insult to a gentleman. A mistake is a **Titelfehler**, and a Titelfehler in a letter to a superior can lose a lawsuit, a post or a fiancée.

The 1691 revision is a sore subject. It moved the Hofräte one step up and the knightly Ritter one step down. The Ritter have been in a sulk for eight years, and a Ritter will tell you so at length if you address him by the 1691 form.

| A Ritter, an Edler | *Euer Wohlgeboren*; *Herr von …* | | |

**Wives take their husbands' titles and offices** (*Frau Hofrätin, Frau Oberstleutnantin, Frau Doktorin*), and widows keep them with *-witwe*. In the **Spindelhäuser** it runs the other way: the husband becomes *Herr Gemahl* and is addressed by his wife's title, *der Herr Gemahl der Frau Fürstin* (→ R05).

### Titles for those the Titelbuch didn't expect
- **Futas** take female forms in everything (→ R10).
- **Zierherren** take male forms. Some prefer, among intimates, to be called *Fräulein* in jest, or not in jest (→ R11).
- **Mondbräutigame** of Ilsenau are *Prinz* by courtesy, and *Herr Gemahl* in the house.
- **Linkskinder** (left-hand children) take their mother's surname with a *von* and no title (→ I56).
- **Kesrâni captives** are addressed by first names by their owners and as *Efendi* or *Hanım* by everyone else who wants something from them.
- **Gereifte**, the protagonist's quick-born children (→ R13), will be whatever the forged papers say.

## 2. The pronoun ladder

Valdauer German in 1699 has four ways to say "you," and each says something about the speaker as well.

1. **Du**: intimate. Used to children, lovers, family (a modern habit among the young nobility; old houses still say *Ihr* to their parents), close friends, servants among themselves, God, the Bride and animals. To a stranger it is a provocation or an advance.
2. **Ihr**: the old courtly plural of respect. Old nobles use it to each other, Rocalbans use it to everyone above them, and children of conservative houses use it to their parents. It sounds stately, Almagran and a little old-fashioned. A young man who says *Ihr* to a lady is being either very proper or very romantic.
3. **Sie**: the new polite form, spreading from the bureaucracy since the 1650s. Bürger, officials and modern courtiers use it among themselves and to their superiors. Old nobles find it clerkish.
4. **Er / Sie** (third person singular): the address of superior to inferior. *"Hat Er die Pferde gefüttert?"*, "Has *he* fed the horses?", said to a groom's face. Masters use it to free servants, officers to soldiers, judges to commoners. Being "*ge-Er-t*," addressed as Er, by someone who should say Sie is an insult.

**Moving between them** is the language of every relationship:
- A lady who switches from *Sie* to *Er* with her steward is about to dismiss him.
- A gentleman who switches from *Ihr* to *du* with a lady in private has said something no letter needs to.
- Two Bürger who have drunk **Bruderschaft** (§5) never say *Sie* to each other again.

**Rocalban Catalan**:
- **vós**: respect and distance; the old courtly form.
- **tu**: intimacy.
- **vostra mercè** ("your grace"): to anyone above oneself. Shortened in fast speech to *vostè*, which old Rocalbans find vulgar.
- **En/Na** prefixes for nobles and honored citizens.

**Court Almagran** at ceremonies: *Vuestra Majestad*, *Vuestra Excelencia*, *Vuestra Merced*, *Su Señoría*. Courtiers pronounce it badly and are corrected by the Almagran ambassador's secretary, who is paid to (→ I69).

## 3. Bows, curtsies and kneeling

**Men: the Reverenz**
- **Der halbe Bückling** (the half-bow): a bend from the waist, eyes down briefly, hat in hand. For equals and the slightly superior.
- **Der tiefe Bückling** (the deep bow): low, right foot drawn back, hat sweeping. For great nobles, bishops and the Queen's ladies.
- **Der Hofbückling** (court bow): the deep bow done three times while advancing. For the King, at audiences.
- **Der Kniefall** (kneeling): to the King when petitioning, to the Abbess for a blessing, and to a lady in a romance.

**Women: the Knicks**
- **Der kleine Knicks**: a quick dip of the knee. For equals, and for every adult a girl meets.
- **Der Hofknicks** (court curtsy): a deep, slow sinking, back straight, skirts spread, eyes lowered, held for a breath, rising without wobbling. Girls practice it for months before their presentation. The Stiftsjahr Elevinnen in the Ilsenau household practice it until they cry (→ I19).
- **Der Almagraner Knicks** (Almagran curtsy): to royalty only, sinking until the knee touches the floor. It is a serious test of the knees and of one's stays.

**The bound**:
- **The Bandgruß** (ribbon-greeting): a pledge greeting their owner or the owner's family lowers the eyes and touches two fingers to the **Pfandband** at the throat (→ R07). It acknowledges whose they are.
- Done slowly, with eyes up, between two people who have shared a bed, it means something else entirely, and both know it.

**Clergy**: the faithful kiss a Zeuge's hand, or kneel for the Abbess's blessing.

## 4. The hand-kiss

**"Küss die Hand, gnädige Frau"** ("I kiss your hand, gracious lady") is the most Wiedenbrunn sentence there is. Men of every class say it to women of rank, usually without kissing anything. The actual **Handkuss** has rules that every gentleman knows, and the rules are broken carefully:

1. **Only to married women and widows.** A hand-kiss to an unmarried girl is a claim. Her brothers will want to know on whose behalf.
2. **Never outdoors.** Not in the street, the park or the Corso. A hand-kiss is an indoor courtesy.
3. **Never to a gloved hand** at a formal occasion; she removes the glove, or he doesn't.
4. **The lips do not touch.** The gentleman bows over the offered hand and **breathes** a kiss a finger's width above the skin: *der gehauchte Handkuss*.
5. **The lady offers; the gentleman doesn't take.** She extends her hand palm-down and slightly raised. If she doesn't offer it, he doesn't reach.

**The broken forms**:
- **Der nasse Handkuss**, the wet hand-kiss: lips actually on skin. A declaration.
- **The hand turned over**, a kiss on the palm or the inside of the wrist: an advance, or something already arranged.
- **The held hand**, kept a breath too long after the kiss: noticed by everyone.
- **A lady who offers her hand unasked** to a man of lower rank is giving him a public favor that will be discussed.

Rocalbans don't kiss hands much. They embrace (§6), and find the Valdauer hand-kiss either exquisitely romantic or a bit Almagran. Ormándi men say **"Kezét csókolom"** ("I kiss your hand") to women and kiss them with gusto, lips and all, which Valdauer ladies pretend to find shocking.

## 5. Drinking Bruderschaft

The **Bruderschaft** (brotherhood; for women, **Schwesternschaft**) is the ritual by which two people move from *Sie* or *Ihr* to *du*, permanently.

1. It is proposed by the **senior**: the higher in rank, the older, or **the woman**. A man who proposes it to a woman of equal rank is being forward. One who proposes it to a woman of higher rank is being absurd.
2. The two stand, glasses filled with wine, or Sturm in season.
3. They **link right arms** at the elbow, so each drinks from their own glass with their arm through the other's.
4. They drink, all of it.
5. They **kiss**: the **Bruderschaftskuss**. Between men it is a kiss on each cheek. Between a man and a woman, or two women, custom says the mouth, briefly. It is very often not brief.
6. Each says the other's first name and *du*. From that moment **neither may ever again say Sie to the other**, and to do so is an insult as deep as a slap.

**Refusing** a proposed Bruderschaft is a grave snub. Proposing it and being accepted makes a public intimacy that can't be taken back.
- Among young nobles it has become the season's flirtation: a **Duzkuss** at a Heuriger table is half a seduction.
- Between a man and woman who are not kin, Duzen in public is assumed to mean they are lovers or about to be.
- Between kin it is unnecessary, since kin say *du* already, so a brother and sister who make a show of drinking Bruderschaft are playing the Nähespiel (→ R06).

## 6. Greetings, by place and person

### The faithful (village, clergy, the pious)
- **"Gelobt sei die Hochzeit!"** ("Praised be the Wedding!"). Reply: **"Und das Mahl in Ewigkeit."** ("And the Feast forever.")
- On parting: **"Behüt dich die Braut"** ("The Bride keep you"), shortened in the Hallgau to **"Pfiat di Braut,"** and further to **"Pfiat di."**
- In Rocalba: **"La Núvia us guard!"** Reply: **"I a vós."**

### Wiedenbrunn
- **"Servus!"**: the universal greeting among equals and friends, hello and goodbye. It is Church Latin for "*your servant*," or more exactly "*your bondsman*," and in a city full of ribboned pledges the joke writes itself. *"Servus" — "Na, so weit kommt's noch"* ("Well, it hasn't come to that yet").
- **"Habe die Ehre"** ("I have the honor"): to a superior or a stranger of standing; men of the Bürger class to anyone. In fast Brunnerisch, *"D'Ehre."*
- **"Küss die Hand, gnädige Frau"**: to any lady (§4).
- **"Ergebenster Diener"** ("most devoted servant"): from a clerk to anyone.
- **"Grüß Euch"** ("Greetings to you all"): entering a room.
- **"Gehorsamster Diener"**: from tradesmen, said with a bow that measures exactly how much they expect to be paid.

### Court
- To the King: nothing, until spoken to. Then the deep bow and *"Euer Majestät."*
- Among the Seven Chairs: the half-bow and the title. Starkenberg princes bow to Ilsenau women at precisely the minimum depth the Ceremonial allows, and everyone watches them do it (→ I69).
- Among kin of great houses: the **Stirnkuss** (forehead), **Wangenkuss** (cheek), or in private the **Spiegelkuss** (→ R06).

### Rocalba
- **"Bon dia tingui!"** ("Good day may you have"); among friends **"Bon dia!"** or **"Salut!"**
- The Rocalban **abraçada**: a full embrace with two pats on the back, between men.
- **Dos petons**, two kisses on the cheeks, between women and between men and women. Valdauers find this very intimate. Rocalbans find the Valdauer bow very cold.
- *"Adéu-siau"* on parting; *"A reveure"* if you'll meet again.

### Ormánd
- **"Kezét csókolom"**: men to women, children to elders, servants to masters.
- Among men: **"Adjon a Menyasszony!"** ("May the Bride give!"). Reply: **"Fogadj Isten!"**, an old greeting the Ormándi have never translated for anyone.
- A hussar's greeting is a bear-hug and a demand that you drink.

### The Sworn Valleys
- **"Grüess Ech"**, and a **handshake**: palm to palm, as equals, looking you in the eye. Valdauer nobles find the Valleys handshake shockingly familiar. Valleys men find the Valdauer bow servile.

### Kesrân
- The **three-touch greeting**: the right hand touches the heart, then the lips, then the brow, with a slight bow. It is how the embassy greets everyone. Wiedenbrunn ladies have taken it up as a flirtation.

### Asagiri
- The **bow**, graded by depth and duration with a precision the Ceremonial only dreams of. The envoy's retinue will not touch hands with anyone, and are mystified and alarmed by the hand-kiss (→ I37).

### The Grenzmark
- **"Pomoz' Bog!"** ("God help you!"), answered **"Bog ti pomogo!"** The Grenzer use the old formula and don't care what the Wedding Church thinks of it. Men clasp forearms. Women kiss three times on the cheeks.

### The bound
- To their owners: the Bandgruß (§3) and **"Euer Gnaden befehlen?"** ("Your Grace commands?").
- Among themselves: first names, *du*, and a dozen kitchen-words for the masters (→ I04).

## 7. Gestures

- **Eisen fassen** ("touching iron"): touching something iron (a sword-hilt, a key, a nail) for luck or courage (→ I13).
- **Den Pagat machen** ("making the Pagat"): the thumb tucked over the little finger, held low. "May the lowest win." A hope for luck against the odds, made at card tables, in lawsuits and before duels.
- **Die Feige** ("the fig"): the thumb thrust between two fingers. Obscene, contemptuous, and sometimes an invitation.
- **Hand aufs Herz** ("hand on heart"): an oath, and Kesrâni-fashionable as a greeting.
- **Augen nieder**: eyes lowered. Required of servants and pledges, fashionable in débutantes, and suspicious in wives.
- **Fächer zu**: a fan snapped shut. The lady has finished with you (→ I07).
- **The Rocalban chin-flick**: the back of the fingers flicked out from under the chin. *I don't care.*
- **The Bandgruß**: see §3.

---

## 8. Oaths and swearing

- **"Bei allen Sieben!"** ("By all seven [planets]!"): polite surprise.
- **"Himmelbrautsakra!"** ("Heaven-Bride-sacrament!"): coachmen, soldiers, and ladies when they think nobody is listening.
- **"Sakra!"**: mild; universal.
- **"Kruzi-Halde!"** ("Cross-slagheap!"): a Hallgau miners' curse.
- **"Saturn hol's!"** ("Saturn take it!"): something has gone badly wrong.
- **"Mecagum la Halde!"**: Rocalban, very rude, untranslatable in polite company.
- **"Bei Mutters Milch!"**: the Ilsenau house oath (→ R12).
- **"Hart auf hart!"** ("Hard on hard!"): the Starkenberg motto, used as an oath by soldiers.
- **"Beim Wolf!"** ("By the wolf!"): a recent Rosengrund oath meaning *you don't say*, from the Wolf im Schlafrock (→ R11).

## HOOKS

1. **The Titelfehler.** A letter goes out from the household to the Hofkammer addressing an Ehrenreith clerk by the pre-1691 form. It wasn't a mistake: someone in the household wanted to insult him. Now the clerk wants to know who.
2. **Bruderschaft at the Heuriger.** At a crowded Heuriger table a laughing young countess proposes Bruderschaft to the protagonist, arm already crooked, glass already full, half the table watching. She is the sister of a Starkenberg officer who is also at the table.
3. **The wet hand-kiss.** In front of the whole Cercle, a foreign ambassador gives an Ilsenau woman a hand-kiss that touches. Within the hour the Obersthofmeisterin must decide whether it was a diplomatic insult, an Almagran custom, or a declaration.
4. **"Er."** A free servant of the household has been addressed as *Er* by a visiting Mondbräutigam of another house, who should have used *Sie*. He comes to the protagonist, the only man of the house, to ask whether a man of the house will make it right.
5. **The Bandgruß, slowly.** A pledge of the house who served in the Stille Haus greets the protagonist on his first walk through the Palais with the Bandgruß: two fingers to the ribbon, eyes not lowered. It is unmistakably not the ordinary gesture, and several women see it.
6. **The handshake.** A Sworn Valleys pike-captain of the Schwurgarde offers the protagonist his hand to shake, as an equal, in a Greifenburg corridor. Taking it will be gossip by dinner, and refusing it will be gossip in the barracks.
