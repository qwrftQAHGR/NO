# I17 · Weddings: Brautwerber, Kettensperre & Morgengabe

*How people marry in the Double Crown, from a Hallgau farm to the Greifenburg: the go-between and the inspection, betrothal and trial nights, the crockery-smashing eve, the chain across the road, the kidnapped bride, the vows, the feast, the bedding, and the morning gift. With the special weddings: noble contracts, proxy grooms, kin-weddings with their silver thread, left-hand weddings at night, weddings in chains, and weddings of two women.*

## 1. Before: wooing and betrothal

### Brautwerbung (the wooing)
- In the countryside a marriage is not proposed by the groom. It is proposed by a **Brautwerber**, a go-between: an uncle, a respected neighbor, or a professional **Schmuser** (matchmaker), who takes a fee and a drink at every house he visits.
  - He visits the bride's family on the groom's behalf.
  - He praises the groom's farm, character and prospects, lying as needed.
  - He negotiates the dowry (**Heiratsgut**) and the bride's settlement.
- **The Beschau** (inspection): the bride's family **visits the groom's farm** to inspect the house, the beasts, the fields, the linen-chest and the mother-in-law. A bride's mother has called off a match on the strength of a dirty cowshed.
- **Among the Bürger**, matches are made by parents with a notary, and by the young people with each other at the Kirtag and the ball, usually in the opposite order.

### Verlobung (betrothal)
- **Handfasting**: the couple join right hands before witnesses and exchange a **Treupfand**, a pledge of troth: a ring, a coin broken in two, a silver ribbon.
- **The Probenächte** (trial nights) begin in the mountain valleys: the betrothed may sleep together under her parents' roof (→ R08). A betrothal that produces a pregnancy is sealed. A barren one can be broken without shame after a year.
- **The Aufgebot** (banns): read at the Sonntag Mahl three weeks running. Anyone with an objection must speak. Objections usually mean an earlier betrothal, an unpaid debt, an undeclared kinship (→ I55), or a jealous former lover.

### The Hochzeitlader (the inviter)
Two weeks before the wedding the **Hochzeitlader** goes from house to house with a **ribboned staff** and a sprig of rosemary, reciting rhymed invitations. He is a village figure, part herald and part comedian. Each household gives him a drink and a ribbon for his staff. By the wedding day his staff is a fountain of color and he is extremely drunk.

### The eve
- **Der Polterabend** (the crash-eve): the night before, friends and neighbors **smash crockery** on the bride's doorstep, the more the better, since the noise frightens off the Schlacken (→ I16) and shards are luck. The **bride and groom must sweep it all up together**, which is meant to teach them to work as a pair and mostly teaches them to quarrel.
- **Der Kranzlabend** (the wreath-eve): the bride and her unmarried women friends gather to bind the **Brautkranz**, her bridal wreath of myrtle and rosemary. They sing, weep, drink, tell her what to expect, and say goodbye to her as one of them. It is the most intimate night of a woman's friendships. What happens at a Kranzlabend among the bride's friends is a famous subject of songs (→ R09).

## 2. The wedding day

### The procession and the Kettensperre
- The bride is fetched from her parents' house by the groom's party, with musicians, the Hochzeitlader and the **Kranzljungfern** (bridesmaids, unmarried young women in wreaths).
- On the way to church, the **Zeche** (the village's young men, → I60) **bar the road with a chain**, a rope, or a felled tree. This is the **Kettensperre** (chain-barrier). The groom must **pay to pass**: a toll of coins, beer, schnapps, and a verse of Gstanzl in which he praises his bride and mocks himself.
  - A stingy groom is held up for an hour and lampooned for a year.
  - In the Eisenmark the chain is a real iron chain from the mines.
  - In great houses' weddings the Kettensperre is done by the servants with a silken rope, and the groom pays in gold Greif.

### The rite (the Wedding Church)
- **Before the altar of the Bride and Bridegroom**, the couple **drink from one cup**, the **Brautbecher**, a two-handled silver cup of wine with salt. Neither may spill.
- **The exchange**: the bride gives the groom a pinch of **salt** (the body, which she brings to the marriage); the groom gives the bride a **silver coin** (the Moon, which is fertility, and silver, which is wealth). The bride keeps the coin **under her tongue** through the vows (→ I13).
- **The rings**: placed on the **right** ring finger. True wedding rings in Valdau are worn on the right, which is why a ring on the left means something else (→ I56).
- **The vows**: *"Wie oben, so unten. Wie die Braut den Bräutigam, so nehm' ich dich."* (As above, so below. As the Bride took the Bridegroom, so I take you.)
- **The Zeuge** (witness-priest, → I54) binds their joined hands with a silver ribbon and says the blessing. If the Pope-card's bearer witnesses a wedding, the vows bind supernaturally, and couples travel to the Hallgau village of Hallreith for exactly that (→ I46).
- **The Brautlied** is sung as they leave the church (→ I11).

### The feast
- **The Hochzeitsmahl** (wedding feast) lasts all afternoon and into the night: soups, roasts, sweets, wine, beer, and a speech by the Hochzeitlader in rhyme.
- **The Ehrentanz** (dance of honor): the bride dances once with every male guest, in order of precedence. In Rocalba it is **el ball de la Núvia**, the money dance: each man pays a coin to dance a turn with the bride, and the coins are her linen-money.
- **The Brautschuh** (bride's shoe): someone steals the bride's shoe under the table. It goes round the feast and is filled with coins, and the groom must buy it back.
- **The Brautentführung** (bride-kidnapping), the great custom: during the feast, the groom's friends or the Zeche **kidnap the bride**. They smuggle her out to a nearby inn, where she drinks with her kidnappers until the groom finds her. He must then **pay the inn's bill** to get her back.
  - A groom who takes too long to find his bride is mocked.
  - A bride who doesn't seem to want to be found is mocked worse.
  - What happens in the hours at the inn is a matter of jokes so old they have no punch-lines, only looks.
- **Hosenbandwerfen** (garter-throwing): the bride's garter is thrown over her shoulder to the unmarried men. The one who catches it dances with the next Kranzljungfer and is expected to marry within the year.

### The bedding
- **The Bettsetzen** (putting to bed): late at night the guests escort the couple to the **Brautbett** (bridal bed), decorated with flowers and ribbons. They undress them, or help, put them into it, and draw the curtains, with jokes, songs and advice. The Zeche sings Gstanzl outside the window until a candle is put out. In the high valleys the guests sit outside the bedroom door with wine until they've heard enough.
- **The Hochzeitssuppe** (wedding soup): a strong broth with eggs, brought to the couple in bed after midnight, *"for strength."*
- **The Nestel**: all knots in the house are untied, against the Nestelknüpferin (→ I13).

### The morning
- **The Morgengabe** (morning-gift): the husband's gift to his wife on the morning after the wedding night, **legally hers forever**, beyond his creditors and his heirs. Among peasants it is a cow, a silver chain or a featherbed. Among the rich it is jewels, a house, an income, and sometimes **a pledged maid or valet of her own** (→ R07). A meager Morgengabe is a public statement about the wedding night.
- **The Nachhochzeit** (after-wedding): a second feast the next day for the people who were too drunk to enjoy the first.

## 3. Noble weddings

- **The Ehepakten** (marriage articles): negotiated by families and lawyers over months. They fix:
  - the **Brautschatz** (the bride's dowry);
  - the **Widerlage** (the groom's matching settlement on the bride);
  - the **Morgengabe**;
  - the bride's **Witwensitz** (her dower house if widowed, → I18);
  - and pin-money.
  
  In Rocalba they are **capítols matrimonials**, with clauses on who inherits the house (→ I59).
- **The Assay**: both parties' **karat** certified by the Hofprobieramt, with the streaks attached (→ I49). Mismatched metals are negotiated like mismatched dowries.
- **The Stundenamt** sets the wedding hour (Jupiter or Venus) and the **Beilager** hour (→ R02, I51).
- **If the couple are near kin**: the **Nahbrief** from the Church, priced by degree, and for nearest kin the Annealing Edict's conditions (→ I55).
- **Proxy weddings** (*Prokurationshochzeit*): when a royal or great bride marries a groom far away (the Almagran cousins, a foreign prince), she is married **by proxy** in her own city. A proxy, a great noble standing in for the groom, speaks the vows. Then, by a custom older than anyone can explain, the proxy is put into the bride's bed in front of witnesses, fully dressed but for **one bare leg**, which he lays against the bride's, under the covers, for the space of a prayer. This symbolizes the consummation. Proxies are chosen with care. Some proxy-legs have caused scandals.
- **The Beilager** (the witnessed bedding of royal and high marriages): presided over by the **Obersthofmeisterin of Ilsenau**, with the two mothers and a Zeuge at the foot of the state bed. The curtains are drawn at the Stundenamt's hour, and the Obersthofmeisterin certifies before witnesses what she certifies (→ I69, R12).

### Spindelhaus weddings
In a distaff house the **groom marries in** (*Einheirat*). He takes the bride's name, wears the house's livery and kneels to the **Hausmutter** at the feast, and the Ehepakten fix his allowance, not hers. Wiedenbrunn's comic stages have a whole repertoire about Spindelhaus grooms (→ R05, R12).

## 4. Special weddings

- **The kin-wedding (Nahbrief)**: by the **1633 Annealing Edict**, a bride marrying a near or nearest kinsman by dispensation wears a **single silver thread** woven into her veil, the **Nahfaden**, so that the congregation knows. Guests count silver threads at weddings. A bride who has *two* has married a sibling, and the silence in the church is very loud (→ R06, I55, I75).
- **The left-hand wedding** (*Ehe zur linken Hand*): a morganatic or otherwise unequal bond, recorded in the **Linkes Buch**. It is held quietly, **at night**, in a side chapel, with few witnesses. The **ring goes on the left hand**. There is no feast, and sometimes a very good supper (→ I56).
- **The Kettenhochzeit** (chain-wedding): the wedding of two bound people, performed by a Zeuge, with the owner's consent. The rings are **iron**, and the cup is pewter. Marriage does not free them, but by church rule they may not afterwards be sold apart. The household gives the feast from the leftover wine (→ R07).
- **The Verschwisterung** (sister-making) of two women: braided ribbons, one cup, *"Wo du spinnst, will ich spinnen"* (→ R09). Since the 1620s the Bride's See also quietly performs the **Doppelhochzeit** between a **futa** and a woman as marriage, with "bride" and "groom" left out of the vows (→ R10).
- **The Knappenhochzeit** (miners' wedding): in the **Weißach** silver valley, all the year's miners' weddings are held **on one day**, the feast of Saint Daniel the Miner in July. Thirty or forty couples are married at once in the mine-chapel and paraded through the valley, and the betrothed sews the groom's first **silver button** on his Knappenrock (→ I06, I26).
- **The Wildehe** (wild marriage): an **unblessed** union, couples living together without the Church. Common in the poor quarters and the Grenzmark. Children of it are legitimate only if the couple later marries. **Ofenkinder** (children of unblessed kin) are its saddest case (→ R06).

## 5. Foreign weddings

- **Almagra**: **las arras**, thirteen gold coins given by the groom to the bride as a pledge of his support; the bride in black silk and a white mantilla; the couple bound with a **lazo**, a silk cord in a figure-eight, during the vows (→ I32).
- **Ormánd**: the bride-price paid to the bride's family; the night-long feast; at midnight the **kontyolás**, when the bride's hair is put up and her **married woman's cap** pinned on, and she dances her first dance as a wife (→ I33).
- **Kesrân**: the **kına gecesi**, the henna night, when the bride's women paint her hands with henna and sing laments; the wedding contract signed before witnesses; the bride carried to the groom's house on a horse (→ I34).
- **Vellora**: the **dote** (dowry), the notary, the ring; the **mattinata**, a dawn serenade by the groom's friends; and among the patricians, marriage for one sibling only (→ I35).
- **The Sworn Valleys**: Clear Church weddings are plain vows, a handshake, a psalm and a long meal. The Kiltgang (night-courting) leads up to it (→ I36).
- **Asagiri**: the **san-san-kudo**, three sips of rice wine from three cups, three times each, exchanged between bride and groom in silence (→ I37).

## HOOKS

1. **The Brautentführung.** At an allied house's wedding the groom's friends kidnap the bride to an inn, and on the way they also kidnap the protagonist, as a "hostage of honor," by the custom. The bride, at the inn table, is not at all in a hurry to be found.
2. **The proxy's leg.** A cousin of the house is to be married by proxy to a Rocalban count. The proxy chosen for the bare-leg ceremony is the protagonist, as the house's only man of suitable rank. The Obersthofmeisterin presides, and the bride has opinions about the custom.
3. **Two silver threads.** At a court wedding the bride's veil carries two Nahfäden, and the whole church goes silent. The groom is her brother. Afterwards, the bride seeks out an Ilsenau woman about the Beilager, about the Anlasskur, and about how anyone survives the looks.
4. **The Kettensperre.** On the road to a village wedding near the Spiegelsee, the Zeche bars the path with a mine-chain and demand, as the toll for the house's carriage, that the protagonist sing a Gstanzl in praise of a bride. Every Ilsenau woman in the carriage is watching what he will say.
5. **The Morgengabe.** A newly married lady of the circle received, as her Morgengabe, a body-pledge: a beautiful young Zierherr, chosen by her husband. She doesn't know whether it is a gift, a test or a spy, and asks the protagonist, a Zierherr himself by every appearance, to find out.
6. **The Kranzlabend.** A cousin of the house is to marry, and her Kranzlabend is held in the Ilsenau women's wing. By the strictest custom no man may be in the house that night. Somebody has made sure the protagonist doesn't know that.
