# I11 · The Dreher, the Gstanzl & the Bänkelsang

*Music and dance outside the opera house: the scandalous spinning couple-dance creeping up from the villages, the improvised insult-verses sung at the Heuriger, the street-singers who sing the news, the court's dances and the foreign ones, the instruments, and the songs everyone knows.*

## 1. Court dances

- **The Menuett**: the court's principal dance since the 1670s. Small, measured, gliding steps in three-time, the couple approaching and retreating, touching hands, bowing, describing a Z on the floor. Grace, precision and restraint, and every glance counts. A lady's Menuett partner at a court ball is noted in the next day's gossip.
- **The Courante**, **Bourrée** and **Gavotte**: older, livelier French-style dances, taught by Velloran dancing-masters.
- **The Sarabanda**, from Almagra. Slow, stately and smoldering, danced with **castanets**, the couple circling close, with a long pause in which they stand nearly touching and the music holds. In Almagra it was **banned in 1583** as lewd, revived as a slow court dance, and banned again whenever a bishop noticed it. In Wiedenbrunn it is danced late at night, at private balls, by couples who want to be watched. The **Schokoladenpartei** dances it as a declaration of loyalty, and the rest of the court dances it as a declaration of something else.
- **The Spiegeltanz** (the mirror-dance): the kin-dance, two relatives matching each other like a reflection (→ R06, I70).
- **The Kontratanz** (contra-dances): long lines of couples, partners changing down the line. Merry, democratic, and a way to touch everyone's hand in an evening. The last dance of a ball, the **Kehraus** (sweep-out), is always one.

## 2. The Dreher

**Der Dreher** ("the turner"), in some valleys **der Landler**: the village couple-dance of the Hallgau, the Eisenmark and the Sonnleiten.
- The man and woman **hold each other close**: his hand at her waist, hers on his shoulder, bodies touching from chest to knee.
- They **turn**, and keep turning, round and round in a circle in three-time, faster and faster, across the floor of the **Dreherboden** (the dance-floor of an inn).
- Her skirts fly out. Since Valdauer women wear nothing under them (→ I06), the faster the turning the more the room sees.
- At the end of the dance the man lifts her clean off the floor and spins her. At the very end of the night's last Dreher he may kiss her, by custom, and she may slap him, by custom.

It is **the most indecent thing respectable people have ever seen**, say the respectable, and they have tried to stop it:
- **1679**: the **Dreherverbot** in Wiedenbrunn's city ordinance, which forbids "the turning dance" in the city's inns on pain of a fine.
- **1694**: the **Chastity Commission** renews the ban and has the Rumorwache raid three Dreherböden in the Vorstädte (→ I77).

Neither ban touched the Heurigen in the vine-villages outside the walls, where every Sonntag night the Dreher goes on till dawn. There, since the 1680s, the court's young people have been coming in disguise to dance it with vintners' daughters and miners' sons, and with each other.

**The Dreher question of 1699.** The court's young set wants the Dreher danced at the **Fasching court balls** this winter. The old court is appalled. The Schokoladenpartei calls it peasant lewdness, which settles it for the young. The decision belongs to the **Obersthofmeisterin**, who runs the Queen's balls (→ R12, I69). She has not ruled. Her household includes a son who can't remember ever having danced anything, and the whole court is waiting to see whom he dances with first, and what.

## 3. The Gstanzl

A **Gstanzl** (in the high valleys a **Schnaderhüpfl**) is a four-line verse improvised on the spot to a fixed tune, usually mocking, often obscene, sung at the Heuriger, the Kirtag, the Spinnstube and weddings. One singer sings a verse at someone. The target must answer with a verse of their own, **immediately**, within one breath of the music. Whoever fails to answer, or answers lamely, buys the round.

**The Gstanzlsingen** (Gstanzl-duel) can go on for an hour between two good singers: two villages, two rival lovers, a husband and wife, a girl and the boy who climbed the wrong ladder. They mock each other's looks, families, lovers, failures and sex lives in rhyme while the room roars. Nothing said in a Gstanzl may be held against the singer afterwards, by custom. Everything said in one is remembered forever.

**Some Gstanzl the bible can offer** (in Brunnerisch, with English):

> *Da Herr Graf hat a Kinn*
> *wia a Schauf'l so groß,*
> *und sei Schwester, de Gräfin,*
> *hat des sölbige bloß.*
>
> The Count's got a chin / like a shovel so wide, / and his sister the Countess / has the same one, besides.
> *(On any great house. Very old, endlessly adapted.)*

> *I bin a Mondbräutigam,*
> *mei Frau hat de Hos'n,*
> *i derf nur am Spinnrad sitz'n*
> *und d'Kinder verlos'n.*
>
> I'm a Moon-bridegroom, / my wife wears the breeches, / I may only sit by the spinning-wheel / and raffle off the children.
> *(On Ilsenau's in-married husbands. Sung in Ilsenau's hearing at considerable risk.)*

> *Mei Schatz is a Zierherr,*
> *so zart und so fein,*
> *doch wia er an Schlafrock auszogn hat —*
> *Jessas, a Wolf!*
>
> My sweetheart's a Zierherr, / so dainty and fine, / but when he took his dressing-gown off — / heavens, a wolf!
> *(The Wolf im Schlafrock, → R11. Doesn't rhyme on purpose. That's the joke.)*

> *Im Rosengrund, im Rosengrund,*
> *da kostet's an Bräutling d'Stund',*
> *und wer an Saturn erwischt,*
> *der zahlt und kriagt nix.*
>
> In the Rosengrund, in the Rosengrund, / it's a silver-couple an hour, / and whoever gets a Saturn hour / pays and gets nothing.
> *(→ I23, R02.)*

**The Rocalban equivalent** is the **corrandes** of the island villages and the **cobles** of the coast: improvised mocking quatrains, Catalan, with a guitar, at weddings and in taverns. They are just as rude and, Rocalbans insist, much better rhymed.

## 4. The Bänkelsang

**Bänkelsänger** (bench-singers) are street-singers who stand on a little wooden bench in a market square with a tall painted board beside them, the **Moritatentafel**, divided into panels like a comic strip. As they sing a long ballad they point with a stick at each panel in turn.

Their songs are **Moritaten** (murder-ballads) and scandal-songs: the latest murder, fire, monster, execution, two-headed calf, and above all the latest **Trump fall** and the latest scandal at court. These are the **Fallballaden**, fall-ballads. Each Fallballade tells, with heroic inaccuracy, who died, who got the card, and what that reveals about whose beds they'd been in. When a card falls somewhere surprising, there is a Bänkelsänger on the Hoher Markt with a freshly painted board within three days (→ I48).

After the song they sell the printed lyrics, **Liedflugblätter**, for a Kupferling each. They are censored by the Bücherkommission in theory and tolerated in practice, because the Rumorwache likes the songs (→ I12). The best of them are famous:
- **"Der Fall der Kaiserin"** (1693): who got the Empress? (Nobody knows, but the song names three suspects, all wrong.)
- **"Die Ballade vom gläsernen König"**, the Glass King of Almagra, *"who broke his heart by looking at it."* The Almagran ambassador has asked for it to be suppressed twice.
- **"Das Lied vom Pagat-Franzl"** (1698): the card-sharp who got the luck-card and can't play cards anymore.
- **"Die Nacht der nassen Perücken"** (the Night of the Wet Wigs, 1695), the end of the Chastity Commission (→ I77).

A Bänkelsänger who sings about a living great house is courting the Rumorwache. One who sings about a great house that's out of favor is courting a commission from its enemies.

## 5. Folk and foreign dances

- **Rocalba**:
  - **el ball de bastons** (the stick-dance): lines of men in white with ribbons, striking each other's batons in rhythm, a dance of the **Bastos** guilds (→ I29);
  - **el contrapàs**: a slow, solemn circle dance danced on the abbey terrace at Montalba at the great feasts;
  - **el ball de la Núvia** (the bride's dance) at weddings: each male guest pays a coin to dance a turn with the bride, and the coins go to the couple. A popular bride dances for an hour and earns her linen (→ I17).
- **Ormánd**: the **verbunk**, the recruiting-dance. Hussars dance before the village men to lure them into the army: stamping, leaping, heel-clicking, slapping their boots, ever faster. It's irresistible, and many a peasant boy has danced himself into a uniform. Ormándi women dance the **karikázó**, a ring-dance in which they choose the men who may enter the ring.
- **Kesrân**: the **zenne**, male dancers in women's dress, dancing to the drum and the flute with castanets of brass, slow, swaying, then whirling (→ R11, I34). The embassy's zenne perform at Wiedenbrunn salons for enormous fees.
- **Asagiri**: slow, masked court dances that Valdauers find either sublime or interminable (→ I37).
- **The Grenzmark**: the **kolo**, a long chain of men and women holding hands, dancing in a circle that speeds up until somebody falls.

## 6. Instruments

- **Strings**: **violin** (Geige), everywhere; **viola da gamba** at court; **zither** in the Alpine villages, a box of strings played on a table; Rocalban **guitar**; Kesrâni **oud**.
- **Hackbrett** (hammered dulcimer): an Alpine and Heuriger favorite, its metal strings struck with little hammers, bright and ringing.
- **Winds**: Rocalban **gralla** (a shrill shawm that can be heard three streets away) and **flabiol**; Ormándi **tárogató** (a reed horn); Kesrâni **ney** (end-blown flute); court **recorders** and **oboes**.
- **Maultrommel** (jew's harp): the **Fensterl instrument**. A young man stands under a window at night playing a tune on it, quietly, so only the girl hears. If the candle appears, he climbs (→ R08). Fathers have learned the tune too.
- **Drums**: the Kesrâni **mehter** band of the siege, with kettle-drums, cymbals and shawms, terrifying. Since 1683 its music has been copied by Valdauer regiments as **Kesrânermusik**, and is now the fashion for military bands and ballrooms.
- **Ormándi cimbalom**: a great hammered dulcimer, the heart of the Ormándi band.
- **The Glockenspiel** of the Dom: Venus-cast bells (→ R03) that play a wedding-tune on the hour on Venustag.

## 7. Songs everyone knows

- **The Brautlied** (bride-song), sung at every wedding as the bride enters: *"Jetzt kommt die Braut vom weißen Berg"* ("Now the Bride comes from the white mountain").
- **Wiegenlieder** (lullabies). Every Valdauer knows *"Schlaf, Kindlein, schlaf, / der Vater hüt' die Schaf'…"* In the Ilsenau household it is sung in Dides-Catalan as *"Son, soneta, vine, vine…"* The protagonist knows both by heart and doesn't know how (→ R12, I53).
- **Weinlieder** (wine-songs) of the Heurigen: sentimental, death-haunted and cheerful about it. *"Es wird a Wein sein, und mir wer'n nimmer sein"* ("There'll be wine, and we'll be gone"). Wiedenbrunn's great theme is that it's lovely to be alive, tomorrow we'll be dead, and therefore another Seidel.
- **Trinklieder** (drinking songs), soldiers' songs, and Grenzer songs of the frontier.
- **"Die Spinnerin"**: the queen's song, about Margarete die Spinnerin (→ R05). The women of the Spindelhäuser sing it at their spinning.
- **Hymns**: the **Hochzeitsvesper** chant on Midsummer, and the slow Erbentag carols.

## HOOKS

1. **The first Dreher.** At a Heuriger in the Wiedenwald, in disguise, a woman of the household pulls the protagonist onto the Dreherboden. He has never danced anything that he remembers. His body seems to know the Dreher anyway, perfectly, and she wants to know who taught him.
2. **The Gstanzl at the Heuriger.** A Sonnleiten vintner's daughter sings a Gstanzl at the protagonist across the Heuriger tables: *"the Moon's new boy so pale and fine / I wonder if he's a wolf or a lamb."* The whole room waits for him to answer within a breath.
3. **The Fallballade.** A Bänkelsänger on the Hoher Markt has a new board, panel six of which shows a white castle on a lake and a sleeping figure in a bed. Nobody in the household commissioned it.
4. **The Sarabanda.** At a private ball of the Schokoladenpartei, a lady asks the protagonist to dance the Sarabanda with her, with castanets, in front of the Almagran ambassador.
5. **The Obersthofmeisterin's ruling.** The Dreher question must be settled before the first Fasching ball. The young set has sent a delegation to the protagonist, who sleeps under the Obersthofmeisterin's roof, to plead their case, and to teach him the steps in case he has to show her.
6. **The Maultrommel.** At Bad Sulzau, the third night running, someone is playing a jew's harp under the protagonist's window. It isn't for him; the window used to be someone else's. Whoever it is doesn't know that yet.
