# I10 · Sport & the Hunt

*How the Double Crown exercises its bodies and its rivalries: the court hunt and the lake-hunt, the ladies' tournament, indoor tennis and skittles, shooting matches with satirical targets, winter on sledges and ice, summer on water, the village strength-contests, fencing schools and the riding school, and the animals kept along the way.*

**See also:** I09 Parlor & Table Games · I71 The Damenduell · I20 The Rumorwache (the Ehrenhandel) · I26 The Weißach & the Spiegelsee · I24 Hallgau & Bad Sulzau · I22 Wiedenbrunn · I06 (no drawers, and why it matters on a sledge)

## 1. The Hofjagd (the court hunt)

Autumn is the hunting season. From the **Ägidi Fair** (1 September) until the snow, the court hunts.

- **The eingestellte Jagd** (enclosed hunt): the Baroque court's great spectacle. For days beforehand hundreds of peasants (on Robot, → R01) drive the game of a whole forest, red deer, boar, roe and hare, into a vast enclosure of canvas and netting. On the day the beasts are driven past a gilded pavilion, the **Jagdschirm**, from which the court shoots. It is a slaughter, it is a party, and the bag is counted in hundreds and painted on a board.
- **The Damenstand** (ladies' stand): a separate, prettier pavilion from which the court's ladies shoot with light fowling-pieces. Ladies' bags are kept in a separate book. Several great ladies are very fine shots. The best shot of 1698 was an Ilsenau Erbtochter, which the Starkenberg princes still won't discuss.
- **The Parforcejagd** (par force hunting): the Almagran style, imported with the Ceremonial. A single stag is chased with hounds on horseback across country until it stands at bay. It is fast, dangerous and muddy. Ladies ride it astride, in breeches, which is the only time a respectable lady wears **Unterhosen** (→ I06), and everyone notices.
- **The Beizjagd** (falconry): Ormándi **saker falcons**, the Kerecsényi family's pride (→ I45). Ladies fly merlins from the wrist, and a lady's merlin is a love-token as often as a hunting bird.
- **The Wasserjagd** (water-hunt): a stag is driven into a lake and shot from gilded boats as it swims, with music playing on the shore. **Ilsenau** holds the most famous one each September on the **Spiegelsee**, from white boats, with the court's ladies in silver and blue (→ I26, R12). The stag swims toward the Stille Haus shore by long habit, and the boatmen say no stag of the Wasserjagd has ever been allowed to reach it.

**The hunt as assignation**: a hunt spreads a court over miles of forest. Couples are separated from the field, horses go lame in convenient copses, and lodges have back rooms. *"Sich im Wald verirren"*, to get lost in the woods, means exactly what it sounds like. The **Jagdball** (hunt ball) that evening sorts out who got lost with whom.

## 2. Ring-tilting and the Damenkarussell

- **Ringstechen** (ring-tilting): a rider at the gallop tries to carry off a small ring hung from a post with the point of a lance. The court's gentlemen tilt in costume at the **Winterreitschule** (winter riding school, see §8) every Fasching and on Brauttag. Points are given for rings, for style and for not falling off.
- **The Kesrânerkopf**: since the Second Siege, the targets of the tilting-course include painted wooden **Kesrâni heads** on posts, struck at the gallop with lance, pistol and sword. It is crude, popular, and an embarrassment when the Kesrâni ambassador attends. He has attended every year since 1690, and applauds the hits with unsettling enthusiasm.
- **The Damenkarussell** (ladies' carousel): first held in **1689**, after Queen Margarethe Ludovika's ladies complained that tilting was wasted on men. The court's women ride (and some drive light carriages) through the same course as the men, at the rings, the Kesrânerköpfe and the painted wooden **Planetenschilde** (planet-shields), with lances, pistols and sabres, in costume as Amazons, Ormándi hussars or the Planets.
  - It is held each **Brauttag** (→ I14).
  - The prize is a silver crescent, offered, by tradition since 1689, by the **Obersthofmeisterin**.
  - It is fiercely contested, and some of the court's sapphic rivalries began there (→ R09).
  - The men watch from the galleries, and their wagers are heavy.
  - Several great ladies have won it three times. One Mirabent countess won it riding astride in breeches, and was fined by the Kleidervisitatoren and cheered by the court.

## 3. The Ballhaus (indoor tennis)

**The Ballhaus** on the **Ballhausgasse** behind the Greifenburg is a tall, long, whitewashed hall with a gallery for spectators. There the court plays **Ballspiel**, tennis with racquets of gut and a hard ball of leather stuffed with dog-hair, off the walls and the sloping roofs of the galleries. The rules are complicated and the **Ballmeister** keeps them.
- Players play in **shirtsleeves and breeches**: the only time a gentleman takes off his coat in public. The galleries are therefore full of ladies.
- **Wagers** are heavy, recorded by the Ballmeister in his book.
- **Ladies play too**, in the mornings, by an arrangement with the Ballmeister that everyone knows about and nobody mentions. They play in their shifts and petticoats, and the gallery is closed. The gallery is never entirely empty.
- Wiedenbrunn's other Ballhaus, in the Rosengrund, is a gambling hall that has never seen a racquet.

## 4. Kegeln (skittles)

Every Gasthaus, Heuriger and Bürger garden has a **Kegelbahn**, a long wooden alley with nine pins set in a diamond at the end. Players roll a heavy wooden ball and count the pins.
- **"Alle Neune!"** ("All nine!") is a perfect strike, and the shout for any total success.
- The best player at a Kegelbahn for the season is crowned **Kegelkönig** (skittles king). He wears a paper crown and drinks free on Kegel nights.
- **Women's Kegel nights**: the Wäschermädel, the Bürger wives' circles, and since the 1690s some daring court ladies at the Heurigen. Their Kegelkönigin crowns are more fought over than the men's.
- Wagers are in beer, and the losers pay the round. A **"Pudel"** (poodle) is a ball that misses every pin, and "*einen Pudel schieben*" (to roll a poodle) means to fail completely.

## 5. Shooting: the Schützenfest

Every town has its **Schützengesellschaft**, a shooting company of Bürger with crossbows and muskets. Each summer it holds a **Schützenfest**: a week of shooting at targets, feasting, and crowning the **Schützenkönig** (best shot), who has the right to choose the queen of the festival ball.

- **The Schützenscheiben** (painted targets): Valdauer targets are wooden discs painted with pictures, the bull's-eye set somewhere in the scene. By tradition the pictures are **satirical**, about the scandals of the year, and the shooting tears them apart. A good Schützenscheibe is commissioned from a real painter and kept afterwards, shot to pieces, in the company's hall. Some famous ones:
  - **1683**: the Grand Vizier's tent (bull's-eye on the coffee sacks).
  - **1688**: the Philter Trial, with the bull's-eye on the countess's cup (→ I42).
  - **1692**: **the Wolf im Schlafrock** (→ R11), a wolf in a silk dressing-gown aiming at a row of ladies' hearts. Every man in the company shot at it, and it was shot to splinters.
  - **1694**: the Chastity Commissioner with his breeches round his ankles (→ I77).
  - **1697**: the Glass King, with the bull's-eye on his heart, "to see if it would break." The Almagran ambassador complained; the Schützengesellschaft declined to apologize, and the Schokoladenpartei boycotted the feast.
  - The 1699 target has not been chosen. Everybody is wondering.
- **Grenzer sharpshooters** win the long-range prizes every year with their long rifles (→ I28).
- **The Sworn Valleys'** pikemen of the Schwurgarde compete with crossbows and are very good at it (→ I36).

## 6. Winter

- **The Schlittenfahrt** (sledge procession): the great court entertainment of Fasching when there is snow. Thirty or forty gilded sledges are carved as swans, dragons, shells and planets. Each is drawn by a horse in bells and plumes, with a **lady seated in front** wrapped in furs and **her cavalier standing behind** to drive. The procession runs by torchlight through the streets to a country inn or a palace outside the walls for supper and dancing, and back at dawn. Who drives whose sledge is assigned by the Obersthofmeisterin, which makes the list the most politically significant document of the winter. On the way back, sledges get separated, and inns have back rooms (→ R08).
- **Sledging** (*Rodeln*): wooden toboggans down the hills of the Wiedenwald and the Spiegelsee bank. Upsets are frequent, and women wear no drawers (→ I06). The court finds this very funny, and everybody comes back rosy.
- **Skating** (*Eislaufen*): on the frozen arms of the Selde, the castle moats and the **Spiegelsee**, which freezes black and clear as glass so you can see the fish under your feet. Ladies are pushed in **Eisschlitten** (chair-sledges) by gentlemen on skates. Couples skate arm in arm, then hand in hand, then however they like.
- **Eisstockschießen** (ice-stock): curling with wooden stocks with handles, slid at a target. It is the Hallgau peasants' winter game, with beer and wagers, and the Bad Sulzau court plays it in furs for fun.

## 7. Summer and the water

- **Swimming**: few Valdauers swim well. The Ilsenau women all do, because of the **Mondnacht** (→ R12). Wiedenbrunn has **Badeplätze**, bathing places on the Selde, with separate men's and women's stretches divided by a fence that does not reach all the way into the water. Every summer someone swims round the end of it.
- **Boating**: rowing and sailing on the Spiegelsee, the Hallgau lakes and the Selde. **Gondolas** have been imported from Vellora for the Greifenburg's garden canal, along with a gondolier who sings, for evening parties.
- **The Fischerstechen** (fishermen's joust): two boats rowed at each other, a man standing in each bow with a padded pole, each trying to knock the other into the water. It is a Hallgau and Selde festival sport. Bad Sulzau's summer court holds a gentlemen's version, which ends with half the court's young men soaked through in white shirts. The ladies' gallery is always full.
- **Horse races** in the Hirschau meadows on Brauttag and at the Ägidi Fair: Ormándi riders against Valdauer, wagers enormous (→ I63).

## 8. The Winterreitschule (the riding school)

**The Winterreitschule** is a long white hall beside the Greifenburg, with galleries and chandeliers, built in **1681**. There the court's **white horses**, bred at the **Hofgestüt Kornau** from Almagran blood (→ R01), are schooled in the **Hohe Schule**, the high school of dressage: the **Levade** (the horse rearing and holding the pose), the **Capriole** (leaping and kicking out in the air), the **Piaffe** (trotting in place).
- Performances on feast days are among the court's most admired spectacles.
- **The Bereiter** (riding masters) are Almagran-trained and very grand.
- **Riding lessons** for the court's young people happen here, with mirrors on the walls. The riding masters are much in demand as tutors, of riding and otherwise, and the lessons are famously long.

## 9. The village contests

At the **Kirtag** (parish feast), the Brauttag and the harvest:
- **Fingerhakeln** (finger-pulling): two men sit facing each other across a table, their middle fingers hooked through a leather loop, and pull until one is dragged across the table. Fingers dislocate.
- **Ranggeln** (Alpine wrestling): in linen shirts and breeches, on grass, holding each other's belts, until one has both shoulders down. The **Hågmoar**, the champion of a valley, is a man every girl's mother warns her about.
- **Steinheben** (stone-lifting): lifting a great stone to the shoulder, or carrying it.
- **The Brautlauf**: the unmarried women's footrace on Brauttag (→ I14).
- **Baumkraxeln** (pole-climbing): a greased pole with a ham, a sausage-ring and a ribbon at the top.
- The winners have the attention of the girls, the Gstanzl-singers and the Straw Court (→ I60, I11).

---

## 10. Fencing

- **The Fechtboden** (fencing school): Wiedenbrunn has six, run by **Klingenmeister** (blade-masters). Gentlemen take lessons in the small-sword and the sabre. It is the only honest preparation for the **Ehrenhandel**, the duel of honor (→ I20).
- **Ladies' lessons**: since the **Damenduell** became fashionable (→ I71), two Fechtböden give lessons to ladies in the mornings, in shifts and stays, with the doors locked. The Klingenmeister **Matthias Rauhenwald**, a famously handsome Rocalban-trained master, has taught half the duelists of the 1690s and is the subject of at least one Kammerbüchl.
- **Mars tincture** is forbidden in the Fechtböden, and in honest duels (→ R03). Students who take it are expelled.

## 11. Animals

- **Hounds**: **Bracken** (scent-hounds) for the Parforcejagd, greyhounds for coursing, and the great **Saupacker** (boar-hounds) in spiked collars.
- **Falcons**: Ormándi sakers for the men, merlins for the ladies.
- **Horses**: the white Kornau horses for the riding school and state carriages; Ormándi horses for war and racing; Hallgau **Haflinger**-type mountain horses, chestnut with blond manes, for everything else.
- **Lapdogs**: the **Velloraner Hündchen**, small, white and curly, in every lady's lap and in many ladies' beds; the **Mops** (pug), imported from Asagiri by way of Vellora, the newest fashion and the most expensive. A pug on a velvet cushion in the Loge is the 1699 lady's accessory (→ I03).
- **The royal menagerie** in the **Hirschau** (→ I22): a lion, two bears, Kesrâni gazelles, a camel, an Asagiri crane, and since 1685 an **elephant**, a gift of the Padishah with the Peace of the Willow Bridge, named **Selim** and much beloved. The Kesrâni ambassador visits him on Venustag afternoons.
- **Cockfights** and **dogfights** in the Rosengrund. **Bear-baiting** was banned in 1690, and still goes on in the Kornau on fair days.

## HOOKS

1. **The Wasserjagd.** At the Ilsenau lake-hunt the stag breaks the tradition and makes for the Stille Haus shore. The boat that must turn it back is the protagonist's, and the woman beside him with the gun is in no hurry to shoot.
2. **The sledge list.** The Obersthofmeisterin's list for the Fasching Schlittenfahrt assigns the protagonist to drive the sledge of a lady of the Schokoladenpartei, whose husband is drinking chocolate with the Almagran ambassador, and whose sledge is carved as a crescent moon.
3. **The Damenkarussell.** A futa countess of the Mirabent, three times winner, challenges any Ilsenau woman to take the silver crescent from her at Brauttag. She has said publicly, and at length, what she will claim as a forfeit if she wins.
4. **The 1699 Schützenscheibe.** The Schützengesellschaft is choosing this year's target, and a rumor about the Ilsenau Moon has reached the painters. The household has a fortnight to make sure the target is about something else.
5. **The Ballhaus gallery.** On a ladies' morning at the Ballhaus, the gallery is supposed to be empty. The protagonist is in it, by an invitation he didn't know was a dare.
6. **The Fischerstechen.** At Bad Sulzau the protagonist is entered in the gentlemen's Fischerstechen against a Starkenberg officer. Everyone expects the Zierherr to go into the brine in the first pass, and the ladies' gallery is fuller than it has been all summer.
