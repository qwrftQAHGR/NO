# I04 · Below Stairs: Livery, Wages and the Gesindebuch

*The people who run a great house: their ranks, their colors, their wages and tips, the book that follows a free servant from post to post, the line between the free and the ribboned, and the night each Fasching when the servants dance with the masters.*

**See also:** I03 The Palais & its Hours · R07 Bondage · I02 The Card Visit (Türgeld) · I20 The Rumorwache (the Naderer) · I57 The Ammenhaus · R12 House Ilsenau

---

## 1. Ranks

A great Wiedenbrunn household runs to between eighty and two hundred people, plus the stables. They are arranged in a hierarchy as rigid as the court's, and enforced at least as jealously.

### The upper servants (the *Kammertisch*, who dine apart)
- **The Haushofmeister**, house-steward. He runs everything below the family: accounts, hiring, the Hausordnung and the keys. Often a minor noble or a Hofrat's son, and addressed as *Herr Haushofmeister*. In a Spindelhaus he is often a **Haushofmeisterin**.
- **The Kammerfrau**, the lady's chief woman. She dresses her mistress, keeps her jewels, her letters and her secrets, and commands the Zofen. The most powerful woman in the house after the family.
- **The Kammerdiener**, the gentleman's valet. He shaves, dresses and undresses his master, sleeps at his door and keeps his purse. He knows every one of his master's habits.
- **The Sekretär**: letters, ciphers, accounts. Usually a Bürger with ambitions.
- **The Hofmeister**: tutor and governor of the young gentlemen, and their companion on the Kavalierstour (→ I19).
- **The Hauskaplan**: the house's own Zeuge, who says the daily Mahl in the Hauskapelle and hears the family's Zeugnis (confession).
- **The Leibarzt**, the house physician, and in alchemical houses the **Hausalchimist(in)** (→ R03).
- **The Mundkoch**, master-cook, and the **Zuckerbäcker**, confectioner. Both are paid more than most officers.
- **The Kellermeister**, cellar-master, and the **Stallmeister**, master of horse.

### The middle servants
- **Zofen** and **Kammerjungfern**: the lady's maids, who dress hair, care for clothes, carry notes and lie for their mistresses.
- **Kammerknechte** and **Bettmägde**: body-servants of the bedchamber, usually pledged, often more (→ R07).
- **Lakaien**, footmen in livery. They wait at table, ride on the back of the carriage, carry cards up and hold doors. They are chosen for height, legs and looks, and paired by height for the carriage.
- **Heiducken**: very tall footmen in Ormándi-style dress (braided coats, fur caps, sabres). They are a fashion of the great houses since the Second Siege, standing at doors and behind chairs to impress. Many are genuine Ormándi, and most are Kornau farm-boys with mustaches.
- **Läufer** (running footmen): they run ahead of the carriage in feathered caps and white silk, carrying a silver-topped staff to clear the way and announce their master. They are fast, beautiful and short-lived, since running Wiedenbrunn's streets in all weather ruins the lungs. They live on a little bottle of Eisenmut and die young (→ R03).
- **The Portier** at the gate (→ I03), with his staff, his bandolier and his memory.
- **Kutscher** (coachmen), **Vorreiter** (postilions) and **Sesselträger** (chair-bearers, in houses that keep their own sedan chairs, → I21).
- **The Köchin** (cook) for the servants' table, and the kitchen staff.

### The lower servants
- **Stubenmädchen**: chambermaids who make beds, sweep, dust and light the candles.
- **Küchenmägde** (kitchen maids) and **Küchenburschen** (kitchen boys); **Wäscherinnen** (washerwomen); **Büglerinnen** (ironers).
- **Wasserer** (water-carriers), **Holzknechte** (wood-carriers), the **Ofenheizer** in the Heizgang (→ I03), and **Stallburschen** (stable boys).
- **The Leibstuhlmagd**, who empties the close-stools and is the lowest rank in the house.

### Special to some houses
- **Ammen** (wet-nurses) and **Kindsmägde** (nursemaids). In Ilsenau, the Ammenhaus over the Milchsteg (→ I57).
- **Schlafwärterinnen** (sleeper-nurses), in houses with a sleeper (→ I53, R12).
- **The Stundenglöckner** (→ R02).
- **Hofzwerge** (court dwarves), kept by two or three old-fashioned houses and despised by the modern ones.
- **The Kesrânerl**: a Kesrâni captive kept as a coffee-servant in Kesrâni dress, the fashion since 1683. They are taught to brew in the embassy's way, and are often far better educated than the household realizes (→ R07, I34).

## 2. Free and ribboned

A great house's staff is roughly **half free and half pledged**.

- **Free servants**, the **Dienstboten** or **Gesinde**, are hired for a year at a time from **Lichtmess** (Candlemas, 2 February), the realm's hiring day. They are paid wages, fed and clothed, and may leave at the end of the year with a good reference in their **Gesindebuch** (§3).
- **Pledged servants**, the **Pfandleute** (→ R07), are bound for a term. They are unpaid beyond board, clothes and pocket-money, and can't leave. They wear the house's **Pfandband** at the throat. A Leibpfand, a body-pledge, is the house's to use.

**The free despise the pledged** as **Bandler** ("ribbon-folk"). Bandler work for nothing and take free people's posts, and the free servants fear the day they'll be replaced by them. **The pledged despise the free** in return. Free servants have to worry about wages and references; the pledged have only to please. And a pledge may be **loved**, which a free maid can only hope for.

**In practice they marry each other, sleep with each other, betray each other and cover for each other**, like any household.

## 3. The Gesindebuch

Every free servant in Valdau carries a **Gesindebuch** (servant's book), required since the **Gesindeordnung of 1642**. It is a small bound book, stamped by the Rumorwache, in which each employer writes a **Zeugnis** (a reference) when the servant leaves: dates of service, conduct, reason for leaving. A servant with no Gesindebuch can't be hired. A servant with a bad Zeugnis can't be hired by anyone who matters. A servant **"entlassen ohne Zeugnis"** (dismissed without a reference) has one place left to go, which is the Rosengrund.

So the Gesindebuch is the strongest hold a master has over a free servant: stronger than wages, and almost as strong as a ribbon. Masters write in it, by long convention, in **a code every servant and every master can read**:

| Written | Means |
|---|---|
| *"treu und fleißig"* (faithful and diligent) | a good servant |
| *"fleißig"* alone | diligent, and nothing else to recommend her |
| *"stets zur Zufriedenheit"* (always to satisfaction) | adequate |
| *"bemüht"* (made efforts) | useless |
| *"verschwiegen"* (discreet) | knows things; keep her |
| *"neugierig"* (curious) | a gossip, a listener, possibly a Naderer (police informer) |
| *"anhänglich"* (attached, devoted) | slept with the master, or the mistress |
| *"sehr anhänglich"* | slept with *both* |
| *"gesellig"* (sociable) | drinks; or entertains followers in the attic |
| *"von angenehmem Äußeren"* (of pleasant appearance) | hired for looks, and the looks were used |
| *"kinderlieb"* (fond of children) | left pregnant |
| *"verlässt den Dienst auf eigenen Wunsch"* (leaving of her own wish) | the mistress threw her out and doesn't want to say why |

Servants read their own books, of course, and a master who writes *anhänglich* knows the next employer will read it too. Forging Gesindebuch entries is a thriving trade in the Rosengrund. A **good forged book** costs 2 Bräutlinge, and a book in the forged hand of a famous house costs ten.

## 4. Wages, keep and tips

*Yearly wages in a great Wiedenbrunn house, plus board, lodging and livery. Compare X02 prices.*

| Position | Yearly wage |
|---|---|
| Haushofmeister | 40–80 Greif |
| Kammerfrau / Kammerdiener | 6 Greif (60 Bräutlinge) |
| Mundkoch | 10 Greif |
| Zofe | 3 Greif |
| Lakai | 2½ Greif (25 Bräutlinge) |
| Heiduck | 3 Greif (paid for height) |
| Läufer | 3 Greif, and a pension to the widow if he dies in service, which he will |
| Portier | 2 Greif, and the door |
| Stubenmädchen | 1½ Greif (15 Bräutlinge) |
| Küchenmagd | 1 Greif |
| Leibstuhlmagd | 8 Bräutlinge |
| Pledges | no wage; pocket money of a few Kupferlinge a week, at the house's pleasure |

Wages are the smaller part of what good servants earn. The larger part is **tips**:
- **Türgeld** ("door-money"), from visitors at the door and the stair (→ I02). The Portier and the Lakaien at the Feststiege can double their wages in a good season. Footmen fight over who stands at the door during calling hours, and the Haushofmeister sells the posts.
- **Neujahrsgeld**: New Year's money from the family and from every regular visitor.
- **Vails**: the departing guest's gift to the servants who served him. Guests at country houses tip the whole household at the end of a visit, and a stingy guest's carriage will lose a wheel at the gate.
- **Schlüsselgeld** ("key-money"): the Portier's fee for opening the gate after hours to a visitor who will not be mentioned. It is the most reliable income in the house.
- **Discarded clothes**: a lady's Kammerfrau and Zofen inherit her last season's gowns, which they wear, sell or cut down. The second-hand clothes market of the **Tandelmarkt** runs on them.

## 5. Livery

Servants in livery are walking heraldry: the house's colors on a coat, waistcoat and breeches, with braid in the house's metal. Pledges wear the same colors at the throat. **Free servants never wear the colors at the throat** (→ R07).

| House | Livery |
|---|---|
| Greifenstein (royal) | scarlet and gold |
| **Ilsenau** | midnight blue ("Ilsenauer Blau") and silver |
| Mirabent | cinnabar red and white |
| Holdegg | verdigris green and copper |
| Starkenberg | black and red |
| Ehrenreith | grey and white |
| Kalteneck | black and lead-grey |
| Sulzbach | salt-white and blue (the cheek of it) |

A house's livery is **exempt from the Kleiderordnung** (→ I06). A great house's footman may wear silver lace that his Bürger cousin would be fined for, which the Bürger resent more than almost anything. On Sundays liveried servants promenade on the Graben in their colors and look down on the shopkeepers.

## 6. Customs of the servants' hall

- **The two tables**: upper servants dine at the **Kammertisch**, served by under-servants, with wine. The rest dine at the **Gesindetisch** with beer. Precedence at the Gesindetisch mirrors precedence at the masters' table: visiting servants take the rank of their own masters, so a duke's valet outranks a count's cook. Servants' precedence disputes are as bitter as the court's.
- **Names**: servants are called by first name or by post (*"the Portier"*). Upper servants are called by surname (*"Huber!"*). By custom, Kammerfrauen are *Frau* + surname to everyone below stairs, married or not.
- **Kitchen-words for the masters**:
  - **die Herrschaft**: the employers, collectively.
  - **der Gnädige**, **die Gnädige**: his lordship, her ladyship.
  - **oben**, upstairs: *"Oben ist Gewitter"*, there's a storm upstairs.
  - **die Alte**, **der Alte**: the old woman, the old man. Never within earshot.
  - **das Fräulein**, **der junge Herr**: the daughter, the son.
- **Gossip**: the servants of every great house know the servants of every other. They meet at the Bader, the market, church, the **Ammenhaus** courtyard and the Heuriger. Together they know everything in Wiedenbrunn before the coffeehouses do, and they sell it to the Naderer (→ I20) and the Fall-Reckoners (→ I48).
- **The Kammerkätzchen** ("chamber-kitten"): the stock figure of the flirtatious, available chambermaid in every comedy and Kammerbüchl. In real houses she is usually a Leibmagd, a contract, and a person with her own opinion of the comedies.
- **Marriage**: servants need the Haushofmeister's leave to marry. Upper servants who marry each other often stay on. Others leave. Pledges' Kettenhochzeiten are celebrated in the servants' hall with the family's leftover wine (→ R07).

## 7. The Dienstbotenball

Every Fasching each great house gives a **Dienstbotenball**, a servants' ball, in its own Salon or in a rented hall. The masters come down for the first hour. The servants dance in their best, the pledges in borrowed silks, the Heiducken in full braid.

The night's great custom is **the Herrschaftstanz**, the masters' dance. For one dance, each member of the family dances with a servant chosen by the servants: the Fürstin with the Portier, the young lord with the prettiest Stubenmädchen, the daughter of the house with the tallest Heiduck. The choosing is done by the servants in secret beforehand, and is a matter of intense politics below stairs. Then the masters withdraw and the real ball begins.

What happens in the Herrschaftstanz is not talked about afterwards. That rule is the most broken rule in Wiedenbrunn.

## 8. The Ilsenau household (specifics)

*(House in R12.)*
- It is **overwhelmingly women**: Kammerfrauen, Zofen, Bettmägde, Stubenmädchen, the nurses over the Milchsteg, and a Haushofmeisterin. The men are coachmen, a few footmen, the Portier, the Mondbräutigame's valets, and (formerly) the Silberknechte (→ I50).
- **The Ilsenau Pfandband** is midnight-blue silk velvet with a silver crescent clasp. It is the most beautiful pledge-ribbon in Wiedenbrunn, and some free servants of the house wear a plain blue one at the wrist, not the throat, as a mark of belonging.
- **The Schweigeschwur**, the house oath of silence, sworn by anyone who serves near the family's secrets (→ R12).
- **The Schlafwärterinnen** of the Stille Haus, the six sleeper-nurses, outrank Zofen and dine at the Kammertisch. Below stairs they are regarded with awe, and some envy, and they are asked about him constantly.
- **The Dides**, Rocalban wet-nurses trained at the Torre, speak **Dides-Katalanisch** among themselves and have their own table, their own saints and their own opinions (→ R12, I57).

## HOOKS

1. **"Sehr anhänglich."** A maid who served the household before the waking shows the protagonist her Gesindebuch, asking him to write her new Zeugnis since the Haushofmeisterin refuses. The previous entry, from another house, reads *sehr anhänglich*. She'd like this one to say something else, and has an opinion about what.
2. **The Herrschaftstanz.** The servants' secret vote for the Dienstbotenball has chosen a partner for the son of the house. The partner is a body-pledge whose term ends in the spring, and the servants' hall is betting on what happens before then.
3. **The Läufer's bottle.** The house's best Läufer collapses on the Graben, his Eisenmut bottle empty. Taking him to the Pockenhaus means losing him, while keeping him means the house physician, and he has something to say before either.
4. **The Portier's book.** The Portier keeps a private book of every after-hours visitor, the Schlüsselgeld ledger. Someone outside the house has offered him a great deal of money for the last month's pages.
5. **Lichtmess.** On hiring day a strapping young woman arrives at the Palais gate with a perfect Gesindebuch, every entry from a great house, every Zeugnis *treu und fleißig, verschwiegen*. Every entry is forged, beautifully, and she wants a post near the son of the house.
6. **The Heiduck.** One of the household's Heiducken turns out to be a genuine Ormándi nobleman of a ruined family who took the post to be near Wiedenbrunn's court for reasons he won't explain. He is very tall, very proud, and has been watching the protagonist with an expression nobody can read.
