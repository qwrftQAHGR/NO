# I02 · The Card Visit

*How the Double Crown calls on one another: calling cards written on the backs of playing cards (and what each card means), calling hours, the Jour, how long to stay, what to bring, what to tip, and how a visit becomes an assignation.*

**See also:** I01 Address & Greeting · I03 The Palais & its Hours · I04 Below Stairs · I07 The Toilette · R04 The Cards · I67 Tarock & the Coffeehouses

---

## 1. Playing cards as calling cards

Nobody in Wiedenbrunn has a printed visiting card. When you call and the lady isn't at home, or you want her to know you called, you leave a **playing card** with your name written on the back in ink. The fashion began in the coffeehouses of the 1660s, where Tarock players scribbled messages on spare cards. By the 1680s it had reached the Greifenburg, and now every great house's **Portier** receives them on a silver tray, the **Kartenschale**, and carries them upstairs.

Because a playing card has a face as well as a back, **the choice of card is a message**. People choose carefully. Recipients read carefully. Misunderstandings are a recognized genre of comedy. This is the **Kartensprache**, the language of cards.

### Which deck
- **Valdauer Tarock suit-cards** (French suits, *Herz / Karo / Pik / Kreuz*) are standard in Wiedenbrunn. They are cheap, and a pack lasts a season of calls.
- **The Rocalban baralla** (*Copes / Oros / Espases / Bastos*) is used by Rocalbans, by Wiedenbrunners who want to seem Rocalban, and by those sending a sharper message. The baralla cards are the marks' own images, and slightly more serious (→ R04).
- **Never a Tarock Trump.** Leaving a Trump image is presumption: you are claiming a gift you don't have, or insulting someone who does. It is also bad luck. The single exception is below.

### The suits
| Valdauer | Rocalban | Means |
|---|---|---|
| **Herz** (hearts) | **Copes** (cups) | friendship, affection, a social call; "thinking of you" |
| **Karo** (diamonds) | **Oros** (coins) | business, a debt reminder, a petition |
| **Pik** (spades) | **Espases** (swords) | formality, offense, a challenge; "we must speak" |
| **Kreuz** (clubs) | **Bastos** (batons) | family matters, condolence, kin business |

### The values
- **Low pips** (2–5) mean a small matter or a short visit.
- **High pips** (6–10, or 6–9 in the baralla) mean a matter of some weight.
- **Face cards** mean a matter of consequence. The **King of Pik / Rei d'Espases** left at a gentleman's door is understood as a **challenge to a duel** (→ I20). A **Queen** (Valdauer deck only; the baralla has none) is left only by women to women and means a matter between ladies, and is very much not to be discussed with husbands.
- **The Ace** means "**only you.**" An Ace of Herz or Copes left for a lady is a declaration. An Ace of Karo means "the whole debt, now." An Ace of Kreuz means "a death in the family." An Ace of Pik means a final insult.

### The Sküs
The one Trump permitted. Leaving the **Sküs**, the Fool, the unnumbered card that beats everything, means **"I was never here."** It records a visit that officially didn't happen. Lovers use it. Spies use it. Creditors with a sense of humor use it. A Portier who receives a Sküs tells no one, takes it upstairs face down, and expects a better tip than usual.

### Folded corners
- **Top left folded**: "I came in person," as opposed to sending a servant with the card.
- **Top right folded**: "for the lady only," not for her husband.
- **Bottom left folded**: condolence.
- **Bottom right folded**: congratulations, on a birth, a marriage or a promotion.
- **A corner torn away**: farewell before a journey. Written beside it, **"z.A."** (*zum Abschied*, in parting).
- **The whole card torn across**: the friendship is over.

A card can carry several messages at once. An Ace of Herz, top right folded, means *only you, and not your husband.* A Five of Karo, bottom left folded, means *condolences, and also the money*. People have been dueled over less.

## 2. Calling hours

- **Mornings**: only intimates call. They are received at the **Toilette** (→ I07), often in Negligé, often in the bedroom or dressing room. Being received at a lady's toilette is a mark of intimacy and a matter of gossip.
- **Noon to two**: nobody calls. People are dining, or at Mass, or at the Kronrat.
- **Three to five**, after the Mittagstafel and before the Jause, are the **Besuchsstunden**, calling hours. This is when the ordinary social calls are made, the Portier is busiest and carriages line the Herrengasse.
- **The Jause** at five: an invitation to Jause (coffee or chocolate and cakes) is warmer than a call.
- **Evenings** are not for calling. Evenings are for the theatre, cards, balls and invitations. An unannounced evening caller is family, a lover, or bringing bad news.

### The Jour
Every lady of standing has her **Jour**: one fixed afternoon a week when she is "at home" to anyone who has been presented to her, without invitation. *"Die Frau Gräfin hat Venustag Jour."* Her Jour is her salon, her market and her intelligence service. Some Jours are crowded and brilliant, and some are a footman and a plate of biscuits. Not attending a friend's Jour for three weeks running is noticed. Attending an enemy's is a declaration.

The Ilsenau women's Jours on the Ammengasse are among the most important in Wiedenbrunn, because whoever attends may also hear something about the court's beds (→ R12).

## 3. The visit itself

- **The Portier** at the gate takes your card on his tray and sends it up. You wait in the **Vorsaal** (antechamber). He returns with one of three answers:
  - **"Die Herrschaft lässt bitten"**: "the family asks you up." You're received.
  - **"Die Herrschaft ist nicht zu Hause"**: "not at home." Everybody knows this means *not to you, today*, and nobody minds. It is the polite refusal.
  - **"Die Herrschaft bedauert"**: "the family regrets." It means *not to you, ever*. It is not polite, and it is meant.
- **How far down the rooms you're taken** tells you exactly how you stand (→ I03). The **Audienzzimmer** is for formal calls, the **Salon** for friends, the **Kabinett** at the end of the row for intimates.
- **How long**: a formal call lasts **a quarter hour**, the **Viertelstunde**. The caller rises when the quarter strikes, or at the hostess's signal: she stands, or picks up her fan. A caller who stays half an hour is a close friend, and one who stays an hour is a lover or a bore. A caller who leaves in five minutes has delivered an insult or a message.
- **Who calls on whom**: the inferior calls first; the newly arrived calls on the established; the younger calls on the older; the man calls on the woman. In the Spindelhäuser, women outrank their husbands in calling order, and Wiedenbrunners still can't get it right (→ R05).
- **The Antrittsbesuch** (inaugural call): anyone arriving at court or in a new office must call, within three days, on everyone above them who matters. A list is circulated by the Portiers' gossip. Missing a name is an insult that will be remembered for a generation.
- **Returning a call**: within a week, card for card. A card not returned is a cooling.

## 4. What to bring

- **Flowers** always **in odd numbers**: three, five, seven roses. **Even numbers are for graves** (→ I18). A dozen roses given to the living is a death-wish, and a bouquet of twelve has ended an engagement.
  - **Carnations** are for friendship (and, worn rather than given, for women who love women, → R09).
  - **Lilies** are for the Bride and for virgins, and are rarely given.
  - **Violets** are for secret love.
  - **Rosemary** is for remembrance, and also means "my parents are home" in the Fensterl code (→ R08).
  - **Yellow roses** are a recent joke about the Lagoon-Pox (→ I66), and are now given only to enemies.
- **Sweets** from the **Zuckerbäcker** (confectioner): candied violets, marzipan, Brautkrapfen in Fasching (→ X03).
- **Coffee** in a tin, or **chocolate** tablets in a box. Which you bring is a political statement (→ I05).
- **To a woman in childbed** (she receives only women, → I65): the **Kindbettschüssel**, a lidded porcelain soup-bowl, usually filled with a rich chicken broth. Families keep a cupboard of them.
- **To a house in mourning**: nothing but your card, Kreuz or Bastos, bottom left folded.

## 5. Paying the house: Türgeld and Bscheidessen

- **Türgeld** ("door-money"): a visitor tips the Portier on arrival, the Lakai who carries the card up, and the Kammerdiener or Zofe who shows them out. Not tipping is noticed: the next card goes up slower. Footmen fight over who stands at the door during calling hours, because Türgeld can double a footman's wage (→ I04).
- **Neujahrsgeld**: the New Year tip to every servant of every house one visits regularly.
- **The Bscheidessen** (from *Bescheid*, a portion): after a dinner or feast, the hosts send each guest home with a covered basket of the best leftovers for their household, so that the guests' servants eat well too. A generous Bscheidessen is remembered. A mean one becomes a story the guest's cook tells for years.
- **Hosts' gifts to departing guests** of rank: a fan, a snuffbox, a small silver thing. The Ilsenau give a tiny silver crescent, and half the court owns one.

## 6. Visits that are not visits

- **The Toilette visit** (→ I07): received in Negligé while the lady is dressed. Intimate, public and gossiped about.
- **The Kammerbesuch** ("chamber-call"): a visit in the bedroom itself, at night or in the early morning. Only family, lovers, physicians and very bold friends make one. A Kammerbesuch is written in no calling-book. The Portier is tipped to forget it, and the card left is a Sküs.
- **The Sküs-Besuch**: any visit that officially didn't happen.
- **The night stair**: in many palais a private stair leads to the lady's or lord's apartment. The key, the **Nachtschlüssel**, is given or lent as a token (→ I03). *"Sie hat ihm den Schlüssel gegeben"* means everything.
- **Visiting a pledge-school or a Rosengrund house** is "visiting a friend in the Rosengrund." A card is left there too. The Rosengrund houses keep their visitors' cards in locked boxes, and one sold box can bring down a family (→ I23).

## 7. Card visits abroad

- **Rocalba**: Rocalbans call on each other with less ceremony and more food. They leave baralla cards, never Valdauer ones, and expect an **abraçada** at the door (→ I01).
- **Almagra**: calls are an elaborate ritual of the **estrado**, the ladies' raised platform with cushions, where women sit and receive. Men may not sit on the estrado, and a man invited to is being honored or seduced (→ I32).
- **Vellora**: one doesn't call; one meets at the **ridotto** (gaming room) or the courtesan's **conversazione** (→ I35).
- **The Sworn Valleys**: one knocks, shakes hands, sits at the kitchen table, and is offered cheese.
- **Kesrân** (the embassy): a visitor is offered coffee, sherbet and rosewater, and must accept the coffee or insult the house. The embassy's Portier speaks four languages and reports to the ambassador on every card (→ I34).
- **Asagiri** (the embassy): visitors leave **folded paper** instead of cards, and the embassy has adopted Valdauer playing cards with alarming thoroughness. Its staff leave cards whose meanings they have studied from the Kartensprache handbooks and get subtly, hilariously wrong (→ I37).

## HOOKS

1. **The Ace on the tray.** On the morning after the protagonist's first public appearance, the Portier brings up an Ace of Copes, top right folded, *only you, not your husband*, with a name on it that nobody in the household expected. The protagonist has no husband, which makes the fold either a joke or a code.
2. **The Rei d'Espases.** A Starkenberg cousin leaves a Rei d'Espases with no fold for a man of the household. It is either a challenge or a clumsy gesture by a young officer who doesn't know the Kartensprache.
3. **Not at home.** For three weeks the Palais Ilsenau has been "not at home" to a particular lady. She has begun leaving cards with corners torn, *farewell*, and the household is unsure whether she is leaving Wiedenbrunn or threatening something.
4. **The Sküs left in the Stille Haus.** A Sküs card is found in the boathouse at the Spiegelsee with no name on the back. Someone visited the far shore who was never there.
5. **The Jour.** The first Jour after the waking, the Ilsenau salon is fuller than it has been in years. Every lady has come to see whether the rumor is true, and the Hausmutter must decide whether he comes down.
6. **The Bscheidessen.** After a dinner at an allied house, the protagonist's covered basket comes home with something hidden under the pâté: a note, a key, and a Kammerbesuch hour.
