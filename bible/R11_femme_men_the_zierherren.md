# R11 · Femme Men: the Zierherren

*Men who are slight, smooth, soft-voiced, beautiful, or who dress and carry themselves as the court's ornaments: where they come from, where they are wanted, where they are scorned, what they wear, and the very old joke about what a slight man may be hiding.*

## 1. Names

| Register | Word | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Court, polite | **Zierherr** ("ornament-gentleman") | The general word. Neutral to warm at court, faintly mocking in town. |
| Court, affectionate | **Fräuleinherr** ("miss-gentleman") | Said by ladies, with a fan. |
| Ilsenau | **Mondbräutigam** ("Moon-bridegroom") | A femme man married into the house (→ R12). |
| Rocalban | **donzell** | Old word for a young noble page, now for a pretty man. The ending sounds like *donzella*, maiden, which is the point. |
| Almagran | **lindo** ("pretty one") | From the stage-type of the vain fop. Contemptuous. |
| Kesrâni | **zenne** | Strictly, a dancer in women's dress; loosely, any femme man. |
| Velloran | **damerin** | A ladies' man in the other sense: a man who lives among ladies. |
| Asagiri | **hanaotoko** ("flower-man") | The envoy's people's word, admired in salons. |
| Bürger, sneering | **Puderherr** ("powder-gentleman") | |
| Viennese, sly | **halbseiden** ("half-silk") | Means shady, effeminate, not quite respectable. A *Halbseidener*. |
| Vulgar | **Schwanerl** (little swan); **Weibling** (womanling) | For fights. |

Plain usage: a man who *is* femme by body and a man who *dresses* femme by fashion are both called Zierherren. Nobody much distinguishes them until it matters.

---

## 2. Where they come from

- **Blood.** High fineness in **Mercury** and **Moon** makes bodies light, smooth and changeable (→ R03). Mercury- and Moon-blooded families throw sons who are slight, nearly beardless, high- or soft-voiced, with fine hands and fine skin. Casa Mirabent and the silver houses have more Zierherren born to them than anyone. The Ilsenau sons, rare as they are, are proverbially pretty (→ R12).
- **Fashion.** Since the 1680s it has been the mode among young courtiers to wear their own long hair (**Eigenhaar**) instead of a wig, to shave close and powder, to wear lace and soft colors, and to cultivate the languor of the Almagran **galán** and the elegance of the Velloran **damerin**. Plenty of perfectly ordinary young men are Zierherren from ten in the morning until supper.
- **Trade.** Singers (the **Silberstimmen** of the Hofoper, → I68), dancers, actors in **Rockrollen** (skirt-roles), courtesans of the Rosengrund's Herrenhöfe, and body-pledges in the catalogues, where a Zierherr is priced like a woman, and higher if well-endowed (→ R07).
- **Temperament.** And then there are men who are simply this way, and would be in any coat.

Zierherren desire in every direction. Being femme says nothing reliable about whom a man wants, which surprises the people who assumed.

---

## 3. Where they are wanted and where scorned

- **At court: fashionable.** Great ladies keep Zierherren as dance partners, card partners (the **Kartenherr**, who partners a lady at Ombre and holds her cards when she leaves the table), confidants, toilette companions (→ I07) and lovers. A Zierherr at a lady's elbow is less suspicious to her husband than a captain of hussars, which is the second-best thing about him.
- **In the Spindelhäuser: preferred.** Matriarchal houses marry Zierherren in as husbands, *"weil ein Zierherr nicht mitregiert"*, "because a Zierherr doesn't try to co-rule." Ilsenau's in-married husbands, the **Mondbräutigame**, are traditionally femme. Wiedenbrunn calls this the **Ilsenauer Geschmack**, the Ilsenau taste.
- **Among the Bürger: mocked to the face, watched in private.** A Bürger son with lace on his cuffs is a family's shame and its daughters' favorite cousin.
- **In the regiments: scorned officially.** A Starkenberg officer won't serve beside a Zierherr. But Mirabent's couriers, who ride faster than anyone, are famously femme, and nobody calls a Mirabent rider a lady to his face twice (→ I41).
- **In the villages: joked about.** At Fasching, young men in women's dress are a carnival staple (→ I70). A man who stays that way after Ash Day is talked about.
- **In the Church: "Heir-touched."** Considered gentle, lucky, and especially pleasing to the Bride. The **Confraria de l'Hereu** makes femme men its clergy, the *fadrins de l'Hereu* (→ I58).

## 4. Dress and fashion

*(Sumptuary rules in I06; cosmetics and patches in I07.)*

- **Eigenhaar**: their own hair, long and loose or tied with a ribbon, unpowdered or lightly powdered. The **Mondlocke** is a single lock left loose at the temple with a silver ribbon, an Ilsenau fashion now general.
- **Close shaving** and **powder**; **patches** (a crescent at the cheekbone is the Ilsenau mode); **rouge**, discreet or not.
- **Lace**: Velloran and Rocalban, at throat and cuffs, as wide as the Kleiderordnung allows a man's rank, and wider.
- **Soft colors**: pale blue, pearl, rose, lilac, the colors the Bürger call *Damenfarben*, ladies' colors.
- **Silk stockings** on good calves; low shoes with **red heels** for those with Kammerzutritt.
- **At home**: the **Schlafrock**, a silk dressing-gown of Kesrâni cut, worn over shirt and breeches all morning. A Zierherr receiving in his Schlafrock is the image of the type.
- **The Turmhaube scandal of 1697**: a group of young Zierherren appeared at a Redoute in the tall lace **Turmhaube** headdress of ladies (→ I06). The Kleidervisitatoren fined them, the ladies applauded, the fines were paid by the ladies, and the fashion lasted a month.
- **Earrings**: Ilsenau's Mondbräutigame wear a single silver crescent earring. The rest of the court copied it for a season and then stopped, and only the Mondbräutigame kept it.

**The law**: the **Kleiderordnung** forbids men to wear "women's garments" in public outside Fasching (→ I06). It is enforced against commoners and actors, and was enforced against nobles only during the **Chastity Commission** (→ I77). A Zierherr in lace and rouge is wearing men's clothes. A Zierherr in a skirt is breaking the law, and that line is policed exactly as far as anybody wants it to be.

## 5. Desire

- **Zierherren and women**: ladies like them very much. A Zierherr is assumed to be gentle, attentive, unthreatening, discreet and a good dancer. These assumptions are frequently true, and occasionally a disastrous underestimate.
- **Zierherren and men**: some prefer men, and are sought by them. The Rosengrund's **Herrenhöfe** price Zierherren highest of their Lustherren (→ I23).
- **Zierherren and futas**: a famous fashion of the 1680s, celebrated in a hundred bad poems (*"the Moon and Mercury reversed"*).
- **In the bound trade**: a Zierherr body-pledge is a luxury. A Zierherr with a soldier's endowment is a rarity, and the catalogues say so in a code everybody reads: *"von verborgenen Vorzügen,"* of hidden excellences.

## 6. The Wolf im Schlafrock

The oldest joke in Wiedenbrunn about Zierherren: **"Ein Wolf im Schlafrock."** A wolf in a dressing-gown.

It means a slight, pretty, femme man who turns out, when the Schlafrock comes off, to be built like a grenadier. The joke goes back to at least **1687**. At the Bad Sulzau summer court that year, a young Zierherr of the Holdegg household, **Ignaz von Rosenegg**, had his bath-sheet whipped away by a gust in the Mondbad brine pool. He was revealed before forty ladies of the court, and the **Freifrau von Aichberg** is supposed to have said, loudly and to general agreement, *"Schau, ein Wolf im Schlafrock!"* Rosenegg dined out on it for a decade, married a Spindelhaus heiress the next year, and is now a respectable Hofrat with seven daughters.

Since then:
- *"Wolf im Schlafrock"* is said with delight of any Zierherr who turns out to be more than expected in any respect: in bed, in a duel, in a lawsuit.
- The **Kammerbüchl** chapbooks of the Rosengrund have a whole genre of them, the *Wolfsgeschichten* (→ I08).
- At the **Schützenfest** of 1692 one of the satirical painted targets showed a wolf in a silk dressing-gown taking aim at a row of ladies' hearts (→ I10). It was shot to pieces and is still talked about.
- Ladies have been heard to ask a Zierherr's friends, behind fans, *"Ist er ein Wolf?"*

**The protagonist** fits the type so exactly that the joke is waiting for him before he has said a word: slight, smooth, long-haired, flushing, a Zierherr by every sign. Whoever finds out first will have the best story at court for a year.

## 7. Abroad

- **Almagra**: the **lindo** is a stage clown, a vain fop who is mocked and beaten in comedies. Real Almagran grandees dress so elaborately that Valdauers can't tell the difference (→ I32).
- **Kesrân**: the **zenne**, male dancers in women's dress, are a celebrated profession with their own coffeehouses, poets and patrons, and the Kesrâni embassy's zenne are invited to every Wiedenbrunn salon that can afford the scandal (→ I34).
- **Vellora**: men dress as women at carnival and courtesans dress as boys all year. The Night Wardens prosecute what happens next, when it's men (→ I35).
- **Ormánd**: contempt, at least in public. Ormándi hussars wear more braid, lace and perfume than any Zierherr, and do not see the irony (→ I33).
- **The Sworn Valleys**: the Clear Church preaches against "men in women's softness," and the Valleys' mercenaries in Wiedenbrunn learn to keep their opinions behind their pikes (→ I36).
- **Asagiri**: their theatre has adult male actors who play women so perfectly that Asagiri ladies copy *them*, and the envoy finds Valdauer anxiety about Zierherren baffling (→ I37).

---

## 8. Famous Zierherren

- **Ignaz von Rosenegg**, the original Wolf im Schlafrock (§6).
- **Der schöne Ignaz** (no relation): the 1660s favorite of Queen Margarethe Ludovika's household, a Mirabent page who was never proven to be anything but her chess partner. The Chastity Commission, which she founded thirty years later, never examined him.
- **Giuseppe "il Perlino" Morosin**, Velloran Silberstimme, one of the two rival singers whose admirers rioted in the **War of the Two Silvers** in 1694 (→ I68).
- **En Galceran Mirabent**, the Mercury-fine Rocalban nobleman who, by the record, bore two children in 1589 and 1591 (→ R14).
- **The Mondbräutigame of Ilsenau**, a portrait gallery of very pretty men who never had a vote.

## HOOKS

1. **"Ist er ein Wolf?"** At the protagonist's first public appearance, a lady's fan-whispered question goes round the room. By the end of the evening there is a wager in the betting book at Café Sküs, and three ladies who intend to settle it personally.
2. **The other Mondbräutigam.** An in-married Ilsenau husband, a Zierherr of thirty-five who has been the house's pretty ornament for fifteen years, offers to teach the protagonist how a femme man survives in the house. His lessons are useful, and his reasons are not simple.
3. **The Kartenherr.** A great lady of the Schokoladenpartei asks the protagonist to be her Kartenherr at Ombre for the season: to sit at her side, hold her cards, read her signals and be seen with her four nights a week. It is the most public possible start at court, and it ties him to her party.
4. **The Kleidervisitator.** A Zierherr friend is fined on the Graben for a skirt-cut coat. He was wearing it to make a point, and he wants a member of a Seven Chairs house to walk beside him on the Graben in the same coat next Sonntag.
5. **The catalogue's code.** A pledge in the Quartalsschau catalogue is listed as a Zierherr, Rocalban, *"von verborgenen Vorzügen,"* Schulbrief. He has written a letter to the house, smuggled out, saying he served in the Stille Haus during the long sleep and was sold when his term "ended early."
6. **The zenne's dance.** The Kesrâni embassy's lead zenne dances at an Ilsenau salon, and afterwards asks, through the interpreter, to see "the young Prinz who dances like us." The ambassador's interest in what is said at that meeting is not artistic.
