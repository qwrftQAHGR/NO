# R06 · Kinship & Nearness (incest)

*How the Double Crown thinks about love and sex between kin: what the Church says, what the blood does, what it costs, how it is played as a game at court, how it lives in farmhouses and garrets, and why something you can never undo still doesn't end your respectability.*

**See also:** R03 The Work (karat, brittleness, annealing) · R04 The Cards (falls) · I55 The Tariff of Nearness · I56 The Left Hand · I75 The Glass Prince & the Annealing Edict · I59 The Casa Pairal & the Fadrins · I60 The Straw Court · I70 The Ball Season & the Mirror Dance · I17 Weddings

## 1. Words

Valdauers don't say "incest" in company. The Latin word, *incestus*, belongs to sermons and lawsuits. The everyday word is **Nähe**, nearness.

- **fern** (far): second cousins and beyond. Nobody cares.
- **nah** (near): first cousins; uncle and niece; aunt and nephew. These marriages need a church dispensation, which is a fee and a sermon. They are common in the great houses and raise eyebrows elsewhere.
- **nächst** (nearest): siblings and half-siblings, parent and child, grandparent and grandchild. Marriage is possible only by an expensive **Nahbrief** under strict conditions (→ I55). Love affairs are common, and everyone pretends otherwise.

Related terms:
- **Nahliebe**: love between near or nearest kin.
- **Nahpaar**: a couple who are kin.
- **Nahkind**: a child of nearest kin.
- **"eine nahe Verbindung"**: the polite phrase.
- **"häusliche Neigung"** ("domestic inclination"): the very polite phrase.
- **"im Haus bleiben"** ("staying in the house"): what families say they are doing when they are doing it.

In Rocalban Catalan: **parents propers** (near kin), **amor de casa** (house-love, → I59), **fills de casa** (house-children).

## 2. What the Church says

The Church of the Wedding (→ I54) has an awkward problem at its very center. **The Bridegroom and the Bride are brother and sister.** They are the Sun and the Moon, born together from the Undivided, and their marriage made the world. The faith's most sacred image is a sibling wedding. Every church altarpiece shows it, and every noble who has ever wanted his sister has pointed at it.

The Church's answer, formulated by the Abbess **Brunissenda of Montalba** in the eleventh century and repeated in every sermon on the subject since:

> **"Was oben einmal geschah, ist unten Hoffart."**
> *What happened once above is pride below.*

The divine wedding happened once, for all, and to imitate it is to make oneself a god, which is the sin of **Hoffart**, pride. Nearness is therefore not a sin of the flesh. Pleasure is no sin in this faith (→ R08). It is a sin of **presumption**. That has three consequences, all of them very convenient for the Church:

1. **Near kin may marry by paying.** The fee for a dispensation is a penance for presumption, scaled to the degree of presumption. This is **the Tariff of Nearness** (*der Nähetarif*), a published price list (→ I55). First cousins are cheap. Siblings are ruinous and conditional. Parents and children are not sold as marriage at all.
2. **Nearness that doesn't presume to make heirs is a small sin.** Lovemaking between kin that cannot make a child (two sisters, two brothers, hands and mouths, the back way) is **Spiel**, play: a venial sin, confessed with a smile and penanced with a candle. Lovemaking that can make a child is **Ernst**, earnest: presumption, needing either a Nahbrief or a long penance (see §5).
3. **The Church doesn't punish; it records and it charges.** There is no criminal law against nearness in Valdau or Rocalba. The Church's courts can annul an unblessed near marriage, refuse a sacrament, or record a family's nearness in the **Nahvermerk** (§5). Beyond that it collects the fees.

**The Clear Church** (→ I74) rejects all of this. It holds that the Bridegroom and Bride are an allegory of the soul and God, that nearness is abomination, and that selling dispensations is simony. That argument is a large part of why the Brides' War was fought.

## 3. What the blood does (why it tempts)

There are four reasons, in order of how often they are spoken aloud.

1. **Karat** (→ R03). *"Gleiches löst sich in Gleichem,"* like dissolves in like. The child of two unrelated parents fine in the same metal assays at their average plus one. The child of close kin assays at their average plus two or three. A great house that marries its daughters to their cousins for two generations can climb from 17 to 21 karat: from strong to formidable.
2. **The Cards** (→ R04). A sibling who is also a lover scores 6 in the reckoning of a fall, nearly a certainty. A Trump-house that wants its card to stay home keeps the heir in the bearer's bed. *This* is the reason the great houses never speak aloud, and the one everyone knows.
3. **Secrecy.** A house that marries inside keeps its money, its secrets, its servants' loyalties and its alchemical recipes inside. Whatever is said in the bedroom stays in the family.
4. **The Moon's way.** In the silver houses, kin-sense (the Gespür) makes the body of a near relative *loud*. An Ilsenau woman feels her sister's arousal from across a room. Moon-blooded families say they don't choose nearness so much as fail to avoid it.

And the fifth reason, which nobody counts and everybody feels: **it is forbidden, and the forbidden is delicious.**

## 4. What it costs (why it is not the main strategy nowadays)

- **Brittleness** (→ R03 §6). Blood folded too close too often breaks: Greifenkinn jaws, bleeders, fragile bones, stillbirths, short lives, sterility, and the temper-madnesses (Glanzwahn, Mondsucht, Zitterblut, Liebeswahn, Rostwut, Starre, Bleischlaf). Every great house has a name to say when this comes up. The royal house's is **the Glass Prince**, Archduke Leopold Balthasar (1608–1631). He was the son of a brother-sister marriage whose parents had been uncle and niece. He was brilliant and beloved, and his bones broke when he sneezed. He died at twenty-three when his horse shied (→ I75).
- **The Annealing Edict of 1633** (→ I75), the Church's response. A nearest-kin marriage by Nahbrief requires: proof that the couple's own parents were *not* near kin (the **Drittel rule**: after one close generation, two generations outward); a conception timed by the Stundenamt to a Jupiter hour; and the **Anlasskur**, the annealing course of tinctures, through the pregnancy (300–600 Greif). Cheating on any of these is common and dangerous.
- **Money.** The Tariff, the Anlasskur, the Stundenamt, and the bribes to make the paperwork match.
- **The Nahvermerk** (§5).
- **Mockery.** Abroad, Valdau is famous for its chins. In the Gstanzl at home, the great houses are famous for their bedrooms (→ I11).
- **Better options.** Same-metal marriage across houses, pit-blood concubines and the Anlasskur all keep most of the karat without the risk. Houses that never fold, like **Holdegg**, which cannot (→ I42), and the healthy Briefadel are doing very well. Since 1633 the great houses have learned to fold **rarely, deliberately, expensively and on schedule.** One generation in three is the respectable pace. Kalteneck, which never stopped, is the cautionary tale everyone visits (→ I44).

**What history actually looks like.** Of the Seven Chairs:
- **Greifenstein** alternates cousin marriages with outside ones (→ I39).
- **Ilsenau** keeps its women close in Spiel and breeds outward through in-married husbands (→ R12).
- **Mirabent** marries cousins who look like their siblings (→ I41).
- **Holdegg** marries out by necessity.
- **Starkenberg** marries cousins and anneals with pit-blood concubines (→ I40).
- **Ehrenreith** marries uncles to nieces by tradition (→ I43).
- **Kalteneck** has done the nearest marriages for three hundred years and is dying of it slowly, at a hundred years of age apiece (→ I44).

There are winners and losers on both sides of the line.

## 5. The Nahvermerk: what you can't get out of

When a nearest-kin pair **produces a child**, or **is issued a Nahbrief**, or **is entered together in the Left Hand register** (→ I56), the **Hofprobieramt** writes a small **N.** with the date beside the family's entry in the Feinbuch. That is the **Nahvermerk**, the nearness-annotation. Rocalban notaries keep the same mark as **la nota**.

It is never erased. No fee, royal grace or later marriage removes it. *"Einmal N., immer N."*

What the N. does:
- **It does not end respectability.** An N.-family is still received, still hoffähig, still invited to the Cercle and courted for alliances. Two of the Seven Chairs have an N. in the current generation, and everyone knows which two.
- **It closes certain doors.** An N.-person cannot stand **godparent** (*Gevatter*, *Gote*) to a child outside their own family. They cannot be elected **abbess, prior or Zeuge-elder**, or sit in the Bride's chapter at Montalba. They cannot serve as **Kämmerer** to an unmarried royal. *Nahkinder*, the children themselves, are assayed with extra care: a splitter on an N.-child's streak is assumed to be the parents' fault, even when it isn't.
- **It changes the marriage market.** An N.-family's daughters are a prize to houses hunting karat and a risk to houses afraid of brittleness. Matchmakers price the N. like a dowry.
- **It changes how people look at you.** This is the real effect. An N.-person enters a room and the room knows. Some look away. Some look *longer*. N.-women are said, in a certain kind of conversation, to be the best lovers at court. N.-men are said to be dangerous, because a man who has done *that* has nothing left to be careful about. The mark is a stain and an invitation. You can't get out of it, and some people would never want to.

## 6. The Nähespiel: the game of nearness at court

Because Spiel is a venial sin and Ernst marks you forever, high society has built a whole art on the line between them. The **Nähespiel** (game of nearness) is a public, decorous, intensely watched flirtation between kin. Everyone reads it and nobody speaks of it. Its instruments:

- **The kin-kiss ladder.**
  - The **Stirnkuss**, a kiss on the forehead, is innocent.
  - The **Wangenkuss**, on the cheek, is affectionate.
  - The **Spiegelkuss**, the "mirror kiss" on the mouth between siblings or cousins, is the edge of the game. It is permitted in the family's own salon at Erbentag and Brauttag, scandalous anywhere else, and done anyway.
- **The Spiegeltanz**, the mirror-dance. A court dance performed by two kin facing each other, matching each other's steps like a reflection, never quite touching except at the turn. It is either the most elegant or the most indecent thing at a ball, depending on who dances it and how (→ I70).
- **Geschwisterschleifen**, sibling ribbons. A brother and sister, or two cousins, wear ribbons of the same color at a ball. It is a declaration that they will dance together, sit together, and leave together. Whether they then go home separately is everyone's question.
- **The shared Loge.** A brother and sister alone in an opera box with the curtain half drawn (→ I68).
- **Fans and gloves**: a fan painted with a sibling's initials; a glove carried that belongs to a cousin (→ I07).
- **Witty acknowledgment.** *"My brother and I are very close,"* said with a particular smile, is understood perfectly.

The thrill is in staying on the Spiel side of the line in front of everyone, and in everyone watching to see whether you will. A pair who play the Nähespiel brilliantly for years without an N. are admired. A pair who suddenly *stop* playing in public are assumed to have crossed into Ernst in private.

## 7. Below the great houses

### Peasants
- **Ofenbankliebe** ("stove-bench love"). In a Valdauer farmhouse the family sleeps near the great tiled stove in winter. Brothers and sisters, cousins and lodgers share the warm bench and the beds above it. What happens there is called **Stubenliebe** (room-love). It is common, unblessed, not illegal, and not a subject for daylight.
- **The single-heir farm.** In the Hallgau and the high valleys, land goes whole to one heir, the **Anerbe**. Unmarried brothers and sisters stay on for life as hands, the **Dienstgeschwister**, the "serving siblings." Near arrangements in such houses are a long, quiet, practical matter. The village knows, the Zeuge knows, and nobody finds it glamorous.
- **The Straw Court** (→ I60). The village's young men, the **Zeche**, may stage a mock trial of a pair caught at it: rough music under the window, a straw effigy of the couple on the roof, a fine in beer. It is humiliating and it is over in a night. Nobody has ever died of one.
- **No blessing, no heir.** Children of unblessed nearest-kin unions are **Ofenkinder** ("stove-children"). They inherit from their mother's side only and are teased in the village school. Their mothers' families raise them like any others.

### Rocalba
- In the **casa pairal**, the stem-household, the heir (**hereu** or **pubilla**) inherits the house and the unmarried younger siblings, the **fadrins**, live in it for life. *Amor de casa*, house-love, between siblings and cousins under one roof is old, unspoken and tolerated (→ I59). Rocalbans regard it as a household matter, a matter of **seny** (good sense), and are bewildered by the Valdauer court's theatrical anxiety about it.

### The frontier
- The Grenzer live in **Hauskommunionen**, extended-family households of thirty or forty cousins under one elder (→ I28). In them, cousin love is ordinary, and **sibling love is a horror**. The Grenzer are stricter about it than anyone in the realm, because forty cousins in one house need a hard line somewhere.

### The town
- **The Bürger** are the prudes. Respectable Wiedenbrunn families define themselves against decadent nobles and coarse peasants, and the merest whisper of nearness is social ruin for a Bürger daughter. They marry first cousins constantly anyway, to keep the business together: **"Vetternwirtschaft"**, cousin-economy, which is also the realm's word for nepotism. They do not call that nearness.
- **The poor.** In Wiedenbrunn's outer quarters, people rent beds by the shift as **Bettgeher**, and families of eight share two rooms. Nearness happens there because there is nowhere else for it not to happen. It is spoken of as **Unglück**, bad luck, not sin.

### The bound
- Valdau's law doesn't recognize kinship among born slaves (**Erbleute**) for any purpose (→ R07). Masters may pair them as they like, and some do. The most expensive item in the Seelenmakler's catalogue is a matched pair of grown twins, sold together and trained to perform together.
- Pledged people (**Pfandleute**) keep their family rights, and a Pfandamt clerk will refuse a contract that pairs a pledge with their own kin. That is the law. Then there is what happens.

## 8. Foreign views

| Sovereignty | View (→ files I32–I37) |
|---|---|
| **Almagra** | **Sangre cerrada**, closed blood: nobles are obliged to fold, and the Glass King is the result. Among commoners nearness is a horror policed by the Santo Crisol. Almagrans see Valdau as having lost its nerve. |
| **Ormánd** | Horror. Ormándi gentry marry wide and mock Valdauer chins. Their substitute for kin-closeness is the **sworn sibling** (*fogadott testvér*), and sworn siblings sometimes become lovers, which Ormándi insist is entirely different. |
| **Kesrân** | Cousin marriage is ordinary and sibling marriage an abomination; the Wedding's sibling gods are the first thing Kesrâni preachers mock. The Padishah's own sons are kept apart from each other in the Garden of Princes for reasons that have nothing to do with love. |
| **Vellora** | Legalistic and mercantile. In **fraterna** households one brother marries and the others share the house, and what the others share is a matter for the family's notary. Scandals are hushed with money. |
| **The Sworn Valleys** | Strict taboo under the Clear Church, with jokes about remote valleys where "everyone is their own cousin." |
| **Asagiri** | Half-siblings by *different mothers* ("different hearths") may marry without shame. Children of the same mother never may. By Asagiri reckoning, a man's children by different women are not near kin at all, a principle Valdauers find barbaric or extremely convenient, depending on who is asking. |

## 9. Sayings, jokes and insults

- **"Was kostet die Schwester?"** "What does the sister cost?" From the Tariff. Said of anything outrageously expensive.
- **"Einmal N., immer N."**: once noted, always noted.
- **"Vetternwirtschaft"**: nepotism; literally cousin-economy.
- **"Die Familie zusammenhalten"**: "keeping the family together." Euphemism.
- **"Er hat ein Kinn wie sein Großvater, und der hatte es von seiner Mutter, die auch seine Tante war."** *"He has a chin like his grandfather's, who got it from his mother, who was also his aunt."* A standard joke about the Greifenstein, told only in coffeehouses with thick walls.
- **"Spiegelpaar"**: a mirror-couple; a pair who look alike and are suspected of being kin.
- **"Stubenhocker"**: a homebody; also, in village slang, someone who loves within the family.
- **"Ofenkind"**: a child of the stove-bench.
- **"Die Braut hat auch nicht gefragt."** *"The Bride didn't ask either."* A defiant reply quoting the divine sibling wedding. It is blasphemous, and very popular after the third glass.

## 10. Who benefits, who pays, who is changing it

- **Benefit**: the great houses (karat, falls, secrecy); the Church (fees); **Montalba and Rocalba** (the Nahbrief money funds Rocalban autonomy, → R01, I55); the Fall-Reckoners (complexity is their trade); the matchmakers.
- **Pay**: brittle children; the families who fold without paying and live with Ofenkinder; women married inward against their wishes; the slaves paired by owners.
- **Changing it**: the Clear Church (abolition); the Seventh Faculty's physicians, who publish the **Dean's Tables** of stillbirths and splitters by degree of kinship and are widely read and widely ignored (→ I52); Holdegg's "open line" ideology; and the 1692–1695 **Chastity Commission**, which tried to register every N.-family in Wiedenbrunn in a public **Nahregister** and failed spectacularly (→ I77).

## HOOKS

1. **The Spiegeltanz request.** At the first ball of the season a sister or cousin of the house asks to dance the Spiegeltanz. Saying yes in front of the whole court is a statement; saying no is another.
2. **The N. that isn't there.** A rival discovers that a great house's last nearest-kin child was born without the Nahvermerk being entered. Somebody at the Hofprobieramt was paid. The rival wants the entry made, or wants something in exchange for silence.
3. **Ofenkinder at the gate.** Two grown siblings arrive from a Weißach farm, both with the house's silver-pale eyes, carrying a letter from their dead mother naming an Ilsenau son of the 1660s as their father. They are proud, poor and frank about each other.
4. **A Drittel problem.** A great house wants to arrange a sibling Nahbrief for its heirs, and its reckoners point out that the heirs' own parents were cousins. Under the Annealing Edict that should disqualify them, unless the Stundenamt and the Hofprobieramt can be persuaded to see the grandparents' marriage differently.
5. **The Chastity Commission's list.** A copy of the abandoned 1693 Nahregister, believed burned, surfaces in a Rosengrund print-shop. It names who was suspected of what four years ago. Several people on it have changed their minds, their partners or their positions since.
6. **Asagiri arithmetic.** The Asagiri envoy, asked over dinner about his country's kinship rules, explains "different hearths" with innocent precision. Half the table goes quiet doing a sum.
