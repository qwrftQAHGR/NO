# I09 · Parlor & Table Games

*What the Double Crown plays when it isn't playing Tarock: the Rocalban bluffing game whose signals double as flirting, the Velloran banking game that ruins houses in a night, the two-hand trick game whose melds are called weddings, dice, boards, lotteries, and the forfeit games of the Salon, where the stakes are kisses, ribbons and an hour of service.*

**See also:** I67 Tarock & the Coffeehouses · I10 Sport & the Hunt · R04 The Cards · I08 The Chamber Lexicon · R07 Bondage · I20 The Rumorwache (gambling law)

---

## 1. The law, and the law in practice

The **Hasardpatent of 1673** forbids "games of hazard" (games of pure chance played for money) in public houses and on the street, on pain of fines and the pillory. Games of skill (Tarock, Ombre, chess) are allowed. Every Wiedenbrunner knows which games are which, the Rumorwache doesn't care, and **palaces are not public houses**. In a great house's card-room on a Fasching night, more money changes hands on Basseta than in the Llotja on a quarter-day.

Gambling debts are **Ehrenschulden**, debts of honor (→ I20). They are paid before tradesmen's bills, before the Banco and before the rent, because an unpaid gambling debt gets a man struck from the Hofbuch. Many a Valdauer has paid a gambling debt by pledging himself, his valet or his daughter's maid at the Pfandamt the next morning (→ R07, I61).

## 2. Card games (besides Tarock)

### Truc de Cort (Rocalban)
The Rocalban court's game, played with the baralla in partnerships of two against two. Each player holds only three cards. The game is won by **bluffing and by signals**: partners may tell each other what they hold, but only by **secret signs**, and the opponents may watch for them.

The classical signs:

| Sign | Card it signals |
|---|---|
| a wink | the As d'Espases |
| a bite of the lower lip | the As de Bastos |
| a raised eyebrow | a three |
| a puff of the cheeks | a seven |
| a pout, lips pushed out | a high card of Oros |
| touching the ear | "I have nothing" |
| a glance at the ceiling | "I have everything" |

Each partnership also invents private signs of its own. The bids are shouted: **"Truc!"** (I raise), **"Retruc!"** (I raise you), **"Val nou!"** (worth nine), **"Joc fora!"** (the whole game). It is a loud game.

**At court** Truc de Cort has become a **flirtation**. Mixed partnerships of a man and a woman spend an evening making faces at each other across the table: winking, biting their lips, pouting. Which signs are about the cards is the opponents' problem. *"Fer el Truc"*, "to play Truc" with someone, is Rocalban slang for an affair conducted in public by signals. A Valdauer who learns the signals is considered to have learned to flirt in Catalan.

### Basseta (Velloran)
A **banking game** from Vellora's gambling-rooms (the *ridotti*), and the most ruinous game in the realm. One player, the **banker** (*Bankhalter*), deals two cards at a time from the deck, one winning and one losing. The punters stake on which card will come up on which side, doubling and redoubling their stakes with folded card corners (*paroli*, *sept-et-le-va*) until fortunes are on a single card.
- It is fast and mathematical, and the banker always wins in the long run.
- It is banned by the Hasardpatent, banned again in 1691, and played every night of Fasching.
- The best Basseta bankers in Wiedenbrunn are **Velloran courtesans**, who run banks in their salons and take a commission on the house's winnings (→ I23, I35).
- People stake estates, carriages, jewels, horses and **pledges**: their own body-pledges, or a promissory note on their own body for a night. A pledge staked at Basseta is one of the most dramatic things that happens in the Double Crown, and the Kammerbüchl love it.

### Ombre (Almagran)
The **ladies' game**. A trick-taking game for three, with bidding, trumps and elegant rules, imported with the Ceremonial. Great ladies play it every evening with their Kartenherren, gentlemen who sit at their side, hold their cards when they leave the table and read their signals (→ R11). It is a game of skill and mostly of conversation. The vocabulary is Almagran: *matador*, *codille*, *spadille*, *basto*. Valdauer ladies pronounce it badly and play it brilliantly.

### Hochzeiten (the two-hand game)
A brisk trick-taking game for two with twenty or twenty-four cards, played in every coffeehouse, kitchen and carriage, and between lovers in bed on a rainy afternoon. Its essential feature is the **Hochzeit**, the wedding: holding the King and Queen of the same suit and announcing them for points. A plain-suit wedding is **zwanzig** (twenty). A wedding in trumps is **vierzig** (forty).
- *"Ich sag' eine Hochzeit an"* ("I announce a wedding") is said at the card-table and, with a look, elsewhere.
- In the Rocalban deck, which has no Queens, the game is played with the **Sota** standing in for the Queen. Rocalbans call the meld *el casament del criat*, "the servant's wedding," and think it very funny.
- **"Zwanzig und vierzig"** means everything working out perfectly at once.

## 3. Dice

- **Pasch** ("doublets"): the commonest tavern game. Roll three dice and score by the doublets. *"Ein Pasch!"* means a double, and also a stroke of luck.
- **Brautwurf** ("bride-throw"): two dice. A double six is **the Wedding** and takes the whole pot. A double one is **the Halde** (the slag-heap, → I54) and loses everything. Played at weddings, with the bride throwing first for luck.
- **Knöchel**: knucklebones, for children, soldiers and very drunk Hofräte.
- Dice are always **Hasard** under the 1673 Patent. The Rumorwache arrests dicers on the street when it needs to show the Kronrat an arrest.

## 4. Boards

- **Schach** (chess): played by the educated, the clergy, Kesrâni captives, Asagiri envoys and noblewomen. Kesrâni sets of ivory and ebony are prized. **A lady who plays chess well** is admired and a little feared. Chess between a lady and a gentleman is a classic courtship, played in the Kabinett, and the loser pays a forfeit (§6).
- **Mühle** (morris, mill): nine pieces each on a board of three squares. Peasants scratch the board on tavern tables.
- **Planetenmühle**: a court variant from the 1690s on a seven-pointed star board, each player with seven pieces named for the planets, which move according to their natures. The Moon moves only at night, which is a rule nobody can agree how to apply in a game played after dinner. Fashionable, complicated, and the occasion of many quarrels.
- **Puff** (backgammon): from Kesrân by way of Vellora. It is played in coffeehouses and, notoriously, in brothels, which is why **"ein Puff"** in Wiedenbrunn means **both a backgammon board and a bawdy-house**. *"Gehst du zum Puff?"* ("are you going to the Puff?") is the standard joke whenever someone proposes a game.
- **Das Hochzeitsspiel** ("the Wedding game"): a race game on a spiral board like the game of the goose, sold at fairs. Squares include the **Halde** (back to the start), the **Brautbett** (the bride-bed: throw again), the **Nahbrief** (pay the pot to pass), the **Fall** (swap places with the player ahead) and the **Feast** at the center. Children and adults play it, and the adult version's squares are ruder.

## 5. Lotteries and wagers

- **Lotto di Vellora**: the numbers lottery run from Vellora by the **Banco della Fede**, with agents in Wiedenbrunn and Llucmar. Ninety numbers, five drawn monthly. The poor stake Kupferlinge on dreamed numbers, and there are dream-books that tell you which number a dream of a goose means. The Banco's profit is enormous, and the Hofkammer takes a tenth (→ I35).
- **Glückshafen** (luck-pots): raffles at fairs, for a silver spoon, a pig or a pair of embroidered garters (→ I63).
- **Wettbücher** (betting books): every coffeehouse keeps one, recording wagers between gentlemen: on a duel, a horse, the sex of a royal child, who a famous lady will take as Cortejo this season, whether a certain Zierherr is a **Wolf** (→ R11), and above all **on falls** (→ I48). The **Café Sküs** betting book is the most famous in Wiedenbrunn (→ I67).

## 6. Parlor and forfeit games

These are played in the Salon on winter evenings, at country houses in summer, at Fasching suppers, and anywhere a group of young people is left too long with too little to do. Most of them end in **Pfänder**, forfeits: the loser surrenders a small object (a glove, a ring, a ribbon, a fan) and must perform a penance chosen by the company to get it back. The penances are where the games go.

### Pfänderspiel (Forfeits)
The general game, played with any of the games below. Penances are set blindfold by one player, who doesn't know whose forfeit is held up:
- **Kiss someone** ("the third person to your left," "whoever you love best," "whoever is wearing blue");
- sing a Gstanzl about yourself;
- confess something true;
- carry someone round the room;
- be blindfolded and guess who is kissing you;
- or the hardest: **leave the room with someone, and come back in a quarter hour.**

### Blinde Kuh ("Blind Cow")
Blind man's buff. One player is blindfolded, turned three times, and must catch another and identify them **by touch alone**, so grown men and women end up running their hands over each other's faces, shoulders and waists. Nobody pretends this is innocent, and the great ladies played it with the royal family in the 1680s. At the Redouten it is played in the dark with masks (→ I70).

### Fallraten (the Fall Game)
The tasteless parlor game of the 1690s. The company names a famous card-bearer (a Trump if they're bold, a suit-card if they're cautious), and each player in turn **names who they think the card would fall to if the bearer died tonight**, and why, with the reckoning. Players who are wrong (by the Fall-Reckoners' tables, or by the company's vote) pay forfeits. Played well, it is a parade of everyone's secrets, as they guess at whose beds the bearer has been in (→ R04, I48). It was forbidden at court in 1696 after a Fallraten about the King went much too far. It is still played everywhere else.

### Spiegelpfand (the Mirror Forfeit)
A player stands before a mirror, eyes on the glass. Behind them the company shuffles places. When the caller says *"Spiegel!"*, the player must turn and **kiss whoever is standing behind them**, whom they saw in the mirror an instant before, on the mouth. Refusing is a forfeit. The game is famous for the night in **1690** when a brother and sister of a great house played it at a Fasching supper. The sister turned, saw who was behind her, and did not refuse. The Nähespiel (→ R06) has never recovered, and the game is more popular than ever.

### Die Pfandstunde (the Pledge Hour)
The loser of a game plays **maid**, or footman, to the winner for an hour: fetching, carrying, kneeling, fanning, obeying. Sometimes a ribbon is tied at the loser's throat in the winner's colors, a play-**Pfandband** (→ R07). It was a children's game in the last century. Among the young nobility of the 1690s it has become something else, and the Kammerbüchl have noticed. What happens in a Pfandstunde stays in the hour, by the rules of the game. The **"Pagat"** hour of the Thirty-Two (→ I08) is named after it, or it after the Pagat. Nobody remembers which came first.

### Der Sküs (the hide-and-seek game)
Played in great country houses at night. One player is the **Sküs** and may go anywhere in the house. Whoever finds the Sküs must stay hidden *with* them, until the last seeker finds a cupboard full of breathless people. It is the country-house game, and Bad Sulzau's villas and the Spiegelsee's castle are famous for it.

### Kartenlegen (as a parlor game)
A lady lays the cards for the company, "only in fun," and reads each guest's love-fortune aloud (→ I67). The readings are only in fun until she reads one that's true.

## HOOKS

1. **Fer el Truc.** At a Rocalban supper the protagonist is partnered at Truc de Cort with a Llucmar cousin who begins giving him signals that aren't in any rulebook. Their opponents are her husband and her husband's sister.
2. **The Basseta bank.** At a Fasching card party a young man of an allied house loses everything at a courtesan's Basseta bank, then stakes a promissory note for "one night of a body-pledge of my house" and loses that too. The pledge in question is one of the household's friends, and the courtesan has a very particular buyer in mind.
3. **Spiegelpfand.** At the Ilsenau Mondempfang the Stiftsjahr Elevinnen start a game of Spiegelpfand in the Spiegelsaal, and the protagonist is caught in the line. He knows the mirror shows the room behind. He doesn't know who will be standing in it when the caller says *"Spiegel!"*
4. **Fallraten.** A guest at a supper proposes a round of Fallraten on the Ilsenau Moon. The household's women go still. The protagonist is the only one at the table who doesn't know why the game is dangerous for them.
5. **The Pfandstunde.** A lady who has lost to the protagonist at chess pays her forfeit as a Pfandstunde: a play-ribbon at her throat in Ilsenau blue for an hour of service. She is the wife of the Hofkriegsrat's deputy, and she is enjoying it more than the forfeit requires.
6. **The betting book.** The Café Sküs Wettbuch carries a new wager, *"that the Prinz von Ilsenau is a Wolf"*, at very long odds and with a great many names against it. One of the names for it is a woman of the household.
