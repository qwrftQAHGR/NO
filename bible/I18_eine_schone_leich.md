# I18 · Eine schöne Leich: Death, Burial & Mourning

*Wiedenbrunn's love affair with a good funeral: the deathbed that is also a card-fall, the last rites, the burial of great bodies in three places, the mourning ordinance and its colors, widows and their consolations, the village dead and their painted boards, and the slag-heap where souls are refined.*

**See also:** I48 The Fall-Reckoners (the Sterbewache) · R04 The Cards (the Fall) · I44 House Kalteneck (the Kaltgruft) · I54 The Church of the Wedding (die Halde) · I16 The Zwölften (the walking dead) · I06 (mourning dress) · I13 Folk Belief

---

## 1. "Eine schöne Leich"

**"Eine schöne Leich"** ("a beautiful corpse") is the Wiedenbrunn phrase for **a fine funeral**, and Wiedenbrunn means it as the highest praise. The city's peculiar tenderness about death is famous. Its people save all their lives for a beautiful send-off, sing cheerful songs about the grave at the Heuriger (*"there'll be wine, and we'll be gone,"* → I11), and turn out in thousands to watch a great funeral procession as they would a parade.

- **Sterbebruderschaften** (burial brotherhoods): every guild and parish has one. Members pay a few Kupferlinge a month, and when they die the brotherhood pays for a decent coffin, candles, a procession, a Mahl and a Leichenschmaus. A Wiedenbrunner who can't afford a brotherhood is buried by the parish in a shared shaft. That is the city's private terror.
- **The Leichenbestatter** (undertakers' guild), called by everyone **die Pompfüneberer**, the "pomp-funeral men." They are a trade in black coats and long crape hatbands who provide coffins, hearses with black horses and black plumes, torch-bearers, hired mourners, black cloth for draping the house front, and an itemized bill. A great funeral is graded by the **Klasse** the family pays for: first class has eight horses, sixty torches and the Dom's great bell; fourth class has a handcart. Gossips know every family's Klasse.

## 2. The deathbed

### The Sterbewache (the death-watch)
When someone is dying, the family gathers. The windows are closed, the clocks are watched, candles are lit, and the Zeuge is sent for.

**If the dying person carries a card**, the death-watch becomes a matter of state, of house and of bed (→ R04, I48):
- **The chosen heir lies beside the bearer**, often **in** the bed, skin to skin, holding the hands. That gives presence its tie-breaking weight, and gives the heart its last reminder.
- The room is **cleared** of everyone who might be a dangerous heart: the bearer's favorite servant, an inconvenient lover, a milk-sibling, a beloved pledge. Sometimes this is done gently and sometimes by footmen.
- **The Fall-Reckoners** wait in the antechamber with their books open, their watch-glass and a bottle of wine.
- The family watches **the heir's skin**. When the bearer dies the card falls at once, and **within the day** the Blatt rises on whoever received it.
- **Surprises**: the Blatt rises on someone outside the room (the cook, the Kammerdiener, a pledge in the attic, a lover in another city), and in that instant the whole family learns who the dying one truly loved. *"Ein Fall ist der einzige Tratsch, der nicht lügen kann."* (→ R04)

### The last rites: the Läuterung
The **Läuterung** (refining) is the Church's last rite, given by a Zeuge:
- the dying person's brow, hands and feet are anointed with **salt-oil**, the body's principle consecrated;
- the dying person gives **Zeugnis** (confession) if they can speak;
- the Zeuge places a **gold coin in the mouth** for the **assayer of the Halde**, the coin that pays the soul's way to be weighed and refined (see §6);
- the Zeuge says the formula: *"Geh zur Probe. Was fein ist, bleibt; was Schlacke ist, wird umgeschmolzen."* (Go to the assay. What is fine remains; what is dross will be re-smelted.)

### After death
- **Clocks** are stopped at the hour of death.
- **Mirrors** are covered with black cloth, because a mirror holds a little of whoever looks into it, and the dead mustn't be caught in one (→ I13).
- **A window** is opened so the soul can go.
- The body is washed by women (in great houses by the **Totenwäscherinnen**, the death-washers, a guild of women), dressed in the **Totenhemd** (death-shirt), and laid out on a board.
- **The Totenwache** (wake): the body lies at home for two or three days, with candles and with family and neighbors sitting up through the nights, praying, telling stories, eating and drinking. Peasant wakes are famously lively, with songs and games, and courtships begin at them. The Church disapproves of the games and attends the eating.

## 3. Burial

### The great: three graves
By custom since the Flask King, the **royal family** and the **great houses** that can afford it bury the dead in **three places**, so that each part goes where it belongs:

> **"Das Herz dem Bräutigam, das Innere der Braut, der Leib der Gruft."**
> *The heart to the Bridegroom, the inward parts to the Bride, the body to the crypt.*

- **The heart**, embalmed and sealed in a small **silver urn**, goes to the **Herzgruft** (heart-crypt), a little chapel behind the high altar of the **Dom zum Bräutigam**. Its shelves hold rows of silver urns engraved with names, three centuries of royal and noble hearts.
- **The entrails**, in a copper urn, go to the **Bride's chapel at Brautzell** (→ I31). Ilsenau sends its entrails to the Stille Haus chapel instead, by a privilege of 1499.
- **The body**, embalmed (Zinnsalz, → R03) and sealed in a **lead** coffin inside an ornamented one, goes to the family crypt. For the **royal family** that is **the Kaltgruft**, the royal crypt under the Kalteneck chapel in Wiedenbrunn, kept by **House Kalteneck** (→ I44).

### The Kaltgruft and its ceremony
When a royal body comes to the Kaltgruft, the procession stops at the **iron door** and the **herald knocks**.
- From inside, the **Kalteneck warden** asks: **"Wer begehrt Einlass?"** (Who asks to enter?)
- The herald answers with the dead person's **full titles**: *"His Majesty Ferdinand, King of Valdau, Count-Prince of Rocalba, Bearer of the Sun…"*
- The warden answers: **"Den kennen wir nicht."** (We don't know him.)
- The herald knocks again and answers with the dead person's **karat**, the fineness of the blood as written in the Feinbuch: *"Ferdinand, of twenty-one karat of gold…"*
- The warden answers: **"Hier wiegt man kein Gehalt."** (Here we weigh no fineness.)
- The herald knocks a third time and says: **"Ein Gast der Hochzeit."** (A guest of the Wedding.)
- The warden opens the door.

Every royal funeral since 1591. The court weeps at the third knock. The Kalteneck warden has said "*Hier wiegt man kein Gehalt*" to eleven kings and queens, and the court says the Kaltenecks enjoy it.

### Everyone else
- **Nobles and Bürger**: a family vault or a churchyard grave, a stone, and Mahl said for the soul.
- **The poor**: the parish shaft, or the brotherhood's grave if they paid.
- **Villages**: the churchyard, and the **Beinhaus** (bone-house). In the Hallgau, where the churchyards are cramped between mountain and lake, bones are dug up after ten years, and the **skulls are painted** with the person's name, dates and a wreath of flowers (roses for women, oak for men, ivy for the unmarried). They are stacked in the Beinhaus with their families, where visitors can find their grandmother (→ I24).
- **The Totenbrett** (death-board): in the mountain valleys the board the corpse was laid out on is **painted** with the dead person's name, a verse and a little picture, and **set up by the roadside** or nailed to a barn. Rows of weathered painted Totenbretter line the Hallgau roads, some with very funny verses. *"Hier liegt der Wirt vom Seeblick. / Er trank, was er schenkte. / Jetzt schenkt ein anderer."* (Here lies the landlord of the Lake View. / He drank what he poured. / Now someone else pours.)
- **Rocalba**: burial within a day, as the heat requires. **Ploraneres** (hired women mourners) walk behind the coffin keening. Graves in the walled **cementiris** have niches, family by family, in stacked white walls.
- **Pledges and the bound**: buried by their owners, or not. A household that buries its pledges well is spoken of well. The Grenzer bury Kesrâni captives with a lamp on the grave, by the captives' own custom, and are proud of their decency.

## 4. Mourning: the Trauerordnung

The **Trauerordnung of 1680** (the mourning ordinance) prescribes how long and in what dress the bereaved mourn, by their relation to the dead.

| Relation | Deep mourning | Half-mourning | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| spouse | 1 year | 6 months | 18 months |
| parent or child | 6 months | 6 months | 1 year |
| sibling | 3 months | 3 months | 6 months |
| grandparent, aunt, uncle | 6 weeks | 6 weeks | 3 months |
| cousin | | 6 weeks | 6 weeks |
| **a Left-Hand partner** | not prescribed; by custom 3 months, privately (→ I56) | | |
| **one's lover** | not prescribed; worn as an ornament, a black ribbon in the hair | | |

- **Tiefe Trauer** (deep mourning):
  - **Black wool**, never silk.
  - No lace, no jewels except **jet** or **iron**.
  - For widows, the **Witwenschleier**: a long black veil from a black cap, falling to the waist behind.
  - For men, black coats, black hatbands, black sword-knots, and the **Flor**, a crape band on the left sleeve.
  - **No balls, no theatre, no music in the house**, and no remarriage.
- **Halbtrauer** (half-mourning):
  - **Grey, lilac, violet, white**, with silk allowed.
  - **Silver** jewelry. The Ilsenau women's mourning silver is famously the most beautiful in Wiedenbrunn.
  - Small dinners, cards; no balls.
- **Hoftrauer** (court mourning): for a death in the royal family the whole court goes into black by decree, for months, at enormous profit to the black-cloth merchants and loss to the milliners. A royal death in Fasching **cancels the season**, which the young court has never forgiven any royal for.
- **Servants** wear mourning livery at the family's expense.
- **Mourning jewelry**:
  - rings and lockets holding a **lock of the dead's hair**, plaited by specialist hairworkers;
  - jet from Rocalba;
  - **Starkenberg iron rings**, now a general mourning fashion (→ I06).

## 5. Widows

- **The Witwensitz** (dower house): the house the Ehepakten guarantee a noble widow, usually a smaller property on the family estates, where she lives on her **Wittum** (widow's income) and runs her own life. Many a noble widow's best years begin at the Witwensitz.
- **The Trauerjahr** (mourning-year): no remarriage before a year has passed, by law and by Church. A widow who remarries sooner is talked about. One who remarries the man everyone suspected is talked about much more.
- **The merry widow**: Wiedenbrunn's stock figure of the young widow in her black veil, who has her Wittum, her freedom and no husband. She is the target of every fortune-hunter and the heroine of half the Kammerbüchl. **Witwentrost** (widow's consolation) is the polite word for a widow's lover.
- **Widows' rights**: a widow is legally free to manage her own property and person in a way no wife or daughter is. Bürger widows run their husbands' businesses, and one in five Wiedenbrunn guild-shops is run by a **Meisterwitwe** (master's widow).
- **In Spindelhäuser**: when a **Mondbräutigam** dies, his widow mourns him six months and may remarry at once if the house needs it. Wiedenbrunn finds this heartless. The Spindelhäuser find Wiedenbrunn sentimental (→ R12).
- **Widowers** mourn half as long by the Trauerordnung, and remarry faster. Nobody finds this odd.

## 6. Where the dead go: die Halde

The Church teaches (→ I54) that at death the soul goes **to the assay**. The soul is weighed and tested like ore by the **Probierer** of the Feast, the divine assayer:
- **What is fine** goes to **the Feast**, the eternal wedding banquet of the Bridegroom and Bride, where every guest is seated and fed.
- **What is dross**, the soul's sins, failures, cruelties and waste, goes to **die Halde**, the **slag-heap**. There it waits to be **re-smelted** (*umgeschmolzen*) by the slow fires until it too is fine enough for the table.

The Halde is not hell. It is the spoil-heap of a mine: grey, cold and waiting, but not forever. Every soul is refined in the end, says the Church. The question is how long, and how much it burns.

- **Läutergebete** (refining prayers): the living pray for the dead to speed their refining. A Mahl said for a soul is a day off the Halde, and the rich pay for thousands.
- **Haldentag** (the Day of the Dross), **2 November**: the day of the dead. Graves are cleaned and decorated with lights (red glass lanterns) and late flowers, and families picnic in the churchyards and talk to their dead. Godparents give godchildren **Seelenwecken** (soul-rolls), plaited sweet buns, and the poor go door to door for them. The night before, the **Schlacken** are believed to walk (→ I16).
- **The Schlacken** (unrefined dead) wander in the **Zwölften** (→ I16). The masked **Haldenläufer** play them.
- **Ghosts** are souls whose dross is too heavy to rest: a murderer, a suicide, a mother who abandoned a child, a bearer who died with the wrong person in the room.
- **The miners' version**: the Hallgau and Eisenmark miners, who live beside real slag-heaps, have their own grim jokes. *"Er ist auf die Halde gegangen"* (he's gone to the slag-heap) means *he's dead, and he had it coming*.

## 7. Death and the bound

- A **Muttermagd** (a bound mother whose child her master acknowledged) **becomes free** at her master's death (→ R07). She often stands at the graveside as a free woman for the first time.
- A **Testamentsfreiheit** (freedom by will) is read with the will, sometimes to the family's fury.
- A bearer's **pledge-lover** may receive a **Trump** at the master's death and be **free** in that instant by the Dorl-Patent (→ R04, R07). Trump-houses therefore clear the room.
- The **bound mourn their masters** by custom, dressed in mourning livery at the house's expense. Some mean it.

## HOOKS

1. **The Sterbewache.** An allied house's card-bearer is dying, and the family asks a member of the Ilsenau household to sit in the room "as a friend of the house." Being in the room counts for presence in a fall (→ R04). The family wants the protagonist there to *dilute* the heart of someone they fear, without saying so. The dying bearer, opening her eyes, looks at him for a long time.
2. **The Kaltgruft.** At a royal funeral, the Kalteneck warden says *"Hier wiegt man kein Gehalt"* at the iron door, and catches the protagonist's eye across the procession, as if they shared a joke.
3. **The Totenbrett.** On a Hallgau road a weathered Totenbrett bears the name of an Ilsenau Mondbräutigam of the 1650s, with a painted crescent and a verse nobody in the house remembers commissioning. The verse is a love-poem, to someone who is not his wife.
4. **The merry widow.** A young widow of the Holdegg circle in her first month of deep mourning, with her veil, her Wittum and her new freedom, receives the protagonist in her Witwensitz in black wool. Deep mourning forbids music, balls, theater and remarriage. It says nothing about anything else.
5. **The surprise on the skin.** At the death of a minor card-bearer of the household's circle, the Blatt rises not on the heir lying in the bed but on the protagonist, who was in the next room and had met the dying woman twice. Nobody can explain the tie, the Fall-Reckoners least of all. A Cavall de Copes now sits on his wrist, and the dead woman's family would like an explanation (→ R04, I47).
6. **Haldentag.** On the Day of the Dross, the protagonist goes with the household to the Ilsenau graves. Someone has already lit a red lantern on the oldest neglected grave there, that of the Wilburg daughter who walked into the Spiegelsee in her sleep in 1601, and left a Seelenwecken and a note in a woman's hand, signed only with a crescent. It reads: *"You too were the only one."*
