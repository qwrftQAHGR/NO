# R09 · Sapphic & Queer Life

*Love between women, love between men, and the bodies and presentations that don't sit neatly on either side: how each is seen at court, in town, in the villages and abroad, what rites and words exist for them, and why women's love is the most tolerated love in the realm.*

**See also:** R10 Futas · R11 Femme Men · R12 House Ilsenau · I56 The Left Hand · I31 Brautzell (the Fräuleinstift) · I71 The Damenduell · I68 The Hofoper · I72 The Bath-Slave Empress · I58 The Confraria de l'Hereu · I77 The Chastity Commission

---

## 1. The principle: what makes no heir troubles no one

In a realm where the sins that matter are **disorder** (a child in the wrong place) and **pride** (kin presuming to imitate the gods), love that makes no child troubles almost nobody. The rule of thumb, spoken in every great house:

> **"Was keinen Erben macht, macht keine Sorgen."**
> *What makes no heir makes no worry.*

That puts **women's love** at the top of the realm's tolerance. It is open in the great houses, discreet among the Bürger, joked about in the villages and invisible to the law. **Men's love** is tolerated among nobles, gossiped about among the Bürger, and prosecuted, now and then, among the poor. **Futas** are the great exception to the rule, because a futa *can* make an heir (→ R10).

## 2. Rites and recognitions

### The Verschwisterung (sister-making)
An old church rite, still performed, that blesses two women as **Herzensschwestern**, heart-sisters.

- **The rite**: the two women kneel before a Zeuge in a side chapel, usually the chapel of the **Two Marthas** (see below). Each braids a ribbon of her own color into the other's hair. They drink from a single cup and say the old words, *"Wo du spinnst, will ich spinnen"* ("Where you spin, I will spin").
- **What it creates**: a bond that is not a marriage and is understood by everyone to be more than friendship. Heart-sisters may share a house, inherit from each other by will, be buried together, and act for each other in law when one is ill. A married woman may have a heart-sister. Her husband is not consulted, and a husband who objects is laughed at.
- **The braid**: heart-sisters wear the other's ribbon braided at the wrist, the **Zopfband**. In society, a woman wearing a plaited ribbon at her wrist in a color not her own is announcing a heart-sister. Some women wear one to *suggest* a heart-sister they don't have.
- **The register**: since 1411 (→ I72), a Verschwisterung may be entered in the **Left Hand register** as a bed-bond (→ I56), which matters for card-falls. Matriarchal houses use this constantly.

### The Two Marthas
**Martha the Spinner and Martha of the Bath** were two women of Montalba in the first century of the faith. By legend they lived together for fifty years, nursed the sick through a plague, died within a day of each other, and were buried in one grave. That grave was opened in 1340 and found to hold the two skeletons, one's head on the other's shoulder. The **Marthentag** in October is their feast. Their chapels hang with braided ribbons left by grateful heart-sisters.

The Church teaches that the Marthas were saints of charity. Every woman who has ever left a ribbon in their chapel knows better.

### The Verbrüderung (brother-making)
The men's equivalent, a blessing of two men as sworn brothers with a shared cup and exchanged knives.

- It was common until the **Clear Church** attacked it as a cover for sodomy during the Brides' War (→ I74). The Wedding Church, stung, stopped performing it publicly in 1624.
- It survives:
  - on **the frontier**, as the Grenzer *pobratimstvo*, sworn brotherhood in blood, sealed with a cut palm and a shared drink;
  - in **Ormánd**, as the *fogadott testvér*, the sworn sibling;
  - in the **regimental mess**, where brother-officers swear on crossed swords.
- None of those is officially about love. All of them sometimes are.

## 3. Women with women

### In the great houses: Spindelliebe
In the **Spindelhäuser**, the distaff houses ruled by women (→ R05, R12), love among the house's women is called **Spindelliebe**, distaff-love. It covers wives and maids, mistresses and Bettmägde, friends, rivals' daughters, and the house's own sisters and cousins.
- Kin-love between women is **Spiel**, play (→ R06). It makes no heir and earns no Nahvermerk, and in the Spindelhäuser it is old house custom, not anyone else's business.
- It has its places: the **Schwesternbad** (the house's women's bath), the shared winter beds, the long evenings of the **Spinnzimmer** with the hair-brushing and braiding.
- The houses also use it **as policy**. A Trump held by a woman of the house can be kept among the house's women through registered heart-sister bonds and shared beds, without involving a single man. Ilsenau has done this with the Moon since 1458 (→ R12).

### At court
- **The Busenfreundin** ("bosom-friend"): a married lady's woman-friend, with whom she shares a Loge, a carriage and sometimes a bed. A Busenfreundin is not adultery in anyone's ledger. A husband who objects is ridiculous, and one who doesn't is suspected of watching.
- **The Hofdamen**, the Queen's ladies, live together in the **Damenstock**, a wing of the Greifenburg, under the Obersthofmeisterin's rule (→ I69). Their friendships, jealousies and affairs are the court's longest-running entertainment.
- **The lady and her maid**: the commonest love-story of all, and the least admitted. The lady and her Zofe or Leibmagd share everything a body shares in a day: dressing, washing, illness, secrets. What happens at night is between them (→ R07).
- **The Damenduell** (→ I71): noblewomen settle their quarrels with rapiers, bare-breasted, to first blood, with men barred. Some duels are quarrels. Some are courtships. Some are both.

### In institutions
- **The Damenstift**: a residence for unmarried noblewomen of good family, who live on an endowment, keep a light rule, wear a modest habit at chapel and otherwise do as they please. The most famous is the **Fräuleinstift at Brautzell** (→ I31). In slang a **Stiftsdame** (canoness) is a woman who loves women, and every Stiftsdame knows it.
- **The Stiftsjahr**: noble girls' year of finishing in a great household (→ I19). Many a girl's first love was another Elevin, or the lady of the house.
- **Convents of the Bride**: the **Brautschwestern**, nuns of the Bride's order, keep their own counsel. The Abbesses of Montalba have passed the **Papess** Trump from abbess to chosen successor for three hundred years through the **Abbess's Bed**, by taking the successor as heart-sister and lover (→ I46, I54). It is the Church's best-known open secret.

### In town and village
- **The Kaffeekränzchen**: women's afternoon coffee-circles, Wiedenbrunn's gossip network and its most tolerated sapphic society.
- **The Wäschermädel** and the **Schnitterinnen** (laundresses and harvest-women) work, live and sleep in women's bands. Their bands are famous for their songs and their loyalties.
- **The Sennerinnen**: in the high valleys young women take the herds up to the summer pastures, the **Alm**, alone for three months. *"Auf der Alm gibt's ka Sünd'"*: on the Alm there's no sin. The saying is old, and the Sennerinnen's summers are proverbial in both directions (→ I36).
- **The Badstube**'s women's days (→ I64).
- **Hosenrollen**: actresses who play young men in trouser-roles at the Hofoper and the popular stages. They are adored by women of every class, and some are famous for whom they go home with (→ I68).

### Signals
A Zopfband at the wrist; a crescent patch high on the cheekbone, Ilsenau fashion and widely copied (→ I07); a fan closed and touched to the lips while looking at another woman; a carnation, worn rather than given.

### Words
Polite:
- **Herzensschwester**: heart-sister.
- **Busenfreundin**: bosom-friend.
- **Freundin**, with a certain emphasis.

Knowing:
- **eine Marthe** (a Martha): a woman who loves women.
- **Zopfschwester**: braid-sister.
- **Stiftsdame**: a woman who loves women, from the Damenstift.

Vulgar:
- **Reiberin**, rubber; old, crude, and used by the Straw Court.

## 4. Men with men

- **Among nobles**: tolerated as a gentleman's private matter. A great lord's **Kammerdiener** (valet), his secretary, his officer-friend and his favorite singer are understood. As long as there is an heir somewhere, nobody asks. A nobleman can be laughed at for it but not ruined.
- **In the regiments**: **Kameradschaft**, comradeship, which officially means nothing and unofficially means a code. The Ormándi hussars' messes and the Grenzer border-posts have traditions nobody writes memoirs about.
- **Among the Bürger**: whispered ruin. A Bürger son caught with another man is sent abroad, married off in haste, or blackmailed for years. The Clear Church's influence is strongest here.
- **Among the poor**: joked about, accepted in the bands of boatmen, miners and carters, and exposed to the **Widernatur** statute (§5).
- **Male houses of pleasure**: the Rosengrund has **Herrenhöfe**, "gentlemen's courts," where men pay men. Their Lustherren (men for hire) range from rough boatmen to powdered **Zierherren**. Some are pledged and some are free (→ I23, R11).
- **The opera and the stage**: the **Silberstimmen**, the high-voiced male singers of the Hofoper, have admirers of every sex, and lovers to match (→ I68).

### Words
Polite:
- **ein Freund des Hauses**: a friend of the house.
- **"er ist von der Verbrüderung"**: he's of the brotherhood.

Knowing:
- **ein Warmer**, a "warm one"; *warmer Bruder*.
- **Bruderherz**: brother-heart, used with a smile.

Vulgar:
- **Hinterlader** ("breech-loader"), soldiers' slang, an insult.
- **Arschficker**, gutter.

Rocalban:
- **bardaix**, old and harsh.
- **marieta**, mocking, for a femme man.

## 5. The law: the Widernatur statute

- **The Widernatur-Patent of 1532**, issued in the panic after the First Siege, punishes "acts against nature" with fines, the pillory, banishment or, for the worst cases, the Cinabre mines. In the text, those acts are men with men and men with beasts.
- **Women are not named** in the text. The jurists of 1532 could not imagine women committing an act against nature, and later jurists have found it convenient not to imagine it either. **No woman has ever been prosecuted under it.**
- **It is enforced only against those without karat or patrons.** A boatman caught with a carter goes to the pillory. A count caught with his valet goes to the country for a season. Rumorwache men extort the statute's victims far more often than they arrest them (→ I20).
- **The Chastity Commission** of 1692–1695 tried to enforce it against the great. It arrested the Archbishop's nephew in a Zierherren tavern in the Rosengrund, then had to release him the same night. That was the beginning of the Commission's end (→ I77).
- **Rocalba** has its own statute, rarely used. The Confraria de l'Hereu (→ I58) and the Mercury culture of the coast make Llucmar the realm's most relaxed city on these matters.

## 6. Bodies between

- **Futas** (the **Zweierlei**), women of both natures (→ R10). Socially and legally women; desired, feared a little, and politically important in the Spindelhäuser.
- **Femme men** (the **Zierherren**; Rocalban **donzells**) (→ R11): men who are slight, smooth, soft-voiced or dressed and mannered as the court's ornaments. Fashionable at court, mocked by the Bürger, scorned in the regiments.
- **The Church** sees both through **the Heir**, the androgynous third person of the faith (→ I54). Futas are **erbgestaltig**, "heir-formed," and considered auspicious. Femme men are "Heir-touched." The Confraria de l'Hereu goes further and makes them its clergy (→ I58).

## 7. Abroad

| Sovereignty | Women | Men |
|---|---|---|
| **Almagra** (→ I32) | Tolerated in silence; convents famous for *amistades particulares* ("particular friendships") | The **pecado nefando**, the Unspeakable Sin: the Santo Crisol burns men for it, and the court is full of it |
| **Ormánd** (→ I33) | Harvest-dance brides who "choose a sister" | Hussar sworn-brothers; officially nothing, really everything |
| **Kesrân** (→ I34) | The **Andarun**, the women's palace, where friendships between concubines are poems of their own | A great literature of the beloved; **zenne** dancers; legally shady and socially everywhere |
| **Vellora** (→ I35) | Courtesans' circles; the **Compagnie del Ventaglio**, the fan-companies of young patrician women | The **Night Wardens** prosecute men; courtesans dress as boys to attract them |
| **The Sworn Valleys** (→ I36) | The Sennerinnen's summers | Clear Church condemnation, jokes about shepherds |
| **Asagiri** (→ I37) | Court women's love for each other written openly in poetry | Warriors' bonds between adult comrades, long honored in their histories |

## 8. History

- **The Two Marthas** (first century of the faith); their grave opened in 1340.
- **1411**: the Bath-Slave Empress. An Ilsenau Fürstin's heir takes her mother's bath-maid as heart-sister and registered Left-Hand lover to recover the Empress Trump. This is the origin of the Spindelhäuser's political use of sapphic bonds (→ I72).
- **1532**: the Widernatur-Patent.
- **1572**: **Haus Zweilinden** is founded by two Hallgau heiresses, Ottilie von Thurnau and Kunigunde Mayr, who married each other by Verschwisterung, merged their estates, and raised four children fathered by a hired Kogel stud. The Crown recognized the house in 1580 after a lawsuit that still has its own shelf in the Hofkammer library (→ I45).
- **1624**: the Wedding Church stops performing the Verbrüderung publicly.
- **1671**: **the Two Countesses of Bad Sulzau** fight the most famous Damenduell in the realm's history. Within the year they are heart-sisters (→ I71).
- **1694**: the **Hosenrollen controversy** at the Hofoper. The Archbishop demands that women stop playing men; the Hofoper's female audience riots in the Loge corridors; the Archbishop withdraws (→ I68).
- **1692–1695**: the Chastity Commission (→ I77).

## HOOKS

1. **The Zopfband.** At a Cercle a woman of the household is seen wearing a braided ribbon at her wrist in a color not her own. Within an hour three people have asked the protagonist whose it is.
2. **Marthentag.** On the Marthas' feast a young Stiftsdame asks an Ilsenau woman to stand witness at her Verschwisterung with a Bürger widow, which is scandalous across ranks. The Stiftsdame's family will hold the witness responsible.
3. **The Abbess's Bed.** Montalba's Abbess is old. The novice she has chosen as successor and heart-sister has fled the abbey, and the Papess will fall to *someone* when the Abbess dies. The Church asks Ilsenau, discreetly, to find the girl.
4. **The statute.** A Zierherr who serves the household as music-master is arrested by the Rumorwache under the Widernatur statute in a Rosengrund Herrenhof raid. He has no karat and no patron but the house. Getting him out means saying so in front of the Rumormeister.
5. **Spindelliebe.** The protagonist, newly awake, walks into the Schwesternbad at the wrong hour, a hall of women who have shared that water for generations and have never had a man of the house among them. Nobody screams. Somebody hands him a towel. Everybody watches what he does next.
6. **The Hosenrolle.** The Hofoper's most adored trouser-role actress sends a note asking to meet "the young Prinz von Ilsenau who looks like the part I should be playing." Whether she wants a lover, a rival or a model is not clear from the note.
