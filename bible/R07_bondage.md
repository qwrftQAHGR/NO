# R07 · Bondage (slavery)

*The four ways a person comes to belong to another in the Double Crown, how the arrangement looks from a duchess's dressing room and from the bottom of a quicksilver shaft, and the large, ordinary, unexamined sexual culture that has grown up around it.*

**See also:** I61 The Seelenmakler & the Pfandamt · I62 The Order of the Open Hand · I04 Below Stairs · I23 The Rosengrund · I30 Cinabre · I50 The Pit-Blood Trade · I72 The Bath-Slave Empress · I57 The Ammenhaus

---

## 1. Words

Valdau doesn't like the word *Sklave*. It sounds Kesrâni and makes Valdauers feel like Kesrâni. The law speaks of **gebundene Leute**, bound people. Households speak of **unsere Leute**, "our people," and of each bound person as **Knecht** or **Magd**, the same words used for free servants. Context, and the ribbon at the throat, makes the difference.

Rocalba is less squeamish: **esclau / esclava** for born and bought slaves, **captiu / captiva** for war-captives, **forçat** for galley-convicts.

A bound person is:
- **gebunden**, bound;
- **am Band**, on the ribbon;
- **verpfändet**, pledged.

Freeing one is **losbinden**, unbinding; **freien** or **ledig sprechen**, declaring free; or Catalan **franquejar**.

---

## 2. The four kinds

### Pfandleute: the pledged
The commonest form in Valdau, and the one with the most paperwork.

- **What it is**: a person pledges their own body and labor, or a parent or guardian pledges a grown child, to work off a debt for a term. Everything goes through the **Pfandamt**, the Pledge Office, which registers it, numbers it and taxes it (→ I61).
- **Who**: no one under eighteen may be pledged. A commoner between eighteen and twenty-one may be pledged only with a parent's or guardian's signature, and these signatures are the commonest forgery in the trade. Most pledges are poor young adults: daughters of indebted farmers, sons of ruined craftsmen, widows who signed for a dead husband's debts.
- **Term**: at most **seven years** by the **Pfandordnung of 1354**, made after the Grey Death when labor was scarce and lords were buying peasants. Terms are extended by new debts the pledge "incurs": clothing, medicine, a broken plate, the board during an illness. A clever owner can keep a pledge for life seven years at a time. A clever pledge keeps a copy of their accounts.
- **Redemption**: within the term, kin may buy the pledge back at the registered price plus the Pfandamt's fee. This is the **Rückkaufrecht**. Families save for years to do it. Owners who want to keep a pledge raise the registered price with "investments in training."
- **What kind of service**: the contract must name a **Dienst**, a category of service, and the price depends on it:
  - **Hausdienst**: household work.
  - **Felddienst**: field and stable.
  - **Ammendienst**: wet-nursing (→ I57).
  - **Leibdienst**: bodily service. This is the explicit category for a pledge whose duties include the master's or mistress's bed. It is legal, registered, and since the Patent of 1519 restricted to noble households and licensed houses (→ R05). A body-pledge is a **Leibpfand**, more commonly a **Leibmagd** or **Leibknecht**. The price runs from two to five times a household pledge's, depending on youth, looks, karat and accomplishments.
- **Rights**: a pledge keeps their name, their kin, their faith and their right to complain to the **Pfandgericht** (→ I61). They can marry (with the owner's consent), and their children are born free. That leaves a great deal of room for everything else.

### Kriegsgut: war-goods
- **What**: captives taken on the frontier and the sea. Mostly Kesrâni, some Ormándi rebels from old wars, and corsairs' crews taken by Llucmar galleys.
- **Term**: life, unless ransomed (**Loskauf**) or exchanged under a truce. The **Peace of the Willow Bridge** included a great captive exchange in 1684, and its renewal will include another (→ I28, I76).
- **Where they go**: men to the galleys, the mines, the Grenzer farms and noble stables. Women to households, as maids, nurses, dancers and concubines. Kesrâni women are fashionable in Wiedenbrunn, with their kohl, their music and their coffee. A **Kesrânerin** in a great house is half servant and half ornament, and gossip always assumes the other half.
- **The ransomers**: the **Order of the Open Hand** redeems Valdauer captives from Kesrân and, quietly, resells Kesrâni captives in Valdau to pay for it (→ I62).

### Erbleute: the born-bound
- **What**: children of a bound mother, born bound.
- **In Valdau** this was abolished for children acknowledged by a master in **1412** (the Dorl-Patent, see §5) and for all children in Valdau-proper by the **Distaff Sanction of 1555**, which had a clause about the distaff that clerks extended to slave mothers. Only a few old Erbleute remain, mostly elderly servants in old houses.
- **In Rocalba** it was never abolished. The Corts exempted Rocalba from the 1555 clause, and the Rocalban law of slaves, **els Usos dels Esclaus**, still makes a bound mother's child bound. Rocalba has the realm's only real hereditary slave population, a few thousand, mostly in Llucmar households and on coastal estates.

### Strafknechte: the penal-bound
- **What**: convicts sentenced to bondage for crimes, for a term or for life.
- **Where**: the **Cinabre** quicksilver mines, which are the worst; the **Llucmar galleys**, which are nearly as bad; the **Hammerherren's forges** in the Eisenmark; and the salt-boilers of the Hallgau.
- **Who**: thieves, smugglers, deserters, forgers, men convicted under the Widernatur statute (→ R09), rioters, and anyone whose crime was being poor in front of a judge.
- These are the people Valdauers do not think about when they say bondage "isn't so bad here."

### And what is *not* bondage
To be clear about the spectrum: free **Dienstboten** (wage servants, → I04) are not bound, though they need a good Gesindebuch entry to find work. **Robot** peasants owe labor to their lords but cannot be sold (→ R01). **Apprentices** are bound to their masters by indenture until they become journeymen. Valdauers like to point at all of these to prove that bondage is just one more kind of service. Bound people do not find this persuasive.

## 3. What it looks like

**At the top**: the **Leibmagd** of a great lady. She wears silk her mistress has outgrown, eats from the Kammertisch, sleeps in the Garderobe beside the bedroom, carries her mistress's secrets and sometimes her mistress, has favorites among the footmen and enemies among the maids, and is perhaps loved. She also wears the house's ribbon at her throat and can be sold the day her mistress tires of her.

**In the middle**: a Hausdienst pledge in a Bürger household, worked hard, fed adequately, counting the days of the term and the debts added to it.

**At the bottom**: a **tremoloso** in the Cinabre mines with his teeth gone and his hands shaking, or a galley oarsman chained to a bench in July (→ I30).

**Capital and provinces**: in Wiedenbrunn bondage is mostly household and body pledges, the decorative, intimate, urban kind. In the provinces it is fieldwork and mines. In Rocalba it is galleys, born household slaves and the sea-trade in captives. On the frontier it is captives on both sides, and people who were free last week.

## 4. Marks and dress

- **Valdau doesn't brand** (forbidden since 1602) and doesn't collar (a Kesrâni practice, despised). A Valdauer pledge wears the **Pfandband**, a ribbon of the owner's livery color tied at the throat, and carries a small tin **Pfandmarke** stamped with the Pfandamt registry number.
  - A free servant in livery wears the same colors on coat or dress, **never at the throat**. Ribbon at the throat means bound.
  - Fashionable households have their body-pledges' ribbons made of silk velvet with a little silver clasp. Ilsenau's are midnight-blue with a crescent clasp.
- **The Bandgruß**: a pledge greeting their owner touches the ribbon at their throat with two fingers (→ I01).
- **"Am Band ziehen"**, to pull the ribbon. A light tug at a pledge's throat-ribbon is a command to follow, and between lovers, master and pledge or not, it has become an erotic gesture with its own grammar. Free courtesans of the Rosengrund wear throat-ribbons as a costume.
- **Rocalba**: born slaves wear an iron **anella** (ring) in one ear, and freedmen keep the ear-hole all their lives. Galley convicts are shaved.
- **Kesrâni captives** are forbidden by the Kleiderordnung to wear turbans in public (→ I06).

## 5. Customs and laws that shape lives

- **Bettrecht**, the bed-right. A body-pledge's contract gives the owner sexual access, plainly. Born slaves and war-captives are understood to be accessible by custom. A household pledge's contract does not. The law says a household pledge taken to bed against the contract may complain to the Pfandgericht and have their term reduced. In practice the complaint is rarely made and more rarely won, but it is made, and owners do fear it a little.
- **The Muttermagd.** A bound woman who bears her owner's child, *and whose child he acknowledges*, is protected by the **Dorl-Patent of 1412** (→ I72):
  - the child is **free** from birth;
  - the mother **cannot be sold** after the acknowledgment;
  - she becomes **free at her owner's death** (*"sie freit sich mit seinem Tod"*).

  Owners therefore often decline to acknowledge. Mothers therefore often make sure the whole household knows whose child it is. A great deal of household drama lives in that gap.
- **The Dorl-Patent's other clause**: **a Trump-bearer cannot be held bound in Valdau.** A pledge or slave who receives a Trump is free from the moment the Blatt shows (→ R04). Since the Cards fall by *bed*, a bound person who shares a bearer's bed is a potential free person, and potentially an heir to the house's most precious possession. This is why Trump-houses watch their slaves' beds as closely as their children's, keep their bearers' body-pledges on short terms, and every so often sell one in a hurry.
- **The Kettenhochzeit**, the "chain-wedding." The Church marries the bound: *"jeder Gast wird gespeist,"* every guest is fed, every guest may wed. The vows are the same, the feast is smaller, and the rings are iron. Marriage doesn't free anyone. By Church rule a married bound couple may not be sold apart. The rule is broken constantly, and fines are paid to the Bride's See, which is why the Church enforces it (→ I17).
- **Freeing**: by **Freibrief** (a letter of manumission, registered at the Pfandamt), by **Loskauf** (ransom or self-purchase), by **Testament** (in a will), by expiry of a pledge's term, by the Dorl-Patent, or by becoming too old to be worth feeding. A freed person owes a **Dankjahr**, a year of thankful service, to the former owner. Freed body-pledges often stay on as paid servants, as lovers or as wives, and sometimes because they have nowhere else to go.

## 6. The erotic culture of bondage

Bondage in the Double Crown is not mostly about sex. It is mostly about debt, labor and war. But sex is where it is most visible, most talked about and most enjoyed, and a whole culture has grown up around it.

- **The Bettmagd** (bed-maid) and the **Kammerknecht** (chamber-man) are job titles in great households. They warm the bed, keep the night candle, stay the night when wanted, and sleep on the pallet in the Garderobe when not. In Spindelhäuser the Bettmägde are often attached to the daughters, which is one of several reasons the Spindelhäuser are famous for their women's intimacies (→ R09, R12).
- **The Pfandschulen**, the pledge-schools: training houses in the Rosengrund, licensed by the Seelenmakler's guild (→ I61). They take body-pledges for six months to two years and teach music, reading aloud, dancing, the toilette, massage, conversation, discretion and the bedroom arts (by the book, → I08). A graduate gets a **Schulbrief**, a certificate that raises her or his price by half. The best-known is **Madame Rosl's on the Rosengrund**, whose graduates are called *Röslein*, little roses.
- **The Quartalsschau**: the quarterly showing at the Seelenmakler's hall, where pledges and captives are viewed, examined, assayed and bought. It is a social event for the buyers. The wealthier buyers bring friends, wine and opinions (→ I61).
- **Lending.** Owners lend body-pledges to friends for a season, a journey or a Fasching: **verleihen**. A pledge lent is bound by the same contract to the borrower. Some pledges find the lending the best part of their term, some the worst.
- **The Morgengabe**, the morning-gift a husband gives his bride after the wedding night, is among the rich sometimes a Leibmagd or Leibknecht for the bride's own household (→ I17). It is a sign of trust, or a spy, or both.
- **Matched pairs**: grown twins, sisters, brother-and-sister, sold together and trained together. They are the most expensive items in the trade and the most discussed (→ R06).
- **Futas and femme men** command a premium in the catalogues (→ R10, R11).
- **Karat-pledges**: pit-blood pledges bought for **annealing**, to breed fineness back into a brittle line without the cost of a noble marriage (→ R03, I50). In the catalogue a karat-pledge is listed with metal and fineness: *"Kogel-Eisen, 13 Karat, gesund."* Buyers call them **Zuchtpfand**, breeding-pledges, a stud-farm word that polite people avoid.
- **Free and bound love.** The love story the Double Crown tells most and admits least: the lady and her Leibmagd, the master and his Kammerknecht, the Bettmagd who has been in the same bed for twelve years. Songs call it **Herz an der Kette**, the heart on the chain. The ribbon means the bound one can't leave, and also that nobody can make them stay in love.
- **Play-bondage among the free.** Since the ribbon is erotic, the free play with it: masked ladies at Fasching in throat-ribbons, the parlor game **Pfandstunde** where the loser plays maid to the winner for an hour (→ I09), lovers who exchange ribbons. Real pledges find this either very funny or not funny at all.

The world doesn't moralize about any of this, and neither should the narration. People inside it experience it as everything from tenderness to terror, often in the same week.

## 7. Who benefits, who pays, who is changing it

- **Benefit**: noble and Bürger households (cheap service, intimate service); the **Seelenmakler** guild (fees, schools, auctions); the **Pfandamt** and thus the Hofkammer (registry fees); the **Banco della Fede**, which finances the trade's credit; Rocalban shipowners and galley-masters; mine leaseholders; and the Open Hand.
- **Pay**: the poor who sign; the captives who don't get to; and the miners and oarsmen.
- **Resent it**: **the guilds**, because pledged labor undercuts journeymen's wages. The weavers' guild riots about it once a decade. **The Clear Church**, which preaches that "no guest at the Wedding is another guest's goods" and runs escape routes to the Sworn Valleys. **Free servants**, who despise pledges as **Bandler** ("ribbon-people") and fear being replaced by them. **The pledges themselves**, in a thousand small ways.
- **Reforming it**: **House Ehrenreith's Codex** (→ I43) would register every bound person in one royal book with uniform terms, abolish "debts incurred in service," cap Leibdienst prices and give the Pfandgericht teeth. It would not abolish bondage. Owners hate it because they would lose the extensions. The Seelenmakler hate it because they would lose the margins. Some pledges hope for it, and some fear that a royal register would make their bondage permanent in ink.
- **Escaping it**: the **Bergläufer**, "mountain-runners," flee north over the Firnwand to the Sworn Valleys, where **"Bergluft macht frei"**: mountain air makes free. Under Valleys law any bound person who reaches the Valleys is free. Valdau and the Valleys have argued about extradition for a century (→ I36).

## 8. The black market

- **Menschenfang**, man-catching: kidnapping free people, especially girls from the country arriving in Wiedenbrunn and foreign travelers, and registering them on forged pledge papers with a forged parent's signature. The Rumorwache is bribed to look away (→ I20).
- **Schwarzpfand**: unregistered pledges, held on private contracts to avoid the Pfandamt's fees and limits. It is common in the Rosengrund and illegal. It also means the pledge has no Pfandgericht to complain to.
- **Smuggled captives**: Kesrâni captives brought across the Velika after the truce forbade new taking.
- **Card-catching**: buying, kidnapping or seducing suit-card bearers to own their knack. Illegal, and done.
- **Forged Freibriefe**: for runaways, sold in the Grenzmark and the Hallgau.

## 9. Foreign views

| Sovereignty | Practice and opinion (→ I32–I37) |
|---|---|
| **Kesrân** | Slavery is everywhere and *upward*: slave soldiers, slave officials, slave viziers, and slave mothers of the throne. Kesrâni find Valdauer bondage petty, stingy and hypocritical: "they keep slaves but won't let them rise." |
| **Almagra** | Galleys, mines and **esclavos de ganancia**, slaves who work for wages and pay their owners a daily fee. Harsh and bureaucratic. |
| **Vellora** | The great broker. Every slave sale is notarized and taxed. Courtesans own their maids. |
| **Ormánd** | No slavery, and very proud of it. Serfdom instead (*jobbágy*), whose peasants cannot leave the land and owe the lord nearly everything. Valdauers enjoy pointing this out. |
| **The Sworn Valleys** | Bondage is illegal, and runaways who reach the Valleys are free. Their mercenaries guard the Valdauer king anyway. |
| **Asagiri** | Indentured service by contract for a term (*nenki*), especially for courtesans, who are bought by houses and work off the price. The envoy's retinue found the Valdauer ribbon "very elegant." |

## 10. Slang

- **Bandler / Bandlerin**: a pledge (contemptuous; free servants' word).
- **Pfandl**: affectionate diminutive for a pledge, *"mein Pfandl."*
- **Herzpfand**: a sweetheart, "pledge of my heart," used between free lovers too.
- **"am Band"**: owned; also, of a free person, besotted, wrapped around someone's finger.
- **Kettenhund**: a loyal servant (insult).
- **Kesrânerl**: diminutive for a Kesrâni captive, patronizing.
- **Zittermann / tremolós**: a Cinabre convict.
- **Galeerer**: a galley-man.
- **Röslein**: a graduate of Madame Rosl's school.
- **Zuchtpfand**: a karat-pledge (vulgar).
- **losgekauft**: ransomed; also, of a man, finally married.

## HOOKS

1. **The ribbon in the drawer.** Among the things kept from the years of the Bleischlaf is a Pfandband of Ilsenau blue with a silver crescent clasp, and a Pfandamt registry number. The pledge it belonged to served in the Stille Haus during the long sleep. Her term ended, or didn't.
2. **The Bettmagd's acknowledgment.** A Leibmagd in an allied house is pregnant, and the master has not acknowledged the child. Her mistress, who is fond of her, wants the household to "help him remember" before the birth. The master is a Trump-bearer.
3. **The Quartalsschau.** An invitation to the spring showing comes with a catalogue in which one item is underlined in another hand: a Weißach silver-miner's daughter at 14 karat, Leibdienst, Schulbrief from Madame Rosl's. Someone wants her bought by Ilsenau, and someone else wants to make sure she isn't.
4. **The Bergläufer.** A pledge of the household vanishes north, and a letter from the Sworn Valleys arrives saying she is free and will not return, unless someone in particular comes to ask her.
5. **The Codex hearing.** House Ehrenreith invites a member of the Seven Chairs to sit on a commission hearing testimony from pledges about their contracts, under oath, in the Justice-bearer's presence, where lies hurt. Some of the testimony will be about people the protagonist knows.
6. **Matched.** A Seelenmakler offers the household, at a "family price," a pair of grown twins, brother and sister, Rocalban Erbleute, trained as a matched pair. Their papers say their mother was a slave in Llucmar, and their father's name is left blank in a hand that looks like it was erased.
