# R04 · The Cards (tarot)

*The third Art, and the only one nobody practices. The Cards decide **who**. Seventy living offices are scattered through the known world, each carried in one human body at a time. When the body dies, the card goes to whoever loved it most. That rule has bent marriage, slavery, sex and inheritance around itself for a thousand years.*

## 1. What the Cards are

There are exactly **seventy**:

- **The 22 Trumps** (*die Trümpfe*, called *Tarocke* in the coffeehouses; Catalan *els triomfs*): the **Sküs**, which is unnumbered and beats everything, and **I to XXI**, from the Pagat up to the World. They are named in Valdau by their Tarock names.
- **The 48 suit-cards** (*die Farbblätter*; Catalan **les cartes de pal**, "suit-cards"): four suits of twelve, **Oros** (coins), **Copes** (cups), **Espases** (swords) and **Bastos** (batons). Each suit runs from the **As** (ace) through the **Nou** (nine), then the three figures, **Sota** (knave), **Cavall** (knight) and **Rei** (king). They are named in Catalan even in Wiedenbrunn, because the Rocalban notaries were the first to keep a book of them (→ I47). A Valdauer may call the Rei d'Espases the *Schwertkönig*, but the Fall-Reckoners will correct him.

Each card exists in **exactly one living person** at a time, and in no one when it is lost between bearers. The person carries it as a mark on the skin and a power in the body.

### Where they came from (nobody knows)
- **The Church** teaches that the cards are the **Gastgeschenke**, the guest-gifts, that the Heir handed out at the Wedding feast to the first guests. The Trumps went to the honored guests at the high table, the suit-cards to the guests below the salt. The liturgy still calls bearers *Geschenkte*, "the gifted" (→ I54).
- **The scholars** note that the oldest Valdauer record of a card passing at death is **Brother Udalrich's Chronicle** of 1022, and that the Rocalban notarial books of the cartes de pal go back to the 1180s. The *playing* cards came afterward. The Tarock deck and the Rocalban baralla were drawn from the marks on people's skins, not the other way round. The coffeehouses play with copies of the marks of the gifted (→ I67).
- **The Kesrâni** call the marks *nişan* (signs) and have their own stories about them (→ I34). **Asagiri** has never had any. Its envoy is the first Asagiri ever marked, which has set off the hottest scholarly argument in Wiedenbrunn: whether a card can travel to a land that never knew the Wedding (→ I37).

## 2. The Blatt

When a card settles in a body, a **Blatt** (leaf, sheet, the same word as a playing card; Catalan **la fulla**) appears on the skin within a day. It looks like an old tattoo or a faded bruise in fine lines, the image of the card as the decks show it.

- **Trumps** are palm-sized, drawn in violet-black lines like old ink, usually on the chest, back, belly or thigh. The Sküs is a figure of a running fool, the Moon a moon over two towers and a pool, the Thirteenth a figure no deck will print (→ I46).
- **Suit-cards** are smaller, about the size of a coin, brownish, and usually on the hands, wrists or forearms. Midwives and innkeepers who carry a Copes often wear it openly as an advertisement.
- **Use**: the Blatt warms and darkens faintly while its gift is working. Lovers notice this.
- **Removal is impossible.** A Blatt cut or burned away reappears somewhere else on the body once the wound heals. Valdauers still remember **the Flaying of 1530**, when a Kesrâni pasha skinned a captured Valdauer Trump-bearer's chest to "take" the Star, and the mark came back on the man's thigh before he died. The Star fell to his wife in Wiedenbrunn the same night.
- **Hiding**: high collars, gloves, long sleeves, and **Blattschminke**, a lead-white paste sold by the Toilette-merchants (→ I07). Plenty of suit-card bearers don't hide. Some Trump-bearers can't afford to show.

### Burden and gift
Every card gives a **Gabe** (gift) and lays on a **Last** (burden). The suit-cards' gifts are small, practical knacks, and their burdens are matching small inconveniences. The Trumps' gifts are strong enough to move armies or courts, and their burdens are strong enough to ruin a life. Bearers use a gift by will, and sometimes need touch or presence. Some gifts work by themselves.

*The full list of the 22 Trumps is in I46, and of the 48 suit-cards in I47. The summary table is in §6 below.*

## 3. The Fall

When a bearer dies, the card **falls**: *der Fall*, Catalan *la caiguda*. It goes, at once, to **"das nächste Herz,"** the nearest heart: the living person most deeply tied to the bearer at the moment of death.

What counts as a tie has been argued about for six hundred years. The **Fall-Reckoners** (→ I48), the guild that makes its living predicting falls, use a scoring system everyone at court knows by heart:

| Tie | Score |
|---|---|
| **Blood**: parent, child, full sibling | 3 |
| **Blood**: half-sibling, grandparent, grandchild | 2 |
| **Blood**: uncle, aunt, niece, nephew, first cousin | 1 |
| **Bed**: spouse or lover of years, sharing a bed | 3 |
| **Bed**: lover of months, regularly | 2 |
| **Bed**: occasional lover | 1 |
| **Bed**: a single night within the last moon | 1 (otherwise 0) |
| **Milk**: milk-mother, milk-child or milk-sibling | 1½ ("milk counts half") |
| **Presence**: physically close at the moment of death | breaks ties |

Scores **add**. A sister who is also a lover scores 6, which is almost certain. A wife of twenty years scores 3, and so does a son. Who gets it then depends on who is holding the dying man's hand.

If nobody alive has any tie at all, the card makes a **Wildfall**: it goes to the nearest living person in the room or the street. Wildfalls are how slaves, servants, nurses and passing strangers have become bearers. A person who receives one is a **Wildfang**, a word that also means a tomboy, a wild child, a creature caught in the woods.

### The rule is a model, and the heart is the rule
The Reckoners know their table is an approximation. The real rule is *felt* closeness, which no one can measure. By the guild's own records about **six falls in seven** land where the reckoning predicts. The seventh lands somewhere else: on a valet instead of a son, on a sister instead of a wife, on a bath-maid instead of an heiress (→ I72). Every such surprise reveals something somebody had been hiding. That gives the court its most quoted proverb:

> **"Ein Fall ist der einzige Tratsch, der nicht lügen kann."**
> *A fall is the only gossip that can't lie.*

The folk version is **"Herz schlägt Blut"**: the heart beats blood. A beloved servant can outweigh a cold son.

### The unripe
**No card settles on anyone who has not finished growing.** No Blatt has ever been recorded on a child, not once in six hundred years of Rocalban notaries. If a bearer's nearest heart is a child, the card passes over and falls to the next. The Church explains this with the parable of the Feast: *"Children do not sit at the wedding table; the gifts are for the guests."* Practically, it means a bearer's heir must be grown before the bearer dies, or the card goes elsewhere. Every great house's plans run on that fact.

### Doubled cards
One person can carry two cards. It is called a **Doppelblatt** and is rare, heavy and unenviable, because the bearer carries both burdens. **King Ferdinand II** carried the Sun from his accession and the Empress from the **Milk Fall of 1626** until his death in 1648. He was known as *the Twice-Marked*, and his portraits show a man who never slept. At his death the Sun fell to his son and the Empress to his Holdegg mistress, who was holding his hand while his son was at war (→ I39, I42).

## 4. The Umlegen (laying-over)

A living bearer can pass a card on deliberately. This is **das Umlegen**, the laying-over (Catalan *el traspàs*).

- The giver and the receiver must lie **skin to skin from dusk to dawn**. The giver must **will** the passing steadily through the night, and both must be awake at sunrise. At dawn the Blatt has crossed from one body to the other.
- **The receiver need not want it.** Only the giver's will matters. A person can be made a bearer against their wishes, if someone can be got into their bed for a night, and has been, more than once.
- **The giver cannot be forced.** No threat, tincture or Trump makes an unwilling bearer pass a card. A bearer can, of course, be persuaded, blackmailed or worn down until they decide to.
- **A tie is required.** The receiver must have *some* tie of blood, bed or milk to the giver (by the Reckoners' count, at least 2). A stranger can't be laid over, though one night in bed beforehand is enough to make a stranger a lover. People have noticed this too.
- **The cost**: the giver is **leergelegt**, "laid empty." They are weak, cold, sleepless and flat for a season, as if part of them has been unpacked.

Aging bearers use the Umlegen to choose an heir rather than leave it to the reckoning. The **Abbesses of Montalba** have used it for three hundred years (→ I46, I54). It is also the plot of half the romances in the Rosengrund lending-stalls (→ I12).

## 5. Why the Cards push toward incest

The Trumps are the strongest supernatural force in the world, they pass by the heart, and the heart is unruly. A great house that holds a Trump wants three things: to keep it, to know who will get it, and to make sure that person is *one of us*. Blood and bed together score 6. Nothing else comes close. So:

- **Heirs are kept in their bearers' beds.** A Trump-house's chosen heir is very often the bearer's sibling, cousin or child (grown, see §3), and very often also the bearer's lover. Houses that do this don't advertise it, and everyone knows.
- **Houses register what they can.** A bond recorded in the **Left Hand register** (→ I56) proves a bed-tie in the Reckoners' books. Sapphic bonds between women of a house count, sterile as they are. That is how matriarchal houses like Ilsenau keep cards among their women without involving men at all (→ R12).
- **Houses police their bearers' beds.** A Trump-bearer's lovers outside the family are a threat to the house's card. Every Trump-house keeps a **Bettbuch** (bed-book) of the bearer's partners, so its own reckoners can run the numbers. Servants and slaves who share a bearer's bed are watched, bought off, sent away, or kept very close (→ R07).
- **The Sterbewache** (death-watch): when a bearer is dying, the chosen heir lies beside them in the bed, skin to skin, to have presence on their side. The room is cleared of everyone who might be a dangerous heart. Fall-Reckoners wait in the antechamber with their books (→ I18, I48).

What pushes the other way:
- **Brittleness.** Blood and bed every generation produce Glass Princes (→ R03, I75).
- **The heart itself.** A bearer forced into bed with a sibling they don't love may still lose the card to a footman they do.
- **Milk.** Houses that nurse each other's children make half-kin of strangers (→ I57).
- **Wildfalls**, which happen when a house is too careful and the bearer dies loving no one.

The result is that **the great houses that hold Trumps are the most inbred, the most sexually surveilled and the most anxious families in the realm.** Houses without Trumps can afford to marry for money, looks or love.

Note the distribution. **Seven** Trumps are in great houses of Valdau and its royal family. **Seven** are abroad. **Three** are in the Church. **Three** are with commoners: a card-sharp, a shipowner and a guild master. **Two** are lost. Nobody controls the Trumps. That is the first fact of the realm's politics.

### The suit-cards
*(All forty-eight, with known bearers, in I47.)*

| Suit | Catalan | Domain | Typical bearers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oros** | coins | value, weight, money, trade, assay | bankers, mint-masters, merchants, assayers |
| **Copes** | cups | house, hospitality, drink, birth, small healing | innkeepers, midwives, cooks, wet-nurses, housewives |
| **Espases** | swords | war, cutting, command, judgement | officers, fencing masters, judges, executioners |
| **Bastos** | batons | labor, land, growth, beasts, endurance | farmers, carters, miners, foresters |

A suit-card's power is local and useful. A **Cavall de Bastos** keeps every horse in his train from tiring, which is worth a fortune to a carter and to an army's baggage. A **Rei d'Espases** commands a regiment that does not break, so colonels have married ugly widows to get one. A **Sota de Copes** knows a woman is pregnant before she does, and is the reason three of Wiedenbrunn's sworn midwives are rich. The **As d'Oros** can assay any metal by touch, and the Hofprobieramt has been trying to recruit its bearer for eleven years. Pips are smaller knacks: wine that won't turn, a blade that won't dull, never miscounting a purse.

About a third of the suit-cards are in noble hands, a third with the Bürger, and a third with peasants, slaves and people nobody has bothered to record.

### Why there is no Queen
The baralla has no Queen. The Rocalban deck goes Sota, Cavall, Rei. It has always been so, because there have only ever been three figures in each suit of the marks. Valdau's French-suited Tarock decks *do* print a Dame in each suit, but only for play; no living person has ever carried one. Coffeehouse wits in matriarchal Wiedenbrunn find this hilarious. The Confraria de l'Hereu finds it theologically significant (→ I58). Card-sharps have been selling forged "Dame" Blätter to gullible collectors for a century.

## 7. Cards and the law

- **Cards cannot be bought or sold.** They are not property and cannot be inherited by will, pledged or taxed. The **Pfandamt** will not register a contract that mentions one (→ I61).
- **Bearers can be bought and sold**, with one great exception. By the **Dorl-Patent of 1412**, issued after the Bath-Slave Empress, **no bearer of a Trump may be held in bondage in Valdau.** A pledge or slave who receives a Trump is free from the moment the Blatt shows, and their owner is compensated by the Hofkammer at a fixed rate that has not been raised since 1412 (→ R07, I72). Suit-cards do *not* free anyone. A slave with a suit-card simply becomes a more valuable slave.
- **Rocalba** has no Dorl-Patent. A Trump-bearing slave there would have to be freed by the Corts or bought free by somebody. This has happened twice.
- **Registration**: Rocalban notaries keep **El Llibre de les Cartes**, the book of every suit-card bearer they can find (→ I47). Valdau's Fall-Reckoners keep their own books, and the Diarium prints Trump falls as news (→ I12).
- **Abroad**: Kesrân treats Trump-bearers among its slaves as the Padishah's property by right. Vellora rents them. The Sworn Valleys pretend the cards don't exist, while employing a Trump-bearing captain.

## 8. Commoners and the Cards

The Trumps are far above ordinary people, as far as a comet. The suit-cards are next door.

- Every village knows if one of its people carries a card. **The Kartenbauer** whose Rei de Bastos means his fields never fail is the richest man in the parish and the most courted. **The midwife** with a Sota de Copes is called to every lying-in for three villages around. **The tavern-keeper** with a Tres de Copes, whose wine never turns, has a sign with the card painted on it.
- Villagers think hard about who a bearer sleeps with, because when the old Kartenbauer dies, the card could go to a widow from outside, a hired man, or the girl he's been visiting behind the mill. Village courtship near a card-bearer is a very serious matter. The **Straw Court** has rough songs about women who chased a card-bearer's bed (→ I60).
- **Wildfalls** are the commoner's lottery. Stories of a bath-maid who became an Empress, a stable-boy who became a Chariot or a whore who became a Star are told in every inn. Most are lies. A few are true, and those are the stories people love.
- **Birthmarks** are inspected for resemblance to Blätter, in the folk belief that a pregnant woman who stares at a bearer marks her child (→ I13). A child's birthmark never is a Blatt (see §3), but that doesn't stop the scams.

## 9. What the Cards cannot do

- They cannot be **made**, **destroyed**, **split** or **merged**. There have always been seventy.
- They cannot **settle on the unripe** (§3) or on the dead.
- They cannot be **laid over without the giver's will**, or to someone without a tie (§4).
- They cannot be **stopped from falling**. No tincture, prayer or Trump holds a card in a dead body. The Sküs only protects its bearer from other gifts; it doesn't hold its own card.
- They cannot be **predicted perfectly**, only reckoned.
- A suit-card's gift cannot be scaled up. A Rei d'Espases makes a regiment steady, not an army.

## 10. Divination and play

- **Tarock** (the Austrian-style game with 22 trumps) and the Rocalban **baralla** (48 suit-cards) are the realm's card games. Both are pictures of the marks. Coffeehouse etiquette forbids playing Tarock against a known Trump-bearer. The Pagat-Franzl has been banned from every table in Wiedenbrunn since his Blatt appeared in 1698 (→ I67).
- **Kartenlegen**, card-laying, is fortune-telling with the same decks. It is done in salons and at fairs by **Kartenlegerinnen** (→ I67). It is only as reliable as the reader, which is to say it is weather-forecasting at best. Nobody governs by it, but everyone consults it.
- **Fallraten** ("fall-guessing") is a parlor game in which players bet on who would get a famous person's card if they died tonight. It is tasteless, fashionable and occasionally treasonous (→ I09).

## 11. Sayings and slang

- **"Er hat ein Blatt"**: he's a bearer. Also: he has something you don't.
- **"Gezeichnet"**: marked.
- **"Gefallen"**: fallen, of a card. *"Der Turm ist gefallen"*: the Tower has passed.
- **"Ausgespielt"**: played out; dead, of a bearer.
- **"Fallbrut"**: fall-bait. A lover kept, or suspected of being kept, to catch a card.
- **"Wildfang"**: someone who got a card by Wildfall; a wild girl; a tomboy.
- **"Ein Sküs"**: someone untouchable, someone no rule binds.
- **"Pagat ultimo"**: the lowest trump taking the last trick; an underdog's late triumph.
- **"Herz schlägt Blut"**: love beats kinship.
- **"Ein Fall ist der einzige Tratsch, der nicht lügen kann."**
- **"Leergelegt"**: laid empty, exhausted, after an Umlegen or any great giving-up.

## HOOKS

1. **The reckoning.** The Fall-Reckoners publish a new reckoning of the Moon, Ilsenau's Trump. For the first time in decades it names someone outside the house's women as a possible nearest heart, and the house wants to know who has been in the bearer's bed.
2. **A Wildfang at the door.** A Weißach washerwoman comes to the Palais Ilsenau's back gate with a fresh Blatt on her forearm, the Cavall de Copes, which fell to her when an old innkeeper died in the street beside her. She has no idea what it means. She has heard Ilsenau "knows about these things," and she is already being followed by a man from the Reckoners.
3. **A night to cross.** An aging suit-card bearer in the household (a midwife, a cook, a steward) has decided to lay her card over before she dies. The rite needs a night skin to skin and a tie of at least 2, and she has chosen the person she means it for without asking them.
4. **The lost Empress.** The Reckoners' current best guess for the Empress, lost since 1693, is "a person of the late bearer's household unknown to her family." The Holdegg dowager has engaged someone to find them, quietly. That person needs entry to every bath-house, lying-in room and servants' hall in Wiedenbrunn, and asks the Ilsenau, whose nurses go everywhere, for help.
5. **Children do not sit at the table.** A Trump-bearer in a minor house is dying, and the only person who truly loves them is a growing child. The card will pass over the child to someone else, and the family is trying to work out in days who that will be.
6. **The Sterbewache.** An invitation comes, phrased with the utmost delicacy, for a member of the Ilsenau household to sit the death-watch in an allied house: to be in the room, but on the right side of the bed. Accepting means being counted in someone else's reckoning.
