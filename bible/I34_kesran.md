# I34 · Kesrân

*The Persian-Turkish-flavored empire beyond the river: a Padishah who bears the Emperor and keeps his brothers in a garden; a women's palace where slave-girls become mothers of the throne; slave-soldiers who become viziers; a faith of the Lamp that reads the stars as God's handwriting; coffee, silk, baths, poetry and dancers; the old enemy that besieged Wiedenbrunn twice, and the embassy that now visits the royal elephant on Venustag afternoons.*

## 1. The empire

**Kesrân** (in its own tongue *Devlet-i Kesrân*, "the State of Kesrân") is a great empire stretching **southeast from the Velika** across mountains, plains, deserts and seas, ruling dozens of peoples. Its name comes from its founder-dynasty's ancestor, the legendary king **Kesra**.
- **Daryabad** ("city of the sea"): the capital, on a **strait** between two seas, a city on two shores joined by ferries and bridges of boats. Perhaps **400,000** people. It has domed Lamp-houses with lamp-towers, the **Palace of the Gate** on its headland, bazaars roofed for a mile, hammams, gardens, cypress cemeteries, and a harbor full of every ship in the world. It was taken in **1453** from an older empire, whose name in Valdau is **Thessara**, after a siege the Kesrâni still sing about.
- **The frontier provinces** beyond the Velika: governed by **beys** who command the **Wind-riders**, the light-horse raiders who cross the river by night (→ I28).
- **The lost province**: southern **Ormánd**, held 1541–1686 and lost in the reconquest after the Second Siege (→ I33). Kesrân has not forgotten it.

---

## 2. Names, titles and speech

- **Names**: a given name and a father's name, or a family epithet: *Murad Kayhanzade* (Murad, son of Kayhan), *Şirin Hanım* (Lady Şirin). Persian and Turkish names mix.
- **Titles**:
  - *Padişah* (emperor);
  - *Sultan* (prince or princess of the blood);
  - *Pasha* (governor, general, vizier);
  - *Bey* (lord, commander);
  - *Agha* (officer, steward);
  - *Efendi* (gentleman, scholar);
  - *Hanım* (lady);
  - *Kadın* (a palace lady).
- **The greeting**: the **three-touch salute**, hand to heart, lips and brow (→ I01).
- **The language**: Kesrâni, a court tongue full of Persian poetry and Arabic piety. The embassy's interpreters, the **dragomans**, are Velloran-born and paid by everyone.

---

## 3. The Padishah and the Emperor

- **The Padishah**: **Bahram IV**, about forty, a capable, cautious, melancholic ruler, poet and astronomer. He has reigned since **1687**, when his elder brother was deposed by the army after the disasters of the Siege and the reconquest.
- **He bears Trump IV, Der Kaiser** (Kesrâni *Kayser*, the Emperor), which has stayed in the dynasty since 1453 (→ I46).
  - **Gift**: all who have **sworn fealty** to him feel his commands as **compulsion**. They can resist only with pain, and armies under his eye **do not rout**.
  - **Burden**: **his own word binds him**. A command he gives, or a promise he makes, he cannot break without sickening. So Padishahs who bear the Emperor speak very little and very carefully. Bahram IV is famous for his silences.
- **Keeping the Emperor**: a card falls to the nearest heart (→ R04). A dynasty that wants it to fall to the **heir** must keep **every other heart** away from the Padishah. Hence:
  - **The Garden of Princes** (*Şehzade Bahçesi*). Since **1603**, when the old law of royal fratricide was abolished, the Padishah's **brothers** and his **non-heir sons** have lived in a walled garden-palace within the Palace of the Gate. They are comfortable, educated, given women, and **never allowed into the Padishah's presence**. A brother he never sees can never become his nearest heart. Some princes spend forty years in the Garden and come out to reign, mad. The women given to Garden princes are dosed with Kesrâni silver-water so that no rival lines are born. A prince who fathers a child in the Garden is a crisis of state.
  - **The heir-designate** (*veliahd*) is raised in the Padishah's presence, shares his rooms, rides with him and **sits his death-watch**. The dynasty's rule is the opposite of Valdau's: **keep the heart small**, rather than keep it in the family's bed.
  - **The Empress-bearer**: in **1580** the dynasty's rule failed. The dying Padishah's Emperor fell not to his heir but to his **haseki** (chief favorite), **Gülşah**, a slave-girl from the Ormándi border who had been his beloved for fifteen years. She was freed by the card's law in Kesrân, which frees a bearer as in Valdau. She **ruled** for nine years as **Kayser Gülşah**, the only woman to bear the Emperor, then **laid it over** (by the Umlegen) to her son on his eighteenth birthday. She is the most famous woman in Kesrâni history, and the Andarun's patron saint in all but name.

## 4. Government: the Gate and the Divan

- **The Gate** (*Kapı*): the Palace of the Gate in Daryabad is the government. The Valdauer name for the whole Kesrâni state, **"die Hohe Pforte"** (the High Gate), comes from it.
- **The Divan**: the imperial council, meeting four days a week in the Divan-hall under a grilled window behind which the Padishah may or may not be listening. It is led by the **Grand Vizier**, now **Mehmed Nihani Pasha**: a son of the Gate (see below), Ormándi-born, taken as a boy in a raid, now the second man in the empire. He is the architect of the Willow Bridge peace, which he means to renew.
- **The Sons of the Gate** (*Kapıkulları*, "slaves of the Gate"): Kesrân's great institution. Captives, war-children and tribute-youths are taken into the **Inner School** of the palace, raised, educated, converted to the Lamp, trained in arms, letters, music and law, and become:
  - the **Kesrâni infantry** (the Gate's foot-soldiers, the backbone of the army, with their terrifying **mehter** band, → I11);
  - **the palace officials**;
  - **governors**;
  - and **viziers**.

  They are **slaves of the Padishah**, legally owned, and they rule the empire. Half the Grand Viziers of the last century were born **Wedding**-faithful in Valdau, Ormánd or Rocalba, and taken as children. This is the thing Valdauers find hardest to understand about Kesrâni slavery: it **rises**.
- **The Mother of the Throne** (*Taht Anası*): the reigning Padishah's mother, the most powerful woman in the empire. She rules the Andarun (§5), controls the palace purse, counsels her son, and negotiates with ambassadors through a screen. She is often a **former slave-girl**. The present Mother of the Throne, **Mihrimâh Taht Anası**, a Rocalban captive taken as a girl from a coastal village in 1661, is in her fifties. Her Catalan, which she still speaks, is the scandal and delight of the Rocalban merchants in Daryabad.
- **The Chief Reckoner of Hours** (*Müneccimbaşı*): the imperial astrologer, who sits in the Divan, sets the hour of every campaign, treaty, coronation and wedding, and keeps tables better than the Stundenamt's (→ R02). He timed the **Second Siege** to the Great Conjunction of 1683, and it failed. His successor is timing the **Willow Bridge** renewal, and the **1702** Conjunction (→ I51).

## 5. The Andarun (the women's palace)

**The Andarun** ("the inside") is the women's palace within the Palace of the Gate: hundreds of women, their servants, children, teachers, guards, courts, gardens, baths and politics.
- **The women** are mostly **slaves**, bought, taken or given as tribute from every border of the empire. They are educated in the Andarun's schools in music, poetry, embroidery, letters, the Lamp's faith, and the arts of pleasing, and rise by rank:
  - **şagird** (apprentice);
  - **gözde** ("in the eye," noticed by the Padishah);
  - **ikbal** ("fortunate," taken to his bed);
  - **kadın** (lady, mother of a prince);
  - **haseki** (chief favorite);
  - and finally **Taht Anası**, Mother of the Throne, if her son reigns.
- **A concubine who bears the Padishah's child** is freed at his death and cannot be sold. This is the **umm-i veled** law, which Valdau's **Muttermagd** custom copied in 1412 (→ R07).
- **Women who are not chosen** are married off after nine years to officers and officials with a dowry, as free women. Being an Andarun woman is the best credential a bride can have in Daryabad.
- **The mihrimâh guards**: since **Kayser Gülşah**, the Andarun's guards are **futas** (*mihrimâh*, "sun-and-moon," → R10). They are not men, so they may enter, and are armed, trained, and intensely loyal to the Mother of the Throne. Valdauer ladies find the idea exciting, and Valdauer husbands don't.
- **Friendships of the Andarun**: the women's palace is famous in Kesrâni poetry for its passionate friendships between women, its jealousies and alliances, and its baths (→ R09).
- **Politics**: the Andarun is a court within the court. Its women's sons are princes, so their rivalries are the empire's succession. Ambassadors send gifts to the Mother of the Throne before the Grand Vizier.

## 6. The Faith of the Lamp

- **The One Light**: a single God, unseen, whose image is light. The **Lamp** is the faith's emblem: one flame in a glass, hung in every Lamp-house and home.
- **The stars are God's lamps**, and the sky is God's handwriting, so **astrology is pious**: reading the sky is reading scripture (→ R02).
- **The Lamp-keepers** are the clergy and scholars. The **Chief Lamp-keeper** (*Şeyhülkandil*) issues rulings.
- **On the Wedding faith**: the Lamp regards the Wedding's divine sibling-marriage as **idolatry and abomination**. Brothers and sisters wedding, and gods with bodies! Kesrâni preachers mock it in every sermon on Valdau. Yet the Lamp tolerates Wedding-faithful subjects as **protected peoples** who pay a tax and keep their churches.
- **Kesrâni captives in Valdau** keep the Lamp in **Lamp-houses** in Wiedenbrunn's Kesrâner Viertel, in the Galera of Cinabre, and on Grenzer farms (→ I22, I30, I28).

## 7. Customs and desire

- **The hammam**: steam-baths in every quarter of every city, domed and marble. Men's hours and women's hours, scrubbing, massage, gossip, coffee, poetry, assignations. The **women's hammam** is where marriages are arranged and brides inspected. Wiedenbrunn's single hammam in the Kesrâner Viertel is the most fashionable bath in the city (→ I64).
- **The coffeehouse** (*kahvehane*): **Kesrân invented it**. Men sit for hours over tiny cups of thick coffee, backgammon, storytellers, shadow-puppets and politics. Wiedenbrunn's coffeehouses are its children (→ I67).
- **Poetry**: the **gazel**, the love-lyric of the beloved: *the cupbearer*, *the beloved's down on the cheek*, *the lips like wine*, *the eyebrow like a bow*. The beloved in Kesrâni poetry is as often a young man as a woman, and the court's poets write openly of both. The law is ambiguous and society is tolerant, as long as no one is shamed.
- **The zenne**: **male dancers in women's dress**, with castanets, swaying and whirling to drum and flute. They are a celebrated profession with their own coffeehouses, patrons, poets and rivalries (→ R11). The embassy's zenne dance in Wiedenbrunn's salons for enormous fees, and great ladies quarrel over them.
- **Marriage**: a contract signed before witnesses, a dowry paid to the bride (which is hers), the **henna night** when the bride's women paint her hands and sing her farewell, and a procession on horseback (→ I17). **Concubinage** is legal for any man who can afford it. Divorce is easy for men and possible for women.
- **Kinship**: **cousin marriage** is ordinary, even preferred. **Sibling** union is an abomination (→ R06).
- **Dress**: kaftans, sashes, turbans for men, veils and trousers for women, silk and fur by rank (→ I06).
- **Food**: rice, lamb, yogurt, pastries in honey, sherbet, rosewater, coffee, and **paprika**, which the Kesrâni brought to Ormánd (→ I33).

## 8. Slavery

**Everywhere, and upward** (→ R07):
- the **Sons of the Gate** (§4);
- the **Andarun** (§5);
- **household slaves** in every prosperous home;
- **galley-slaves**;
- **field-slaves** on the great estates;
- and a market in **Daryabad's slave-bazaar**, the largest in the world, where captives from every border are sold under the eye of a market-judge.

**Manumission is a pious act**: freeing a slave after **seven years** of good service is customary, and many slaves sign a **mukâtebe**, a contract to buy their own freedom by installments.

Kesrâni find Valdauer bondage **stingy**: they keep slaves but won't let them rise. *"In Valdau a slave dies a slave; in Kesrân he may die a vizier."*

## 9. Trade and stereotypes

- **Exports**:
  - **coffee**;
  - **silk** and **carpets**;
  - **Daryabad steel** (watered blades);
  - **rosewater** and **attar**;
  - **sugar**; spices re-exported from the far east; **mohair**; **tulips** (bulbs, a craze among Wiedenbrunn's gardeners);
  - **opium** (*afyon*, a Saturn drug, → R03);
  - astrolabes, books, and an elephant.
- **Imports**: Valdauer iron, cannon (in peacetime, by way of Vellora), Velloran glass, Rocalban coral, furs, and captives.
- **What Valdauers say of Kesrâni**: cruel, sensual, luxurious, idle, deceitful, magnificent. The bogeyman of every nursery: *"the Kesrâner will take you."* And they drink the coffee and wear the kaftans.
- **What Kesrâni say of Valdauers**: pale, pig-eating, sister-marrying idolaters who keep slaves but won't let them rise. Stingy even in tyranny. Brave, though, and good gunfounders.

---

## 10. Relations with the Double Crown

- **The old enemy**: two sieges of Wiedenbrunn, 1529 and **1683**; a century and a half of war on the frontier; the reconquest of Ormánd (→ I76, I33).
- **The Peace of the Willow Bridge** (1684), the twenty-year truce, **expires 1704**. Kesrân's Grand Vizier wants it renewed, for now. The border-beys want raids. The Padishah has said nothing, because whatever he says binds him (→ I28).
- **The embassy** in Wiedenbrunn's Kesrâner Viertel: a walled garden-house with a fountain, a hammam, coffee, a zenne troupe and a Reckoner of Hours.
  - **The new ambassador**, **Nihani Efendi**, the Grand Vizier's nephew, arrived in 1698. He is elegant, silent, cultivated and unreadable, a poet in three languages. He has so far said nothing whatsoever, beautifully.
  - **Every Venustag afternoon** he visits **Selim the elephant** in the Hirschau menagerie, a gift of the Padishah with the peace of 1684 (→ I10, I22), and feeds him oranges.
- **Coffee** and the **Kaffeepartei**: Wiedenbrunn's coffee trade depends on the peace (→ I05).

## HOOKS

1. **Selim's visitor.** On a Venustag afternoon, the Kesrâni ambassador is at the elephant's enclosure when the protagonist visits. They are alone except for the elephant and a dragoman. The ambassador speaks for the first time anyone has heard: a single line of Kesrâni poetry about a moon that slept, translated with care.
2. **The mihrimâh.** A mihrimâh guard of the embassy's women's quarters, a futa in Kesrâni armor, has been ordered to deliver a sealed message from the Mother of the Throne to the Obersthofmeisterin of Ilsenau, by hand, to her alone. She has never been in a Valdauer house, and insists on doing it at night.
3. **The zenne's patron.** The embassy's lead zenne asks the protagonist, through a fan-language he learned in Wiedenbrunn, to become his patron in the city, which in Kesrâni custom is a public honor and a private arrangement. Two great ladies who have been competing for the zenne's evenings will take it badly.
4. **A Son of the Gate.** Among the embassy's secretaries is a man who was born in a Weißach mining village, taken by Wind-riders as a child, raised in the Inner School, and now serves the Lamp and the Gate. He remembers the Ilsenau's white castle on the lake, and wants, very quietly, to see it again, and his mother if she lives.
5. **The Garden.** A prince of the Garden, a brother of the Padishah who has lived thirty years in confinement, has escaped Daryabad and reached Valdau, and asks for asylum at the Palais Ilsenau. If the Padishah dies, he is a claimant. If he's returned, he'll never see daylight again. If he stays, the truce may break.
6. **The 1702 hour.** The embassy's Reckoner of Hours, meeting the Stundenamt's Hofastronom over coffee, asks casually for the nativity of "the Ilsenau son, born 1680." The Hofastronom refuses. Three days later the Stundenamt's archive is broken into.
