# I03 · The Palais & its Hours

*The shape of a great house in Wiedenbrunn and how a day passes inside it: the row of doors that measures rank, the corridors behind the walls, where people wash and relieve themselves, how the house is lit and heated, who sleeps where, and the written Hausordnung that governs it all.*

## 1. The shape of a Palais

Wiedenbrunn's great houses are built on cramped medieval plots inside the old walls. They are tall, deep and narrow at the street, with the magnificence inside. A typical Palais from the street inward:

- **The Portal**: a carved stone gateway with the house's arms over it. The **Portier** sits in his lodge beside it in livery with a staff and bandolier. He has heard everything and is paid to have forgotten it.
- **The Durchfahrt**: the carriage passage through the front wing into the courtyard, cobbled and echoing. Lovers say goodbye in the shadows of the Durchfahrt.
- **The Hof**: the courtyard, with a well, a linden tree or two, stables at the back, and the kitchen windows giving onto it.
- **The Feststiege**: the grand staircase, stone, with statues, for guests. The family uses it on show days.
- **The Beletage** ("beautiful floor"): the first floor, where the state rooms are.
- **The Mezzanin**: a low half-floor above or below the Beletage, for the family's private rooms, secretaries and upper servants.
- **The upper floors**: children's rooms, guest rooms, the Stiftsjahr girls in houses that keep them (→ I19), and in the attic, servants and pledges.
- **The cellars**: wine, ice, coal, and in alchemical houses the laboratory (→ R03).
- **The Dienerstiege**: the servants' stair, narrow and dark, running from cellar to attic behind everything. Servants are never supposed to be seen on the Feststiege. Gentlemen are never supposed to be seen on the Dienerstiege, which is why it is so useful.

### The Enfilade
The state rooms of the Beletage run in a line, each door aligned with the next, so that from the first room you can see through every doorway to the last, a vista of gilded doorframes, mirrors and parquet. This is the **Enfilade**, and it is **a map of rank**:

1. **The Vorsaal**: antechamber, for waiting.
2. **The Audienzzimmer**: audience room, for formal calls.
3. **The Salon** and **Spiegelsaal** (mirror hall), for the Jour, music and cards.
4. **The Paradeschlafzimmer**: the state bedroom, with the **Paradebett**, the great canopied bed of state. It is for show: lyings-in, deathbeds, receiving on one's name-day and the Beilager of high marriages. Almost nobody sleeps in it.
5. **The Kabinett**: the small, private, beautiful room at the very end. Here the lady writes letters, receives intimates, and does whatever else is done in a Kabinett.

**How far down the Enfilade you are received is how much you matter.** A tradesman stops in the Vorsaal and a petitioner in the Audienzzimmer. Friends go into the Salon. Being taken into the Kabinett is a declaration. Since every door is aligned, everyone in the earlier rooms can see exactly how far you got.

Privacy is impossible in an Enfilade, because every room passes into the next. Behind it, unseen, are the rooms where people actually live.

### Behind the walls
- **The Heizgang** (stove corridor): Wiedenbrunn palaces are heated by great **tiled stoves** (*Kachelöfen*), one per room, white or painted. They are fed **from behind the wall**, through a little iron door in a narrow corridor that runs behind the rooms, so that servants carrying wood and ash never enter the state rooms.
  - The stove-boys, the **Ofenheizer**, spend the winter in the Heizgang, inches from every conversation in the house.
  - They are the best-informed people in Wiedenbrunn and the cheapest to bribe (→ I04, I20).
  - Lovers have used the Heizgang to cross a house unseen. Wives have used it to listen.
- **The Garderobe**: the dressing-room beside each bedroom. Clothes, a washstand, and the pallet where the Kammerfrau or Bettmagd sleeps.
- **The Retirade**: the privy closet, usually tucked behind the Garderobe. It holds the **Leibstuhl** (close-stool), a padded chair with a lid over a pewter or porcelain pot. In the newest houses there is a seat over a shaft that drains to a cesspit, emptied at night by the **Senkgrubenräumer** (cesspit-men), whose cart nobody mentions. Pots are emptied by the **Leibstuhlmagd**, the humblest maid in the house, who knows more about the family's health than its physician.
- **The Alkoven**: the family's real beds are in alcoves off the private rooms. They are curtained, warm and small, unlike the Paradebett. *"Im Alkoven"* means in bed, privately.
- **The private stair**: many great houses have a narrow stair from the Kabinett or the master's room down to a side door onto the alley. Its key is the **Nachtschlüssel**, the night-key (→ I02).

## 2. Light, heat, water and air

- **Light**: **beeswax candles** for the family and the state rooms, clean-burning and sweet-smelling and expensive. **Tallow** (*Unschlitt*) for the servants, smoky and stinking of mutton. The class line is so plain that Wiedenbrunn says **Wachsleute** and **Unschlittleute**, wax-people and tallow-people, for the rich and the poor.
  - **Spiegelleuchter** (mirror-sconces) with Velloran glass double the light in the Salon.
  - The **Nachtlicht** is a wick floating in oil in a glass, left burning all night in bedrooms and nurseries.
  - Since the **Laternenordnung of 1688**, Wiedenbrunn's inner streets have oil lanterns on iron brackets, lit at dusk by the **Laternanzünder** and out by midnight. The **Laternenbub**, a link-boy, can be hired to light you home (→ I21).
- **Heat**: the Kachelöfen, which hold heat all night. French-style fireplaces are the new fashion in the newest houses, prettier and colder. There are bed-warming pans (**Bettwärmer**) of brass with long handles, and a **Fußsack**, a fur foot-muff, in carriages and church.
- **Water**: carried up from the courtyard well, or bought from the **Wasserer** with his cart and barrels. Washing is done at the washstand with a **Lavoir**, a basin and ewer (the Ilsenau ewer, → R12). A full bath means a wooden tub carried into the bedroom, lined with linen sheets against splinters, and filled by four maids with buckets heated in the kitchen. A great house may have its own **Badstube**, a bath-room with a stove and a copper. Otherwise one goes to the Bader (→ I64).
- **Air**: Wiedenbrunn smells of horses, coffee, woodsmoke, the Selde, tallow, incense and cesspits. Great houses burn **Räucherkerzen** (incense cones) and keep pots of dried rose and lavender. Ladies carry pomanders. In summer the court flees to the brine-air of Bad Sulzau.

## 3. The hours of a great day

*A day in a great house in the Wiedenbrunn season, from the Hausordnung of a typical great household, with the exceptions everyone makes.*

| Hour (equal hours, by the Dom clock) | What happens |
|---|---|
| **5–6** | The servants rise. The Ofenheizer light the stoves from the Heizgang. Kitchen fires. Water carried. The Portier opens the gate. |
| **7** | Upper servants' breakfast. The Haushofmeister reviews the day. |
| **8–9** | The family wakes, or doesn't. **Bettfrühstück**: **chocolate** (Almagran fashion, → I05) or **coffee** brought to bed on a tray by the Kammerfrau or the Bettmagd, with Biskotten. Letters are read in bed. |
| **10–12** | **The Toilette** (→ I07): the lady, or the gentleman, is washed, dressed, combed, powdered and patched, *while receiving*. Intimates, hairdressers, tradesmen, musicians, the house Zeuge and the Toilettenkavalier come and go. |
| **11** | **Gabelfrühstück** ("fork-breakfast"): a hot snack of sausages, a ragout, Gulasch, a glass of wine, taken standing by men of business. |
| **12–2** | Business: correspondence; the Kronrat or the offices; audiences; Mass for the pious; shopping on the Graben. The Corso drive in the Hirschau in fine weather. |
| **2–3** | **Die Mittagstafel**: the main meal of the day. At a great house, six to twelve courses in two or three "services" (→ X03), with guests, served by footmen behind each chair. The seating is by precedence (→ I69). It lasts a long time. |
| **3–5** | **Visiting hours** (→ I02), the Jour, calls returned. |
| **5** | **Die Jause**: coffee or chocolate with cakes and whipped cream. The most companionable hour of the day. |
| **6–9** | The **theatre** or the **Hofoper** (→ I68); in summer the promenade on the Bastei; card parties; music; in Fasching the first balls. |
| **9–10** | **Das Nachtmahl**: supper, lighter than the Mittagstafel. Cold meats, a soup, sweets. |
| **10–2** | Cards (Tarock and Ombre till dawn, → I67, I09), balls in Fasching, assignations the rest of the year. |
| **Night** | **The Coucher**, the undressing, done by the Kammerfrau or Kammerdiener. The bed is warmed. The Nachtlicht is lit. The night-servant settles on the pallet in the Garderobe or across the threshold of the antechamber: the **Vorzimmerschläfer**. The great house is never empty at night, and no bed in it is ever entirely private. |

**The bells**: the house runs on the **Dom's equal hours** for meals and calls, and on the **Stundenuhr** (planetary-hour clock, → R02) for everything that matters. A great house has a servant whose job is to set the Stundenuhr at dawn and dusk, and a **Stundenglöckner** for the bedchamber on nights when the Stundenamt has assigned an hour.

## 4. Sleeping arrangements (who is where at night)

- **Husband and wife** have separate apartments in a great house, each with its own Alkoven, Garderobe and Retirade. Visiting is by arrangement, by habit or by surprise. A husband who visits his wife's apartment is announced by her Kammerfrau. One who isn't is either very sure of his welcome or about to learn something.
- **The children** sleep on the upper floor with their nurses and governesses.
- **The body-servants** sleep at the door: the **Bettmagd** and **Kammerfrau** in the Garderobe, the **Kammerdiener** in his master's antechamber. They hear everything and are expected to.
- **Guests** sleep in the guest apartments. Their own servants sleep at their doors.
- **The Stiftsjahr girls** in a house that keeps them sleep two to a room under the eye of an older woman. They are famously hard to keep there (→ I19).
- **Spindelhäuser** keep the **women's wing** separate from the husbands' rooms, and the women's wing has its own bath (→ R12).
- **Pledges and servants**: the attic, two to six to a room, men and women on separate stairs in theory.

## 5. The Hausordnung

Every great house has a written **Hausordnung**, its house rules, framed in the servants' hall, read aloud at the start of each year by the Haushofmeister, and amended by the mistress at will. Fines are in Kupferlinge, docked from wages. A typical selection:

> *1. No servant shall pass beyond the Audienzzimmer unbidden.*
> *2. The Feststiege is for the Herrschaft and their guests. Servants use the Dienerstiege at all hours.*
> *3. The Ofenheizer shall make no sound in the Heizgang during the Jour.*
> *4. No servant shall receive a card, letter or gift for a member of the Herrschaft except through the Portier.*
> *5. The night-stair is the Fürstin's alone. Whoever is found upon it, or with its key, is dismissed without a Zeugnis.*
> *6. Tallow shall not be burned above the Mezzanin.*
> *7. The Leibstuhl shall be emptied before the eighth hour and after the Nachtmahl.*
> *8. Servants shall not speak of the Herrschaft's affairs at the Heuriger, the Bader or the Ammenhaus.*
> *9. No servant shall marry, nor take a lover, nor be taken, without the Haushofmeister's leave.*
> *10. Whoever brings Venusbitter into the servants' hall shall pay four Kupferlinge and share it.*

The Ilsenau Hausordnung has a famous clause, added in 1612: *"Whatever is heard in the Schwesternbad is not heard."* (→ R12).

## 6. Furnishings and fashions

- **Mirrors**: Velloran glass in the Spiegelsaal, the more the grander. A **Spiegelkabinett**, a small room lined with mirrors floor to ceiling, is the latest luxury. What it's for is obvious, and gossips speak of nothing else.
- **Chinoiserie and Asagiri lacquer**: screens, cabinets, porcelain, and folding fans everywhere since the embassy arrived (→ I37).
- **Kesrâni style**: the **Sofa** (a word Valdau took from Kesrân along with the furniture), cushions, carpets, coffee tables, and the **Türkenzimmer**, a "Kesrâni room" with divans, smoke and dim lamps. It is fashionable for gentlemen's smoking and for less mentionable things (→ I34).
- **The Ahnengalerie**: the family portraits, each with a small plaque giving the sitter's **karat**, the **Gehaltstafel**. Brittle ancestors' plaques carry a tiny cross, and guests count the crosses (→ R03).
- **Clocks**: the Stundenuhr; a tall case-clock by the Dom hours; little gilded table-clocks. A great house sounds like a workshop of chimes on the hour.
- **Pets**:
  - lapdogs, the **Velloraner Hündchen** (a little white curly dog);
  - parrots from the south;
  - a Kesrâni cat;
  - a canary in every Kabinett;
  - a monkey in the most fashionable houses, which bites.
- **The bed**: the Paradebett has a canopy, curtains and a coverlet embroidered with the house arms. The Alkoven bed has curtains, a feather mattress over a straw one, a bolster, and a great many cushions.

## HOOKS

1. **The Heizgang.** On his first winter evening in the Palais the protagonist finds the little iron stove-door in his own room open a crack, and a pair of eyes behind it, which belong to someone who is not a stove-boy.
2. **The Kabinett.** A lady visiting the Jour asks to be shown "the young Prinz's rooms" and is taken by mistake, or not by mistake, all the way down the Enfilade to the Kabinett. Every guest in the Salon can see exactly how far she got.
3. **Clause Five.** The Nachtschlüssel to the Fürstin's private stair is found in the pocket of the protagonist's coat. He didn't put it there, and the Haushofmeister has to enforce Clause Five on *someone*.
4. **The Vorzimmerschläfer.** The pledge who sleeps across the threshold of the protagonist's antechamber is changed three times in the first month. Each change was ordered by a different woman of the house, and each new sleeper has different instructions.
5. **The Paradebett.** By custom a son of the house newly of age, or newly returned, receives the household's congratulations on his first name-day in the state bed. The household, the Stiftsjahr girls and the Jour's ladies file through the Paradeschlafzimmer while he sits in it in a dressing-gown. It is a ceremony with very old rules, and nobody remembers the reason for half of them.
6. **The Leibstuhlmagd.** The humblest maid in the house tells the house alchemist, in confidence, something about one of the household's ladies that only the Leibstuhlmagd could know. It is the first sign that a pregnancy is running very, very fast (→ R13).
