# KÖNIGRUFEN — Setting Pitch

*Working title. Königrufen ("Calling the King") is the Tarock game every coffeehouse in the capital plays: four at the table, and the declarer names a King. Whoever holds that card becomes her secret partner, and nobody else at the table knows who it is until it's played.*

---

## The pitch in one breath

**Norica** is an empire on a great river, modeled loosely on Vienna in its waltz-and-coffeehouse years: a court that still runs on an inherited Southern ceremonial, a capital full of balls and private dining rooms, and a nobility whose blood actually does things. A century ago its ruling dynasty lost its Southern sister-crown, a Catalan/Castilian-flavored kingdom, after the last Southern king was born *curdled* from too many generations of near-kin marriage. The Southern loyalists who followed the losing claimant north still live in the capital, and they never stopped being Southern.

One of those exile houses is **Rocamora**. It's matriarchal by blood and by charter, it holds the Empire's salt, and it runs the most fashionable spa in the realm. Its women age slowly, and the house can do one thing for a woman that nothing else in Norica can: **salt her womb shut for a moon**. That makes them the quiet keepers of noblewomen's freedom, and quietly dreaded by men who want heirs.

Rocamora rarely has sons. It had one, and he fell asleep in his first winter. For nineteen years he slept in a brine-bath room at the spa while the house's women washed him, gossiped over him and told him things they told no one else. Then he woke up, grown, beautiful, with no memories. He also turned out to be **quick**: any woman he gets with child carries for nine days, and the child goes somewhere he can never follow, lives a whole childhood there, and walks back out a grown stranger within a month.

The house has to hide all of this, and it has to decide what he's for. Every woman in it already has her own idea, and very few of those ideas are about politics.

---

## Tone pillars

- **Indulgent, erotic, mostly light.** The world's default register is Viennese: ironic, gossipy, sensual, cushioned by money and custom. There's drama, but it's the drama of houses, beds, secrets and pride, not of famine and massacre. Bad things exist at the edges (the mines, the war-bonds, the abroad), but the center of the story is a rich, scandalous, affectionate household.
- **Desire first, politics second, transactions last.** Blood and fertility have real political value, and that's exactly why the house hides them. Most people who want the protagonist don't know what his seed does. The women who do know mostly wanted him before they knew. Politics makes desire dangerous. It doesn't replace it.
- **Messy and unjustified.** Nothing in this world exists to make the harem acceptable. Near-kin love is a stain that doesn't wash out, bonds are not pretty, house authority is real authority. Characters want things they shouldn't, take things they shouldn't, and don't get punished in neat proportion.
- **Low stakes, high friction.** Few things here kill you or ruin you outright. Almost everything sticks to you. Consequences are social, familial and permanent rather than legal and fatal.
- **Grounded fantastic.** The supernatural is real and heavy in war and statecraft, but it works like weather and craft, not like spectacle. Most scenes will feel it as custom, money, rank and bodies, not as flashes of light.
- **Not modern.** Nobody in Norica thinks in terms of consent, safety or rights. They think in terms of rank, bonds, blood, propriety, discretion and appetite. Pleasure under pressure, pleasure under obligation and pleasure taken are ordinary parts of the world's erotic vocabulary. The world doesn't moralize about them, and neither does the narration.

## 1. The Sky, the Art, the Cards, the Blood

One idea underneath everything: **Signatur**. The seven Wandering Stars (Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn) press their influence into the world, and everything carries their mark: metals, herbs, hours, bodies, bloodlines. That's the whole metaphysics. Everything below is what different people do with it.

### The Stars (astrology): *when*
- Everyone has a **Geburtsbild**, a nativity chart, cast at birth by a midwife-astrologer and kept like a baptismal record. Commoners follow the **Mandlkalender**, the yearly folk almanac that says when to plant, let blood, wean, wed and bed. The court keeps a **Hofastronom**, and great houses keep their own.
- Influence has **seasons**. A blood-gift waxes when its ruling planet is strong and goes thin when that planet is retrograde or "burned" by the Sun. Great houses therefore have weak windows everyone can calculate. Duels, lawsuits and seductions get timed around them.
- Astrology is also the polite language of intimacy. Courtiers say two people are *in conjunction*, a match is *well-aspected*, a scandal is *Saturn's work*. It isn't wordplay, though. People believe it, and they plan their conceptions by it.

### The Art (alchemy): *how*
- **Die Kunst** ("the Art") is a guild craft anyone can learn with enough years and money. It works matter and bodies through the three principles, **Salt** (body, fixity), **Sulphur** (passion, heat, will) and **Mercury** (spirit, change, quickness), and through the seven metals.
- It's the setting's main *non-blood* supernatural, and it's everywhere: apothecaries on every street, tinctures in every boudoir, alchemical powder in every cannon. The Guild's seat is the old crownland city of **Zlatava**, the realm's golden, smoky alchemists' town.
- Everyday products include: **Kupfertee** (copper tea, the common preventative, unreliable); **Schwefeltropfen** (sulphur drops, an aphrodisiac); **Mondmilch** (moonmilk, a cave mineral that brings on milk, used by wet nurses and by lovers who like it); **Bleiweiß** (lead-white face powder for fashionable pallor); **Rebiswasser** (an expensive draught that gives a woman a working cock for a night, very popular at carnival); and **Solvens**, the dissolvent that makes a near-kin pregnancy safer. Solvens is the most expensive bottle in the Empire, and every great house courts its few makers.
- The Guild wants what every guild wants: a monopoly, respect, and the secret of the blood-gifts, which the Art has never managed to make.

### The Cards (tarot): *who holds whom*
The cards do two different jobs.

**The Southern Deck: bonds.** Binding came north with the Southern union, so a bond is always written on a Southern card, with the suits of coins, cups, swords and clubs. A licensed **Kartenschreiber** (card-writer) draws the bond at a fitting hour, in ink cut with the bound person's blood. After that:
- **Whoever holds the card holds the bond.** The bound person feels the holder's will as a *pull*: strong up close, faint at a distance, sharp when disobeying (unease, then nausea, then pain, depending on the degree written). Their hands won't easily rise against the holder. That's all it does. **The card doesn't touch the heart.** A bound person can hate, love, want or loathe exactly as they would anyway.
- The suit gives the kind of bond: **Oros** (coins: debt and hire), **Copas** (cups: household and pleasure), **Espadas** (swords: war, punishment, soldiery), **Bastos** (clubs: labor, mines, fields). The court figures and pips give the degree.
- Cards can be sold, lent, inherited, gifted and gambled. A stolen card works *sour*: weakly, and miserably for both ends. Tearing a card is agony for the bound and a serious crime. **Burning it frees them.** The burning is a ceremony, and freedmen keep the ash.
- **Soldiers are bonds too.** A regiment's enlistment cards sit in its **card-chest**, held by the regiment's proprietor, who is usually a noble. Capturing an enemy's card-chest doesn't make their soldiers yours, but it fills every one of them with a sick pull toward your tent. Card-chests are guarded like colors. Elite units and foreign mercenaries who fight **unbound**, on oath alone, are worth their weight in gold because nobody can capture them that way.

**The Great Deck: the Pact.** Four centuries ago the Empire's twenty-one great houses bound themselves to the Crown on the twenty-one numbered Trumps of one ancient deck. The Crown holds the twenty-second card, the unnumbered **Sküs**, the Fool, which in the game beats everything.
- Each house's Trump is its patent of high nobility, its seat on the Crown Council, and its **precedence**: XX outranks XIX at every door, table and quadrille. Court ceremony literally follows trick-taking order.
- The Trumps carry the Old Images (the Empress, the Hermit, the Tower and so on), and they were handed out by the order in which houses signed, **not by fitness**. The mismatches are a centuries-old court joke: the Empire's most rigidly honest house holds the Devil, and the generals hold the Hanged Man. Houses have long since grown into their cards anyway, through heraldry, nicknames and grudges.
- **XXI is vacant.** It belonged to the dynasty's Southern Branch, and nobody has held it since the Branch died out. Coffeehouse players call XXI *der Mond*, a Viennese mangling of the court-French *le monde* ("the world"). "Who gets the Mond" is the Empire's longest-running political question.
- The lowest Trump, I, is the **Pagat**. By old privilege the Pagat house votes last in Council and breaks ties ("Pagat ultimo"), so the weakest high house is courted the hardest.
- Card-reading as divination exists. Every fairground has a **Kartenaufschlägerin** and every salon has a lady who reads. It works like weather forecasting: often right, never certain, and nobody sane governs by it.

### The Blood: *what the great are*
- The Twenty-One, and a scattering of older lesser families, carry **tinctured blood**: an inherited gift, concentrated, specific and physical. It's real in war and statecraft. A general who can't feel fear, a house that walls a fortress shut, a bloodline that can make a cannon-train's powder catch or fail.
- **Why some houses are matriarchal by blood.** The learned doctrine, old and universally believed, is that *the mother gives the matter and the father gives the form.* Gifts of the **body** (Salt-type: flesh, endurance, preservation) pass through mothers. Gifts of the **spirit or will** (Mercury- and Sulphur-type) pass through fathers. A house's line of descent follows its gift. Rocamora's gift is of the body, so its blood runs through daughters, and sons of the house can't pass it on. The doctrine is "known" the way everyone knows things. The houses' own records mostly agree with it.
- **Like calls to like.** A gift concentrates when blood meets its own kind. Two people of the same tincture make a child with a stronger one, and the nearer the kinship, the stronger the child. This is the plain reason great houses have always been tempted toward cousins, siblings and worse, and it's described in the Art's own terms: **fixing** (*Fixieren*) a line through near unions, **dissolving** (*Lösen*) it through outside blood. *Solve et coagula*, done with daughters instead of salts.

## 2. Near Blood

### What it costs: *Gerinnung* (curdling)
Fixing works, and it also curdles. The nearer and more often a line folds back on itself, the likelier its children come out wrong:
- **In the body:** frailty, crooked jaws and teeth, weak hearts, bleeders, stillbirths, short lives, and above all sterility. The proudest lines die out the quiet way.
- **In the gift:** the gift turns inward. A Sulphur child runs fevers it can't break, a Salt child stiffens at the joints, a Mars child can't feel pain and so never learns caution. In the mind it shows as Saturn's melancholy, rages, and the famous glass-delusion, where a prince believes he's made of glass and won't let anyone touch him.
- **Bleischlaf, the lead-sleep.** Some curdled infants simply go to sleep. Most wake within days. Some sleep for months. A rare few sleep for years, fed with broth through a reed and turned by nurses, and almost none of those wake. Folklore says Saturn takes a fee from a sleeper and leaves a gift. Physicians say the second half is nonsense. The lead-sleep is the most recognizable sign of near blood in the Empire. It can also come from a Saturn-crushed nativity or a quicksilver-poisoned pregnancy, but nobody ever *assumes* that.

### What makes it manageable
- **Timing.** A near conception made in a strong **Jupiterjahr** (when the Greater Fortune is dignified and Saturn is weak) curdles far less. Court astrologers have made fortunes on these windows. The best of them come round every decade or so, which means near-kin conceptions across the high houses cluster in the same few years, and everyone can count.
- **Solvens.** Taken by the mother through the pregnancy, it "loosens" what the near union packs too tight. It works, mostly, and it costs about what a country manor does.
- **Rhythm.** Wise houses fix for a generation and then dissolve for two or three, bringing in healthy outside husbands or wives. Some houses have done this for centuries and are doing fine. Some never fixed at all and are also doing fine. Their gifts are thinner and their people are healthier, and that's a perfectly respectable trade. **Being a near-kin house is not the winning strategy. It's an old, glamorous, expensive temptation.**

### The Southern Lesson (why it's not the main strategy *nowadays*)
The dynasty's Southern Branch fixed hardest, for the longest, and with the brightest results. Its kings could make an army feel their gaze. Then it produced **the Bewitched King**: sterile, glass-mad, unable to chew his food, and so heavy with the gift that rooms went quiet when he wept. He died without an heir. A twelve-year **Succession War** followed, the North lost the South to a foreign dynasty, and the Southern loyalists fled north into exile.

Since then, every Norican house has had a cautionary tale it can name in one word. Near unions didn't stop. They became **deliberate, rarer, timed, paid for, and talked about.**

### How near-kin love is seen (the in-between view)
Norica's taboo isn't really about kinship. It's about **blood**, meaning children. That puts it somewhere between our world's horror and a frank shrug.

**Degrees.**
- **Far kin** (second cousins and beyond): nobody cares.
- **Near kin** (first cousins, uncle and niece, aunt and nephew): can marry with a Church dispensation, which costs a fee and a sermon. It's common in the high houses and raises a few eyebrows.
- **Nearest kin** (siblings, half-siblings, parents and children, grandparents and grandchildren): can never marry. They take lovers anyway.

**Spielerei versus making blood.** Kin intimacy that can't make a child, such as two sisters, a mother and daughter, kisses, hands, mouths, or the famous *back door*, is **Spielerei**: play. It's gossiped about, smirked at, given a penance, and quietly cultivated in great houses. Nearest kin who **make blood** (a seeding everyone knows about, a pregnancy, a child) are **konjunkt**, "in conjunction," and that's permanent.
- Being konjunkt is **not illegal**, and it **doesn't end your respectability**. You're still received, still invited, still courted. But you can't stand godparent, you can't hold certain Church honors, your house's children are assumed to be fixed (dangerous to marry, or a prize, depending who's asking), and **for the rest of your life every room you enter knows it**. It never comes off.
- It also has **glamour**. Konjunkt women are said to be the best lovers at court and konjunkt men the most dangerous. Among the bold, the stain itself works as an invitation.

**The games.** Because play is tolerated and blood is fatal to reputation, high society has built a whole culture on the line between them:
- the first waltz of the Fasching season danced with your brother, which everyone reads and nobody comments on;
- the **Hauskuss**, the mouth-kiss between kin of great houses, which can be chaste or not, and you can't tell from across the room;
- mothers and sons in the same opera box with the curtain drawn;
- coded fans, gloves and seating;
- the knowing joke that a near pair "keeps to the back door."

The thrill is in staying on the play side of the line, and in everyone watching to see whether you will.

**The Church** (the faith of the Most High and His **Seven Regents**, the angels set over the Wandering Stars) calls nearest-kin blood a sin against the Regents' order and Spielerei a venial one. It sells dispensations for cousin marriages, and its confessors know more about the Empire's beds than its police do.

**Below the nobility it looks different:**
- **The bourgeoisie** are the prudes. Respectable capital families define themselves against decadent nobles and coarse peasants, so any whiff of near love is ruin, *socially* if never legally. They marry cousins constantly anyway, "to keep the firm together," and they don't call that blood.
- **Mountain farms** have a different story. The land goes whole to one heir, and unmarried brothers and sisters stay on as hands for life. Some valleys call near arrangements **Hofsachen**, farm matters. The village knows, the priest knows, nobody says anything, and nobody finds it glamorous either.
- **The city poor** live packed in, with lodgers renting a bed by the shift. There near love is common, unspoken, and treated as bad luck rather than sin.
- **The bonded** have no lineage in law, so their kinship doesn't count. Owners pair them as they like. Matched adult brother-sister and sister-sister pleasure pairs are a luxury item, sold together and trained to perform together.

## 3. Norica

*Main flavor: Austrian/Viennese, in the Biedermeier-to-waltz years: gaslight arriving, steamboats on the river, a secret police that reads the mail, and polite society that has gone home to enjoy itself behind its own doors. Second layer: the **Southern** (Catalan/Castilian) ceremonial, vocabulary and families the court kept from the old union. The everyday language is German with a thick Viennese grain. Court-French is for flirting and insults. "Southern" is the language exile families speak at home when they don't want to be understood.*

### The shape of the realm
- **The Crown: House Greifenstein.** Holds the Sküs. It's a Sun-blooded line with the gift of **Glanz** (presence): in a Greifenstein's displeasure, sworn men feel their oaths tighten like collars. The gift passes through fathers, but the dynasty's old *Pragmatic Card* lets a daughter reign when no son lives, and some of its best-remembered rulers were Empresses. The Northern Branch has dissolved for four generations since the Southern Lesson. It's healthy, sensible and outlived, and its Glanz has gone thin. At court, an **Old Court** party murmurs that one well-timed fixing marriage would bring it back. The **Moderns** answer with one word: *Bewitched*.
- **The Twenty-One** (*die Einundzwanzig*) are the high houses, sitting by Trump in the **Crown Council**. Under them are the **Uradel**, old lesser families with faint gifts, and the **Briefadel**, ennobled bankers, contractors and officials with money, a lesser Southern card for a patent, and no gift at all. Blood versus money is the Empire's quiet class war.
- Then the **Bürger** (coffeehouse, salon, piano, profession, propriety), the **little people** (laundresses, seamstresses, cabmen, musicians, building concierges, lodgers), the **mountain peasantry**, and the **bonded**.

### Vindau, the capital
A walled old city now ringed by a grand boulevard where the walls used to stand, on the broad brown **Ister**. Places a story will use:
- **Die Burg.** The imperial palace, a town of courtyards. Every door has a precedence rule, and every rule is Southern. The **Southern Ceremonial** decides who may sit, who kisses which hand, and who may keep a hat on. The court wears black Southern court dress on high days and loathes it.
- **The Southern Riding School.** White horses from the Karst, dancing in a white hall. Exile families still keep the best boxes.
- **The Salzgries.** The old salt-landing on the riverbank, where barges from the salt country unload. **Palais Rocamora** stands here: a wide Baroque front, green copper roofs, a private bath-house wing, and a staff that sees everything.
- **The coffeehouses.** Marble tables, newspapers on wooden rods, Tarock games that last generations. (Coffee arrived in the sacks the High Gate's army left behind after the **Great Siege**, two centuries ago.) Each house has its regulars, its political color, and its card-wagers. Some tables play for bonds.
- **The Séparées.** Private dining rooms above the best restaurants, with a locking door, a waiter who knocks twice, and a chaise longue nobody pretends is for digesting.
- **The Au.** The river-meadow park: chestnut avenues, the carriage corso on the first of May, fairground booths, card-readers, and the old **Lusthaus** pavilion at the end of the main avenue. After dark, the thickets are the city's most democratic bedroom.
- **The Heuriger hills.** Vine villages on the slopes above the city, where growers pour their new wine in their own courtyards under a pine branch. There's fiddle-and-guitar music, long tables, and every class drinking together, which is where mistakes get made.
- **The Southern Quarter.** The exiles' church, hospital and confraternities, and the bakeries that still make the right bread. It's the community's heart, and a perfect place to find "relatives" no one can check.

### The Kammergut and Solbrunn
- The **Kammergut** (the "Chamber Estate") is the Crown's salt country: deep green lakes, limestone walls, brine wells, and mines worked since before history. One mine shows visitors a man found preserved in the salt, a thousand years old, still wearing his clothes. A wooden **brine pipeline**, centuries old, runs forty miles along the valleys to the boiling-houses.
- Rocamora holds the hereditary **Salzamt**, the Salt Office, which administers the Empire's salt: taxes, mines, boiling-houses, transport. In Vindau, "*Go complain to the Salt Office*" means your complaint is going nowhere. People say it about the Rocamora women.
- **Bad Solbrunn** is the realm's most fashionable spa: a lakeside town of hotels, a colonnaded **Kursaal**, a promenade, brine baths, mud, mountain air, and the summer court. "Taking the cure at Solbrunn" is the polite reason for anything, and that has always included disappearing for two weeks to have a child no one should know about. The spa's own coinage is the **Kurschatten**, the "cure-shadow," the lover you pick up during your cure and lose at the station. Solbrunn is the Kurschatten capital of the world, and the house hosting it keeps a **Badbuch**, a book of who bathed when, and with whom.

### Other crownlands
- **The Hochland.** Alpine valleys of farms and summer pastures, rifle-companies, carved masks, old customs, stubborn people.
- **Zlatava.** The alchemists' city: a hilltop castle, a lane of tiny gold-workers' houses, the Guild's halls, glassworks, foul smells, fortunes made and lost in one firing. The Solvens makers live here.
- **Tergeste.** The Empire's free port on the warm sea, where Norican, Southern, Lagostan, High Gate and Eastern goods (and people) meet. Slave auctions are legal here and fashionable in the season.
- **Pannonia** (east) is its own kingdom under the same crown. See Neighbors.

### The social year
- **Fasching** (January to Ash Wednesday): hundreds of balls. The Opera Ball, the Hunters', the Physicians', the Laundresses', the Coffeehouse-Keepers'. Masks, the midnight quadrille, and **Damenwahl** (ladies' choice), when women ask men, and the lady's choice is understood to run past the dance. Masters lend each other's pleasure-cards for the season, and Rebiswasser sells out.
- **Lent:** salons, music, confession, revenge.
- **The May Corso** in the Au: everyone's carriage, everyone's new mistress.
- **Sommerfrische** (June to September): the city empties into the mountains and spas. **The Solbrunn season** is the summer court, and Rocamora is its host.
- **Autumn:** harvest, new wine, the great hunts.
- **Krampusnacht** (the fifth of December): horned masked runners with birch switches chase the streets. Being caught and switched is half the fun, and the night is famous for being caught on purpose.
- **The Rauhnächte**, the twelve "Rough Nights" between midwinter and Epiphany. These are the days the lunar year doesn't contain, "outside the year," and **bonds drawn by the hour go slack in hours that don't exist.** For twelve nights, cards barely pull. The masked Perchten come down from the mountains, some beautiful and some hideous. The bonded celebrate, a few run, and most stay. Masters lock their doors and sleep badly, and a great deal of what everyone really wanted gets done.

## 4. House Rocamora

*Princely house, Trump XIV (the Old Image is **Temperance**, an angel pouring water from one cup into another; the court has been laughing about Rocamora holding "Temperance" for a hundred years). Called the **Salzfürstinnen**, the Salt Princesses, behind their backs and sometimes to their faces. Livery: verdigris green and salt-white. House oath: "beim Salz," by the salt.*

### Where they come from
- They were Southern, from **the March**, the Catalan-flavored principality of the old Southern crown, where the **pubilla** custom made an heiress daughter the owner of the house and her husband the one who married *in*. Their ancestral seat was **Montsal**, a mountain of solid salt the family had mined since the Pact.
- In the Succession War they backed the Northern claimant and lost. During the retreat, the Rocamora women **salted the plain** the Southern army needed for forage, a famous act that still makes Southern farmers spit. Then they followed the losing prince north and gave up Montsal.
- The grateful Crown gave them the Trump of an extinct northern house, which old northern families have never forgiven, and the Salt Office, which made them indispensable. A century on they're Viennese to the bone in dress, wit and dialect. At home they speak Southern, keep Southern saints, and still sign letters with the old salt-cross.

### The gift: *das Salz*
A body-gift, so it passes through daughters.
- **The long bloom.** Salt women age slowly from their middle twenties. At fifty they look thirty, and at eighty perhaps forty. Then, in their last year or two, it all arrives at once ("they keep until they don't"). Grandmothers, mothers and daughters pass for sisters across a ballroom, and the house plays on it.
- **Keeping.** At a touch and with intent, a Salt woman can stop flesh from spoiling: a wound won't fester, meat won't rot, a corpse lies fresh for the long road home. In war that's no gangrene in the hospital tents and no spoilage in the supply trains. Generals pay dearly to have a Rocamora daughter riding with the army.
- **Salting.** The same gift turned against the earth makes a field barren for a generation. The house has done it once in living memory, the March plain, and the threat alone is a diplomatic instrument.
- **Salting a womb.** A palm on the belly at the dark of the moon, and a woman won't conceive until the next. This is the house's real secret power. The Salt women are the only women in the Empire with *certain* control over their own fertility, which is half of why the house can be ruled by its mothers. And they **do favors**. A noblewoman salted by a Rocamora friend can take any lover she likes and never bear a bastard, so half the great ladies of Vindau owe Rocamora a debt they'll never name. Husbands who want heirs, and confessors who want souls, suspect a great deal. A *permanent* salting, a woman made barren for life, is the Empire's darkest whisper about the house. Nobody has proved it has ever been done.
- **Salt-sense.** They find salt in rock, and they can taste things on skin: fever, fear, a pregnancy weeks before the woman knows it. Some claim they taste more than that.

### How the house works
- **The Mestressa** (at court, *die Fürstin*) rules the house: lands, Salt Office, bloodline, purse and bedrooms. **The Pubilla**, the chosen heiress, is normally the eldest daughter, but the Mestressa may choose. That choice is the house's oldest source of knives.
- **The pubills.** Rocamora daughters don't marry out. Husbands marry *in*, take the name, and are picked for healthy dissolving blood, money, alliances, or looks. They have apartments, allowances and no say in blood matters. Some are beloved, some are furniture, some are **Mignons** the house finds restful, and a few were plainly bought. In Vindau a man who marries a Rocamora is called a *Salzmann*, and it isn't entirely a compliment.
- **Women with women.** The house's women take female lovers as freely as male ones: wives, maids, friends, rivals' daughters. Within the house, **Salt-to-Salt intimacy**, between sisters, cousins, even mothers and daughters, is old house custom and counts as Spielerei. It makes no blood, so in Rocamora eyes it isn't anyone else's business. The private baths in the Palais and at Solbrunn are where that custom lives.
- **Sons** are rare, perhaps one birth in five, and they can't pass the Salt. Rocamora sons run **slight, fine-boned and pretty**, and the house quietly considers a pretty son a sign of strong Salt. Traditionally a son was petted at home, then married out to another house as a prize husband, sent into the Church or a regiment, or kept as a comfortable "house uncle." **Except in fixing generations.** In the South, the house fixed its Salt mainly through **Rebis daughters**, born with both natures, who could sire on their sisters and cousins. Only rarely, and quietly, did it use a son. In exile they dissolved for four generations, marrying northern husbands to fit in. The Salt thinned: blooms got shorter and salting got weaker. About twenty years ago the house fixed again. The protagonist is what that produced.
- **The household.** Sisters, aunts, cousins, grandmothers, pubills, the women's lovers, a small secretariat called the **Kammer** that manages the house's secrets, and a large bonded staff, mostly women, mostly Copas. Every bond is held by the house, so **nobody who tended the sleeper can easily talk**.
- **Holdings.** The Palais on the Salzgries, the seat and spa at Solbrunn, the Kammergut's salt (worked by Bastos-bonded miners, the dirty floor the house's silk stands on), the Southern Quarter's confraternities, and a century of favors.

### Friends and enemies
- **The Crown** needs the salt revenue and distrusts a house that can feed or starve an army.
- **The old northern houses** want Trump XIV back.
- **The bankers and Briefadel** want the Salt Office broken up "for efficiency."
- **Hesperia** has never forgiven the salted plain.
- **The noblewomen of Vindau** owe them. Their husbands resent them.
- **The exile community** looks to them, and needs them to keep the lost cause alive. The house is no longer sure it does.

## 5. The Sleeper

### What happened, as far as anyone admits
- A son was born to Rocamora in a Saturn-heavy winter. Before that first winter ended he went into the **lead-sleep** and didn't come out of it.
- He was kept at **Solbrunn** in a warm, tiled room over the brine spring. He was bathed in brine every day, fed by reed, turned by bonded nurses, and **kept** by the hands of the house's women, which is the only reason a body sleeping that long stayed whole. Over the years he grew, straight and slight and perfectly healthy, into a grown man asleep.
- **The official story:** a sickly son raised in seclusion "at the cure." Most of the court forgot him, some think he died, and a few old women count the years on their fingers and say *lead-sleep* to each other. Lead-sleep means near blood. Near blood means someone in Rocamora made blood with someone they shouldn't have. The house's record names a father, one of the pubills. **Who actually sired him is the house's tightest secret.** Different women in the house believe different answers, and some of them are wrong.
- **What the sleep left.** Sleepers wake with **language and habit but no recollection**. They've been *listening* for years, and they come up knowing how to talk, eat, read a little and dress, with nothing to say about where any of it came from. He understands Southern as well as Northern, and the house didn't expect that. **Nineteen years of women talked over his bed:** confessions, quarrels, plans, gossip, love-talk, things said to a sleeping boy precisely because he could never repeat them. He doesn't *remember* any of it. Sometimes a voice or a phrase lands on him as if he'd heard it before, and every woman who ever sat by that bed is privately wondering what she told him.
- **What the women know that he doesn't.** The women who bathed him through his last sleeping years know his grown body far better than he does: its size, its habits, what it does in sleep. They've been curious about him a long time. He wakes into a house of women who have already looked, have already talked about it among themselves, and now find him looking back.
- **His body is the family's type, pushed further.** Slight, unmistakably femme, the house's sharp mouth and eyes, and dark loose waves the women let grow long. He's also enormously endowed, and he was **born without a foreskin**. The Solbrunn midwives' old saying for that is "*he came finished*." The irony is that he didn't.

### Quick: the nine days
Saturn took a fee. The gift it left, in the house's word, is that he's **quick**. Nobody knows yet whether that's Mercury, a curdling, or both.
- **Any woman he gets with child carries for nine days.** Nine days in place of nine months. She shows by the third day and is round by the sixth. She aches, swells, craves, comes into milk, and runs famously *hot* the whole time, restless and wanting. On the ninth day she gives birth, quickly and easily. Quick children come easily.
- **Ordinary preventatives are unreliable against him.** Copper tea and timing help about as much as they help with any young man, which is to say they don't always. The only sure thing is a **Salt palm**, a Rocamora woman salting a womb before he touches it. So the house can, if it chooses, **decide who conceives by him.** It can also choose *not* to salt someone. Or someone can make sure she isn't salted. Or a Rocamora woman can decline to salt *herself*.
- **The danger of near blood still applies.** A child of his by a Salt woman, a sister, aunt, cousin or mother, would carry Salt from her womb with Salt shaped by him on top of it: the most fixed Rocamora child in a century. That's either the house's salvation or another Bewitched. Nobody knows which, because this has never happened before.
- **Does quickness pass on?** Nobody knows either.

### Drüben: where his children go
*"Drüben" just means "over there," which is what the household calls it.*
1. **The door.** At the first moonrise after a quick child's birth, any doorway the mother carries it through opens onto **Drüben** instead of the next room. After that, **any doorway, while the Moon is above the horizon**, opens there for the people allowed in. If the child isn't carried, it frets and burns until the next moonrise, and then the doorway takes it by itself. No one has let it come to that twice.
2. **The place.** A high, wide valley: meadows, larch and stone-pine, a lake, mild winters, a sun and a moon of its own. It was empty land and weather until the house carried in a household. Now there's the **Ammenhof**, the nurses' farmstead, with dairy, chapel, schoolroom and huts. It's real ground, real seasons and real life. **Nobody dreams it.** You walk there, live there, get your boots muddy there, and can be buried there.
3. **Time.** **A year passes in Drüben for every day outside.** An evening's visit costs a mother a few weeks of her own life. An afternoon visit lasts minutes outside. A mother who wants to *raise* her child gives it years.
4. **Who may enter:** the child's kin **by blood** (mothers, grandmothers, aunts, half-siblings) and kin **by milk** (the wet nurses who suckled the child in its first days before the crossing, and the nurses' own children, its milk-siblings). Milk-kinship is an old idea in Norica. Here it's suddenly a literal key to a door.
5. **Who may not: the sire.** He opens the same doorway and finds the next room. Always. The alchemists say *the fire stays outside the vessel*. The astrologers say *the Sun has no road into the Moon's house*. The grandmothers say *a father isn't meant to see that part*. No one actually knows.
6. **Coming out.** Drüben won't let a quick child out before its **eighteenth year inside**. After that it can walk out at any moonrise, and most do somewhere between eighteen and twenty-one, at their bloom. **Once out, never back.** Outside they age at ordinary speed.
7. **The cost and the pull.** Mothers visit often and briefly, or stay for years and come out years older a week later. Rocamora's women, with their long bloom, can afford Drüben-years better than anyone, so in practice the protagonist's **sisters, aunts and mother raise his children**, alongside the nurses. The nurses go in for good. They come out twenty years older three weeks later, rich, freed if they were bonded, and a little strange. Drüben-time is also *useful*: a house woman can step in for two years of study, rest, or an affair and be back in two days. **Only while a child is inside.** The house has noticed.
8. **What comes out.** Grown people with whole childhoods behind them: friends, quarrels, skills, griefs, lives of their own. They know their mothers intimately and know the house as the women raised them to see it. They know their father only from *other people's stories*, and those stories differed. **Children born within a few days of each other grow up together**, so they come out as a tribe, with milk-siblings who aren't his blood at all. And every one of them comes out with an opinion of the father they've never met.

A child conceived at one full moon walks out grown at the next.

### The womb (not yet)
- The sleep left him **unfinished**. His body still has *Mercury* in it, room to change, which no ordinary body has.
- The Art can **open** a change like that, and it does something like it for women with Rebiswasser, but it fails on ordinary men. Salt can **fix** a change once it's made. So a womb for him would take Zlatava's alchemists and Rocamora's palms working together, and it would teach the Guild far more about the house than the house would like.
- What it would mean: a son of Rocamora who could **carry Salt**, the first ever. And, the grandmothers say, he could walk into Drüben **as a mother**, to the children he *bore*. Never to the ones he sired.

## 6. The House's Problem: what Rocamora hides, and how

The house reacts the way great houses do: **one face outside, ten opinions inside.**

**What has to stay inside the walls**
1. **That he's awake**, until the house decides how to present him.
2. **That he slept**, because sleep means near blood.
3. **Who sired him.**
4. **What his seed does.**
5. **Drüben.**
6. **What comes out of Drüben.**

**The tools for hiding it**
- **The bonded staff.** Everyone who ever touched him wears a Rocamora card. Betrayal makes their hands shake. It isn't impossible, but it's very hard.
- **The spa.** Two weeks "at the cure" covers a nine-day pregnancy and the lying-in with days to spare. Solbrunn has hidden noble pregnancies for a century, and nobody blinks at another lady arriving pale and leaving slim.
- **The Salt palm**, to decide who conceives.
- **The exile community.** A Southern family can always produce a "cousin from the March": baptismal records are on the far side of a hostile border, the Southern Quarter vouches for its own, and nobody can check. Vindau will eventually notice that Rocamora has acquired an unusual number of beautiful Southern cousins, all about twenty, all with that mouth.
- **The Kammer** keeps the lists of who knows what.
- **Telling him nothing, or telling him everything.** He has no memories, so every woman in the house can hand him a different childhood, and some will.

**Ten opinions inside**
The house won't agree on what he is. These are the obvious positions. They're roles, not people, and one woman may hold several of them:
- **Shelter him.** Present him late, marry him nowhere, and keep him as the house's treasure.
- **Use him.** Salt a generation back to strength in a single year, with everything that implies about whose wombs.
- **Hide him.** He's evidence of the fixing, and the fixing was a crime against the house's reputation.
- **Marry him out** for a great alliance, or the Mond, before anyone finds out what he is.
- **Keep him.** Some women simply want him for themselves, and they don't care what the house decides.
- **Undo the hiding.** A couple of women think the hiding is the real danger, and would rather tell the Crown on their own terms.

The **pubills** are mostly kept out of all of it, and some of them resent that. **His children**, raised by these same women, will come out sharing their factions.

## 7. Bonds and the Bonded

*Background institution: everywhere, ordinary, unexamined. Not uniformly cruel, never pretty.*

### How people come to be held
- **Debt** (Oros): the most common route in, with term-limited cards that fade as the debt is worked off, unless the holder finds ways to add to it.
- **Sentence** (Espadas): penal bonds for crimes, sometimes for life.
- **War** (Espadas): captives taken in the eastern and southern wars.
- **Sale** (Copas or Bastos): poor families sign their *grown* sons and daughters into bond at majority for a lump sum, or people sign themselves.
- **Import:** bought through Tergeste and Lagosta from the High Gate's and the East's markets.

Two lines hold everywhere in Norica. **Nobody is bound before eighteen**, because a card won't take on a nativity that hasn't "fixed." And **bonds aren't inherited**: a bonded woman's child is born free. Many of those children end up bound anyway.

### What it's like
It depends almost entirely on the card's terms and the holder's temperament.
- A Copas lady's maid in a great house eats better than a free seamstress, wears silk, has favorites and enemies, and may be loved.
- A Bastos miner in the salt dark is worked until his card or his lungs give out.
- Most bonded live in between, with the pull always present the way weather is.

### The suits as slang
- "**A Copas girl**" or "**a Copas boy**" means a household or pleasure bond.
- "**Bastos**" means labor, and is mildly contemptuous.
- "**Drawn Espadas**" means a captive or convict.
- "**Oros**" means "she's working off her father's debts."

### Erotic culture around bonds
- **Lusthäuser.** Training-and-hiring houses for pleasure-bonds. They teach music, conversation, dancing, the toilette, and the bedroom arts. A good Lusthaus diploma is a real credential.
- **The Kartenkuss.** At a binding, the bound kisses the inked card before it's handed over. Some holders ask for that kiss again, on other occasions.
- **The Morgengabe.** The "morning gift" a bride receives after the wedding night has, among the rich, become a pleasure-bond more often than a jewel.
- **Lending.** Cards are lent for a night, a journey, or a Fasching season. Swapping Copas cards between friends at masked balls is old society sport.
- **Playing for cards.** In some coffeehouse back rooms, Königrufen is played for bonds, including, now and then, a player's *own* card for one night.
- **Kammerkätzchen**, "chamber-kittens": the chambermaids of song and farce, bonded or not, always available to the son of the house in the stories, and sometimes in fact.
- **Matched pairs.** Twins, sisters, brother-sister pairs, sold and trained together. The costliest luxury in the trade.
- **Free-and-bonded love.** A lady and her maid, a master and his valet. It's the most common love-story in Vindau and the least admitted. The card means the bonded one can't leave, and also that no one can make them stay *in love*.
- **Burnings.** Manumission for loyal service, a deathbed gift, or a lover's grand gesture. The freed person keeps the ash in a locket.
- **The Rauhnächte**, when every bonded person in the Empire gets twelve nights of nearly free choice and every holder finds out what was wanted all along.

### Ripples
- **Card-burners.** A small radical underground that steals and burns bonds. Heroes to some coffeehouse students, terrorists to the police.
- **Abolition talk** among the reading Bürger, who rent pleasure-bonds by the hour and see no contradiction.
- **The Kartenschreiber guild**, whose ink-recipe is the most closely kept secret in the Empire after Solvens.

## 8. Bodies and Loves

### Women with women
The Empire's most tolerated love, because **it makes no blood**.
- A wife's **Busenfreundin** (bosom-friend) isn't adultery in anyone's ledger. Husbands who object are laughed at, and husbands who don't are suspected of watching.
- **Ladies' circles.** The afternoon coffee circle (**Kaffeekränzchen**) is Vindau's sapphic gossip network. The noble ladies' chapter-houses, where unmarried noblewomen live on endowments, have an unquenchable reputation. Women's hours at the baths are an institution.
- **The Schwesternbrief** (sister-letter). Two women write *each other's* Copas card and each holds the other's. It's a mutual bond of sworn love, the strongest declaration two women can make. It isn't marriage, and it's harder to break.
- **Sapphic drama is drama like any other:** jealousy, a lady running off with her bonded maid, two great houses' daughters whose affair becomes a political problem. It just never becomes a *blood* problem, and that's why it's allowed to go so far.

### Mignons
Femme, non-conforming men, named for the soft favorites of an old court that the Viennese still gossip about.
- **At court:** fashionable as companions, confidants and dance partners, and as husbands for matriarchal houses ("a Mignon husband doesn't compete").
- **Among the Bürger:** mocked to their faces and watched in private.
- **In regiments:** scorned officially. Hussar messes have their own traditions about which nobody writes memoirs.
- **Among the bonded:** a well-trained Mignon on a Copas card is a prized, costly household ornament.
- **In Rocamora:** pretty sons are the family type. That makes the protagonist read as *a proper Rocamora boy*, and as a Mignon to everyone else at first sight.

### Rebis
Women born with **both natures**: a working cock as well as a womb. The name comes from the Art's symbol of the perfected Work, the crowned two-natured figure who appears on half the alchemical frontispieces in Zlatava.
- **Not rare.** Perhaps one woman in thirty or forty.
- **Legally and socially women.**
- **Thought lucky** by the Art, **desired** by nearly everyone, and **a little feared**, because a Rebis *can make blood*. A Rebis lover of a married woman is the one female affair that can end in a bastard. A Rebis sister is the one sister-pairing that falls under the konjunkt line.
- **Politically they matter in matriarchal houses**, where Rebis daughters were historically the means of fixing a female line without involving a man. Rocamora's Southern history is full of them.
- **The Art's version.** Rebiswasser gives an ordinary woman a Rebis body for one night. A permanent version exists. It's rare, dangerous and expensive, and the Bürger find it the most shocking thing a woman can buy.

### Men with men
- **Treated as Spielerei**, since it makes no blood: no crime, no stain, some mockery.
- **Among nobles**, tolerated as a gentleman's private matter.
- **Among the Bürger**, the subject of whispered ruin.
- **In the mountains**, the subject of jokes about the high pastures.
- **Among officers**, a code nobody admits exists.

## 9. Pleasure in Norica (institutions and customs)

### The affair vocabulary of Vindau
- A **Pantscherl** is an affair.
- A **Gspusi** is a sweetheart, a fling.
- **The Hausfreund**, the "friend of the house," is a married woman's lover, welcome at the family table.
- **The Southern Cortejo** is a married lady's recognized gallant, who carries her fan, holds her box and is understood to hold more. Exile houses formalize it, and northern houses have caught the fashion.
- **The süßes Mädel**, the "sweet girl," is the free working girl (seamstress, flower-seller, laundress) who has affairs with gentlemen above her station. She's the bonded Copas's free counterpart and rival.
- **The Kurschatten** (see Solbrunn).

### Rites
- **The Beilager.** The formal bedding of a high marriage, witnessed by the two mothers and a priest before the curtains close, and sometimes after.
- **The Morgengabe.**
- **The Hauskuss.**
- **Damenwahl** at the Fasching balls.

### Places
- **Séparées**, coffeehouse back rooms, the Lusthaus, and the Au's thickets after dark.
- **The baths.** Solbrunn's private brine cabins, the Pannonian hot baths, the Southern Quarter's bath-house.
- **The opera boxes**, with the curtain drawn.
- **The Heuriger courtyards** at two in the morning.
- **Fensterln**, in the mountains: young men climb ladders to girls' windows at night by custom. Who climbs to whose window, and who leaves the shutter unlatched, is the oldest courting game in the Hochland.

### Seasons of licence
Fasching, Krampusnacht, the Rauhnächte, the high-pasture summer, and the Solbrunn season.

### The Art in the bedroom
Copper tea, sulphur drops, moonmilk, Rebiswasser, scented salt-scrubs from Solbrunn, and **Zlatava glass**: blown pleasure-objects, some filled with warm brine or oil. Sold in respectable apothecaries under the counter, under the name *"medical instruments for the hysteric."*

## 10. Other Powers

*Twenty-one high houses sit in Council. These six (plus the Crown) are the ones most likely to touch the story. Each shows a different answer to the near-blood question. None of them is a villain, and all of them want something.*

| House | Trump (Old Image) | Gift | Line | Near-blood stance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Greifenstein** (Crown) | Sküs (the Fool) | *Glanz*, presence that tightens oaths | father | dissolved for four generations; tempted |
| **Rocamora** | XIV (Temperance) | *Salz*, keeping, salting, long bloom | mother | dissolved for four generations, then fixed once in secret |
| **Hohenegg** | XII (the Hanged Man) | *Eisen*, no fear, pain doesn't stop them | father | fixed twice last century; regrets it |
| **Taxau** | III (the Empress) | *der Weg*, always know the road, feel where a letter lies | father | never fixed; proud, healthy, everywhere |
| **Steinach** | XV (the Devil) | *die Mauer*, what they close stays closed | mother | fixed for three centuries; paying for it |
| **Ortenau** | VI (the Lovers) | *die Waage*, feel the weight of an oath or a bond | father | fixes every third generation, on schedule, successfully |
| **Vasvár** (Pannonian) | XVII (the Star) | *die Glut*, make powder catch or fail, fire leap or die | father | never; "Sulphur curdles to fire" |

- **Hohenegg.** Generals for three hundred years. They're proprietors of a dozen regiments, so they hold a dozen card-chests, and they fight every Crown attempt to centralize the cards. They married into the extinct house that held XIV before Rocamora and **still claim that Trump**. A past Hohenegg heir couldn't feel pain and died of a burst appendix while laughing at dinner, and the house has been cautious since. They despise the exiles, Mignons and the Salt Office in roughly that order.
- **Taxau.** The Imperial Post. Their men can't get lost, and they always know which saddlebag a letter is in. They also run, unofficially, the **Black Chamber**, where the Empire's mail is opened, copied and resealed overnight. Rocamora learns things from bodies in baths and Taxau learns them from paper. They've been polite rivals for a century and occasional lovers for longer. Holding *the Empress* is the court's favorite joke about an all-male bureaucracy.
- **Steinach.** A dour, pious, rigidly honest female-line Saturn house, holding *the Devil*. They fixed so long and so hard that their castle has a **Sleepers' Hall**: a long cold gallery where their own lead-sleepers lie, some for decades, tended by sisters in grey. They're the realm's real experts on the lead-sleep. They'll know at once what Rocamora's son was, and they'll want desperately to know how he woke, because none of theirs ever have.
- **Ortenau.** The Crown's lawyers, judges and treaty-drafters, patrons of the Kartenschreiber guild. They can tell a sound card from a sour one and a meant oath from an empty one by holding it. They fix every third generation, in a strong Jupiterjahr, with Solvens bought years in advance, and it *works*. Their near-wed couples are known at court as "the Ortenau Lovers," a card joke that turned out to be true. They're smug, healthy, and the backbone of the Old Court party. Their scholars would give a great deal to study a quick child.
- **Vasvár.** The greatest of the Pannonian houses: artillery masters and hussar colonels. They're hot-tempered and proud, and they lead Pannonian autonomy. A Vasvár castle once burned with the near-wed couple inside, so they treat near blood with horror. They distrust Southern exiles on principle, and their younger sons make spectacular, dangerous suitors.
- **The Pagat house (I).** Old, poor, nearly giftless, with a faint knack for small luck at cards. Their only real power is the **tie-break vote**, so every faction courts them before every Council, and their daughters marry bankers for money.

### Institutions and factions
- **The Church of the Seven Regents.** The confessors know everything. The **dispensation office** prices every cousin marriage in the Empire. The **Cardinal-Archbishop of Vindau** sits in Council without a Trump.
- **Die Kunst, the Alchemists' Guild** (Zlatava). It wants the blood's secret, controls Solvens, and would sell its grandmothers for a living lead-sleeper who woke.
- **The Kartenschreiber.** Card-writers and notaries of bondage. They're licensed, rich, and indispensable, and every one of them has seen the ugliest contracts in the Empire.
- **The Hofkammer and the banks.** The treasury, and the Briefadel bankers who lend it money. They want the salt monopoly reorganized, by which they mean bought.
- **The Southern exiles.** Confraternities, a church, a hospital, a seat on an old **Southern Council** at court, and a lost cause. A younger generation thinks the cause is dead and wants to stop being foreigners.
- **The card-burners.** A small, idealistic, violent underground.
- **The Old Court and the Moderns.** The two court parties: near blood restored versus near blood buried. Each party has members in every house, including Rocamora.

## 11. Neighbors

### Hesperia (southwest): the lost sister
*Castilian with Portuguese in its ports. Names like Doña Inés de Alcaraz y Sotomayor, Dom João Teixeira. Titles: Grandee, Excelencia.*
- **Who rules it.** The old Southern crown, now under the foreign dynasty that won the Succession War.
- **The Blood Tribunal.** Hesperia turned the Bewitched King's lesson into **zealotry**. Its Tribunal of Blood hunts near unions, licenses alchemists, and audits genealogies. "Clean blood" here now means blood that was **never** folded back on itself. The oldest Hesperian houses are exactly the ones with the most to hide, and they keep fixing in secret, at terrible risk, from pride.
- **Sex and display.** Public honor is ferocious and private life is supple. There are chaperone-duennas, go-between bawds called **celestinas**, and **tapadas**: women who walk out veiled with one eye showing, so anonymous that even their husbands can't stop them.
- **Bonds.** Harsh. Hesperia's estates and mines run on Espadas-drawn captives.
- **Relations with Norica.** A cold peace. Rival embassies, rival claims, and Hesperian agents in Vindau watching the exiles, above all the house that salted the March plain.
- **The March.** The Catalan-flavored principality the exiles left behind. Its charters were abolished, its language is discouraged, and it's restless. Its people half-worship the exiles and half-resent them for leaving. **Montsal**, the salt mountain, is worked now by a Hesperian company. Names: Montserrat Queralt i Pinós, Jordi Vilademany.

### Pannonia (east): the partner kingdom
*Magyar-flavored. Names like Vasvári Ilona and Count Esterváry Gábor (family name first at home, reversed in Vindau). Titles: palatine, ispán.*
- **The Compromise.** A kingdom under the same crown, with its own Diet, its own pride, and its own ten-yearly renegotiation of terms with Vindau. That renegotiation is where most imperial politics actually happens.
- **Blood and nobility.** Sulphur-blooded, patrilineal houses. A huge, poor, proud gentry: one Pannonian in twenty is noble and most of them own a horse and a sword. Hussars, fiddle music, plains wine and **hot springs**. Pannonian baths, domed and steaming and centuries old, are the Empire's other great bathing culture, and they rival Solbrunn.
- **Near blood.** Avoided with horror.
- **Desire.** Male friendship among hussars is fierce and physical. Pannonian women are said to choose their lovers at the harvest dances and inform the men later.
- **Bonds.** Mostly rural: Bastos field-cards held by the gentry.

### The High Gate (southeast): the old enemy
*Ottoman-Persian flavored. In Vindau it's called **die Hohe Pforte**, after the palace gate where its government sits. Its own name is the **Hazar Sultanate**. Names like Mihrişah, Gülbahar, Selim Pasha, Rüstem Agha.*
- **The dynasty.** It **never fixes**. Every sultan is the son of a **bought concubine**, so the dynasty's blood is dissolved every single generation. Its gift is faint and its rulers are unfailingly healthy, and Norican Moderns cite it constantly.
- **The harem.** A palace-city of women with its own ranks: *gözde*, the one who caught his eye; *ikbal*, the favored. Above them all is the sultan's mother, the **Valide**, a former slave who is often the real ruler. Matriarchal power born from bondage is the High Gate's great paradox, and Rocamora's women watch it with professional interest.
- **The trade.** The largest slave market on the continent. The hammam. Court astrology as a government ministry.
- **Relations with Norica.** Two centuries since the **Great Siege**, and now trade, embassies, coffee, slaves, and a long cold border with the occasional war.

### Lagosta (Adriatic): the merchant republic
*Venetian and Dalmatian flavored: a lagoon city of canals, masks and glass. Names like Zanetta Morosin and ser Piero Grimani.*
- **Government.** An elected **Doxe**, a merchant oligarchy, banks.
- **Glass.** The best glassworks outside Zlatava. The Art's vessels and mirrors come from here.
- **Pleasure.** The **cortigiane oneste**, educated, poetic, ruinous courtesans who are often Rebis and are the most expensive women on earth. A carnival half the year long. The Rialto auctions, where cards change hands under a public notary.
- **Near blood.** Merchant clans marry cousins to keep capital in the family. Nearest-kin scandals are hushed with money.
- **Relations with Norica.** Rival of Tergeste for the Empire's sea trade, and banker to half the Twenty-One.

### The Cantons (west): the Oathfolk
*Alpine-republican, Swiss flavored. Names like Ueli Zbinden and Verena Hürlimann. They call themselves the **Eidgenossen**, the Oath-fellows.*
- **No bonds.** The one people in the known world that **refuses cards**. No one in the Cantons can be bound, and their mercenaries serve on oath alone, which makes them **uncapturable**. The Crown's palace guard is Cantonal for exactly that reason.
- **Morals.** Austere and prudish. Near blood is a horror. Bankers' discretion is a religion.
- **Relations with Norica.** They sell soldiers and keep accounts. Every great Norican house has money in a Cantonal vault.

### Akitsu (the far East): the distant court
*Japanese and Korean flavored, reached by sea and caravan over a year's travel. Names like Lady Kiyohara no Sanae and Captain Yi Seon-ho.*
- **What Vindau sees of it.** An embassy, silks, lacquer, porcelain, and **pillow-books**: erotic prints that Vindau ladies collect under lock and key. Akitsu fashion is the season's rage.
- **Kinship runs through the womb.** Half-siblings by *different mothers* may marry without shame, and same-mother siblings may never. **By Akitsu law, a man's children by different mothers aren't kin to each other at all.** Vindau finds that either barbaric or extremely convenient, depending on who's asking.
- **Courtship.** Night-visits to a lady's screen, which the Hochland would recognize as Fensterln with better poetry. Court women's love for each other is written about as openly as any other kind.

## 12. Pressure Points

*These are tensions already loaded into the world at the moment he wakes, not plot. Any of them can stay quiet for a long time, or go off on its own.*

**Inside the house**
- **Who sired him**, who believes what, and who would rather it never be asked.
- **The Pubilla.** He can't inherit, but a *daughter by him* could be the strongest heiress in a century. Several women can do that arithmetic, and some of them want him anyway, which makes it much harder to tell ambition from appetite. *They* can't always tell either.
- **The Salt palm.** Who salts whom, who conveniently forgets, who refuses to be salted, and who salts a rival "as a favor."
- **The nurses.** Bonded women who gave years to a sleeping body. Some adore him, some resent the whole thing, and all of them hold secrets the card keeps in their mouths.
- **The pubills.** Husbands kept out of the secret, any of whom might sell a guess to Taxau for a better life.
- **What he heard.** Every woman who ever sat by the bed.
- **Drüben's women.** Sisters and aunts who go in for years come back older, closer to his children than he'll ever be, and with authority in the house nobody voted them. **His children come out raised by the factions.**

**At court**
- **The Mond.** A Rocamora match to the right house could win XXI, and the Old Court party would love a fixing done "properly." Neither has any idea what he actually is.
- **Steinach's Sleepers' Hall.** The one house that will recognize a woken sleeper on sight.
- **The Guild.** It would give anything to study him. So would Ortenau.
- **Hohenegg** wants XIV back. The bankers want the salt. Card reform is coming, and every proprietor house is arming for the fight.
- **Taxau's Black Chamber.** It reads letters. Somebody in Rocamora is going to order cradles, wet nurses and Southern baptismal papers in quantities that make a clerk curious.
- **The salt favors.** Half of Vindau's great ladies owe Rocamora. That's leverage both ways.

**In society**
- **Southern cousins**, more and more of them, all about twenty, all with that mouth.
- **The konjunkt line.** One proven near seeding marks the women involved, and him, permanently. Not ruin. Just forever.
- **A child by an outsider.** If a noblewoman of another house bears his child, whose house is it? It carries her gift through the womb and his quickness, and she can walk into Drüben while he can't.
- **The Rauhnächte.** Twelve nights a year when the cards that keep the house's mouths shut go slack.
- **Akitsu law.** It says his children by different mothers aren't kin to each other at all. Somebody in Vindau will find that very convenient.
- **Hesperia.** It remembers the salted plain. The March still dreams of Montsal. Whether the young exiles want a cause or a life is still open.
